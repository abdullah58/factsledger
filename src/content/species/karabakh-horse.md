## Overview

The Karabakh horse is a mountain-steppe racing and riding horse breed named after the Karabakh region of [Azerbaijan](/places/azerbaijan/), where it was originally developed. It is famed for its endurance in mountainous terrain, its mild temper, its dense chestnut colour with a golden tint, and its exceptional speed, and it is regarded in Azerbaijan as a symbol of national heritage and pride ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/49661.html)).

The Karabakh is one of two horse breeds — the other is the Dilboz (Gazakh) — said to have originated in Azerbaijan among the roughly 260 horse breeds known in the world ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/49661.html)). It is a cross-breed of Akhal-Teke, Persian, Kabardin, Turkoman and Arabian horses, and stands out for what has been described as its "golden glow" ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/49661.html)). Today fewer than 1,000 Karabakh horses remain in the world, most of them bred in Azerbaijan's Sheki region, and government breeding programmes are working to save the breed from extinction ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/49661.html)).

## A mountain horse of the Karabakh region

The Karabakh horse's build suits the country it comes from: a mountain horse, not very tall, slim and sure-footed, with a small, well-defined head, a straight profile with a broad forehead and large nostrils, and a neck set high, average in length, muscular and elegant ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/culture/165993.html)). Its usual colours are chestnut or bay with a golden tint; less commonly, Karabakh horses can be gray or carry white spots, with the palette also described as red, buckthorn, brown, bay, gray and golden-red ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/49661.html); [AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/culture/165993.html)). Temperament is the breed's other signature: it is renowned for good temper, loyalty, strength and speed, and for stamina that has made it a very successful racer despite its light frame ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/culture/131935.html)). The breed's suppleness made it the ideal mount for traditional games, above all [chovgan](/events/chovgan/) — the horse-riding game played by two teams mounted on Karabakh horses — and Sur-papa ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/culture/131935.html); [UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/USL/chovqan-a-traditional-karabakh-horse-riding-game-in-the-republic-of-azerbaijan-00905)); Karabakh horses also appeared in a cavalry show at one President's Cup [chovgan tournament](/events/presidents-cup-chovgan/) kickoff ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/sports/218979.html)).

## History: khans, exhibitions and queens

Karabakh was Azerbaijan's main horse-breeding region, and the breed's story is bound up with the region's rulers and poets. Gravestones in the Mil steppe from the 9th and 10th centuries carry images of local golden-brown Karabakh racers known as sarilar in the Near East and Russia ([Visions of Azerbaijan](http://www.visions.az/en/news/160/f4ee9c9c/)). The historians Simonov and Marder wrote that the Karabakh breed bore a great similarity to the noble Arabian and Persian breeds and was probably the result of crossing Arabian and Persian horses with Turkmen ones ([Visions of Azerbaijan](http://www.visions.az/en/news/160/f4ee9c9c/)). The breed reached its peak of popularity in the 18th and 19th centuries: a Karabakh horse received a silver medal at an international show in Paris in 1867, and Karabakh horses won silver and bronze at an all-Russian exhibition in 1869 ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/49661.html)). Horses from the herd of the poet Natavan, daughter of Karabakh's ruler, were shown at Paris in 1867, Tbilisi and Moscow in 1896, often ranked first with medals and diplomas ([Visions of Azerbaijan](http://www.visions.az/en/news/160/f4ee9c9c/)).

## Decline and survival

The breed's modern history is one of decline and rescue. The number of Karabakh horses began to fall against the backdrop of the civil and ethnic wars in the Caucasus and the Karabakh region: the horses were continually moved from place to place, and the movement of pregnant mares led to miscarriages ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/49661.html)). Yet the breed has also enjoyed remarkable showcases: in 1956 a Karabakh stallion named Zaman was presented to Queen Elizabeth II, the breed's first representative in Great Britain, and Karabakh horses have represented Azerbaijan at the Royal Windsor Horse Show since 2012, including the Queen's 2012 Diamond Jubilee ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/culture/131935.html)). Speed remains the breed's calling card: the Karabakh Foundation reports that in 2004 a Karabakh horse named Kishmish from the Agdam region set a world record over 1,000 metres in 1 minute 9 seconds ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/49661.html)). The government is running horse-breeding programmes to save the animal from extinction, with most Karabakh horses now bred in the Sheki region ([AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/49661.html)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the Karabakh horse?**
The Karabakh horse is a mountain-steppe racing and riding horse breed from Azerbaijan's Karabakh region. It is famed for its golden-tinted chestnut coat, its stamina in mountainous terrain, its mild temper and its speed, and is regarded in Azerbaijan as a symbol of national heritage and pride.

**How big is a Karabakh horse?**
Karabakh horses are not very tall: 1.48–1.52 metres, or up to 15 hands. They are slim with attractive faces, small well-defined heads, straight profiles with broad foreheads, and necks set high, muscular and elegant. Despite their delicate appearance, they are known for stamina and racing success.

**What is the Karabakh horse used for?**
Racing and riding, and as the traditional mount for chovgan and Sur-papa, Azerbaijan's national horse-riding games. Karabakh horses also perform in ceremonial displays, including at the Royal Windsor Horse Show and in cavalry shows at chovgan tournaments, where their stamina and training are on show.

**Is the Karabakh horse endangered?**
Fewer than 1,000 Karabakh horses remain in the world, most bred in Azerbaijan's Sheki region, and government breeding programmes are working to save the breed from extinction. Its numbers fell during the wars in the Caucasus and Karabakh region, when repeated moves of the herds caused losses including miscarriages among pregnant mares.
