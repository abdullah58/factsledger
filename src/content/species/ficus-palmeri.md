## Overview

Ficus palmeri, known as the rock fig, tescalama, Mexican fig, or desert fig, is a strangler fig of the family Moraceae native to the Baja California peninsula in Mexico ([Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum](https://www.desertmuseum.org/programs/succulents_rockfigs.php); [University of Arizona Campus Arboretum](https://apps.cals.arizona.edu/arboretum/taxon.aspx?id=1080); [Royal Horticultural Society](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/187637/ficus-palmeri/details)). The very name under which it is known here is contested: [Kew](/organizations/royal-botanic-gardens-kew/)'s Plants of the World Online synonymizes Ficus palmeri S.Watson under Ficus petiolaris Kunth, following Ibarra-Manriquez et al.'s 2012 treatment of the genus in Mexico, while noting the name is still accepted under the alternative taxonomy of Govaerts (2001) and the IPNI/World Checklist of Vascular Plants 2026 ([Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:104664-2)). The World Flora Online's December 2022 snapshot likewise places Ficus petiolaris (published 1817) as the accepted name and lists Ficus palmeri S.Watson — published in Proceedings of the American Academy of Arts 24: 77 (1889) — as a synonym ([World Flora Online](https://wfoplantlist.org/taxon/wfo-0000689710-2022-12?matched_id=wfo-0000689615)). The University of Arizona Campus Arboretum flags the same controversy on its own accession record, adding that a third species, F. brandegeei — which differs only in having glabrous foliage — also grows on the peninsula ([University of Arizona Campus Arboretum](https://apps.cals.arizona.edu/arboretum/taxon.aspx?id=1080)).

## A fig that strangles rocks

Rock figs are a legacy of the Sonoran Desert's tropical origins: true strangler figs begin life as epiphytes on host trees, but with no desert trees large enough to host a seedling, the desert rock figs took to establishing on cliff faces and "strangling" rocks instead ([Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum](https://www.desertmuseum.org/programs/succulents_rockfigs.php)). Where roots never reach moist soil, a plant can spend its entire life as a dwarfed saxicole; the Desert Museum photographs a Ficus palmeri growing on a hilltop boulder in central Baja California that will never find water and become a tree, noting it appears ancient and "may have clung to life here for centuries" ([Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum](https://www.desertmuseum.org/programs/succulents_rockfigs.php)). Where roots do reach soil or a streambed, the same species can grow into a large tree ([Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum](https://www.desertmuseum.org/programs/succulents_rockfigs.php); [University of Arizona Campus Arboretum](https://apps.cals.arizona.edu/arboretum/taxon.aspx?id=1080)).

That rock-bound life is more than endurance. In arid Baja California Sur, field observations documented the wild fig Ficus palmeri as one of the plants "capable of cracking, growing in and colonizing cliffs and rocks formed from ancient lava flows and, consequently, forming soil for succession by other plant species" ([Bashan et al., 2002, via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222657774_Primary_Colonization_of_Volcanic_Rocks_by_Plants_in_Arid_Baja_California_Mexico)). Follow-up work at the La Purisima-San Isidro lava flow and the Sierra de La Paz found that Ficus palmeri trees grow in rocks devoid of soil while hosting dense layers of bacteria and fungi in their rhizoplane, linking the tree to the chemical weathering of the rock itself ([Puente et al., 2004, via Thieme](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-2004-821100)).

## Description, range, and cultivation

GRIN-Global records the English common name "desert fig" and lists the native distribution as Mexico: Baja Norte, Baja Sur, and Sonora ([GRIN-Global](https://ausgenebank.agriculture.vic.gov.au/gringlobal/taxon/taxonomydetail?id=16926)). The University of Arizona Campus Arboretum describes large, dark green, fuzzy, ovate-to-cordate leaves on fuzzy petioles and branchlets, smooth yellowish (sometimes scaly) bark, and — in older plants — a large, swollen caudex, with wild roots that appear to flow over rocks in search of water or soil ([University of Arizona Campus Arboretum](https://apps.cals.arizona.edu/arboretum/taxon.aspx?id=1080)). The Royal Horticultural Society describes a hardy evergreen tree that can reach 50 m in its native Mexico, where roots gnarl and twist across rocky terrain and the bark and trunk of larger specimens turn bulbous and twisted, but that stays around 2 m in the less favourable UK; its foliage is dark green and oval, and the rare small fruits are inedible ([Royal Horticultural Society](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/187637/ficus-palmeri/details)).

In cultivation it is heat tolerant but drought deciduous, does best with frequent summer waterings, and is hardy to about 28 °F ([University of Arizona Campus Arboretum](https://apps.cals.arizona.edu/arboretum/taxon.aspx?id=1080)). Because it so readily develops a swollen trunk, it is commonly grown in containers as a bonsai subject ([University of Arizona Campus Arboretum](https://apps.cals.arizona.edu/arboretum/taxon.aspx?id=1080)); a California nursery similarly sells it as a rock fig for zones 9b–10, noting that the fat root caudex makes it popular as a bonsai and that in its cliffside habitat the roots wind around rocks in fused webs that hold the plant securely and act as moisture reserves ([Mid Valley Trees](https://midvalleytrees.com/product/ficus-palmeri/)). The Desert Museum's own captions draw a geographic distinction: their Ficus palmeri is the central Baja California hilltop-boulder specimen, while their tescalama photographs on the Rio Guajaray near Alamos are labeled Ficus petiolaris ([Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum](https://www.desertmuseum.org/programs/succulents_rockfigs.php)).

## Conservation

No conservation assessment is published under the name Ficus palmeri itself. The broadened taxon Ficus petiolaris, under which Kew synonymizes it, received a 2022 IUCN Red List assessment whose full text could not be independently read for this profile; Kew's own 2024 angiosperm threat prediction for Ficus petiolaris is "not threatened" (Kew confidence: "confident") ([Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:853408-1/general-information)).
