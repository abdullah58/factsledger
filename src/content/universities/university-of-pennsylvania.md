## Overview

The University of Pennsylvania spent 2026 doing something Ivy League institutions rarely do in public: changing leaders while fighting the federal government. In June, president J. Larry Jameson announced he will step down when his contract expires in June 2027 — the end of a tenure that began as an emergency interim appointment in December 2023, when his predecessor Liz Magill resigned under congressional fire over campus antisemitism ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/06/15/penn-president-leave-next-year)). Three months later, the university's most famous school made the opposite move: Wharton dean Erika H. James was reappointed through 2032, the first Wharton dean in two decades to be tapped for a second term ([Poets&Quants](https://poetsandquants.com/2026/09/24/breaking-wharton-dean-erika-james-reappointed-to-second-term/?pq-category=mba-news)). None of the encyclopedia entries on Penn — all frozen somewhere around the Franklin-and-Wharton canon — capture this churn. The school Benjamin Franklin founded in 1740 is, at nearly three centuries old, still being reshaped by the same pressures hitting every major research university: speech, protest, federal money, and public trust.

Jameson's exit closes a strange chapter. He took the helm when Penn faced sharp lawmaker scrutiny over its handling of antisemitism concerns, replacing Magill after she was, in Inside Higher Ed's phrasing, "skewered by lawmakers during a congressional hearing on antisemitism" ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/06/15/penn-president-leave-next-year)). Penn lifted his interim tag in 2025 and gave him a term through June 2027; his June 2026 announcement said it was "the right time" for new leadership, and board chair Ramanan Raghavendran said a search for the successor would begin that fall ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/06/15/penn-president-leave-next-year)).

## The swimmer, the freeze, and the apology

Part of what Jameson inherited was a fight with Washington over Lia Thomas, the transgender swimmer who competed on Penn's women's swimming and diving team in 2021–2022 ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/06/15/penn-president-leave-next-year)). After the Trump administration froze Penn's federal research funding, the university agreed to apologize to the cisgender female competitors who had lost to Thomas, among other concessions — and the administration subsequently restored the funding ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/06/15/penn-president-leave-next-year)). Jameson maintained that Penn had been fully compliant with Title IX and NCAA rules while conceding that "some student-athletes were disadvantaged by these rules" ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/06/15/penn-president-leave-next-year)). It was the kind of settlement that satisfied nobody completely and cost the university a year of research money — a case study, whether Penn wanted it or not, in how the federal funding lever now works on elite campuses.

## Wharton: the school that started business education

If Penn's recent history is turbulence, Wharton's is continuity. Founded in 1881 through industrialist Joseph Wharton's gift, it was the world's first collegiate business school — a radical idea when higher education meant the liberal arts, law, and theology ([University of Pennsylvania catalog](https://catalog.upenn.edu/undergraduate/wharton/)). Wharton, a self-educated industrialist, approached Penn's trustees in 1880 wanting an institution that would prepare leaders "for business and public service"; the first class numbered just 13 undergraduates ([University of Pennsylvania catalog](https://catalog.upenn.edu/undergraduate/wharton/)). Today the school counts more than 235 faculty, 5,000 students across its undergraduate, MBA, executive MBA, and doctoral programs, and over 105,000 alumni ([University of Pennsylvania catalog](https://catalog.upenn.edu/undergraduate/wharton/)).

That continuity is what made the September 2026 reappointment notable. Erika James, dean since 2020, won backing through 2032 after what President Jameson described as a review of academic investments, new programs, alumni relations, curricular reforms, revenue growth, and operational efficiency — endorsed unanimously by the consultative committee ([Poets&Quants](https://poetsandquants.com/2026/09/24/breaking-wharton-dean-erika-james-reappointed-to-second-term/?pq-category=mba-news)). In her own statement, James said she had spent her first term "betting on yourself" and now had "the utmost confidence in saying that there is no one I'd rather bet on than Wharton to lead whatever comes next" ([Poets&Quants](https://poetsandquants.com/2026/09/24/breaking-wharton-dean-erika-james-reappointed-to-second-term/?pq-category=mba-news)).

## Franklin's printer's-devil university

Most colonial colleges were founded to train clergy; Penn was not. Franklin's 1740 vision was unapologetically practical — commerce, public service, civic life — and the College became the first colonial institution to teach the sciences, government, and commerce alongside the classics, with the colonies' only non-sectarian faculty ([Penn School of Arts and Sciences](https://pan-school.sas.upenn.edu/index.php/about/history)). Its graduates and trustees signed the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution; after the state seized its assets in 1779 as a "hotbed of loyalism," a legal battle ended in the 1791 merger that created the University of Pennsylvania ([Penn School of Arts and Sciences](https://pan-school.sas.upenn.edu/index.php/about/history)). The school moved to its present University City site in 1872 ([Penn School of Arts and Sciences](https://pan-school.sas.upenn.edu/index.php/about/history)). It remains one of the nine colonial colleges and a founding member of the Association of American Universities, with ENIAC — the first general-purpose electronic computer — built at its Moore School in the 1940s and Penn Medicine's mRNA research later earning a Nobel Prize ([Experience Pennsylvania](https://www.experiencepa.com/university-of-pennsylvania/)).

## The Trump family's business school

The queue hook for this profile was Donald Trump Jr.'s alma mater, and the record is specific: he graduated from Wharton's undergraduate program in 2000 with a Bachelor of Science in economics, concentrating in marketing and real estate, according to the university paper cited by the USA Today network ([Beacon Journal](https://www.beaconjournal.com/story/news/politics/2025/07/24/donald-trump-children-college/85360790007/)). [Donald Trump Jr.](/people/donald-trump-jr/) is one of four Trump children with Penn degrees: his father earned a Wharton B.S. in economics in 1968, Ivanka graduated in 2004, and Tiffany took a sociology degree in 2016 before Georgetown Law — only Barron broke the pattern, choosing NYU ([Beacon Journal](https://www.beaconjournal.com/story/news/politics/2025/07/24/donald-trump-children-college/85360790007/)). The irony the profile cannot ignore: the same administration that froze Penn's research funding over the Thomas dispute is led by a Wharton graduate who has long brandished the school as proof of intellect.
