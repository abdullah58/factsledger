## Overview

The University of Waterloo is a public research university in Waterloo, Ontario, Canada, founded in 1957 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/637478)). It opened its doors that year to just 74 engineering students, with co-operative education as its cornerstone — alternating classroom terms with paid work terms — a model that has since grown into what an Ontario government study calls the world's largest postsecondary co-op program ([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/about/); [Higher Education Quality Council of Ontario](https://heqco.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/HEQCO_Waterloo_PD_Summary.pdf)). Today the university says more than 41,000 students attend annually, supported by a global alumni network of over 270,000 people in 160 countries ([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/about/)).

What the encyclopedia entries don't tell you is why this Canadian engineering school belongs in a series about the Trump presidency. In 2008, a software engineering undergraduate named Amir H. Ali graduated with his B.S.E. from Waterloo; three years later he took a J.D., magna cum laude, from Harvard Law School, and in late 2024 he joined the federal bench ([The White House (archived)](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2024/01/10/president-biden-names-forty-fourth-round-of-judicial-nominees/)). As a judge of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, Ali authored the rulings that ordered the Trump administration to pay roughly $2 billion in frozen foreign-aid funds and, in September 2026, blocked the conditioning of about $1.1 billion in counterterrorism grants on election-administration changes ([The White House (archived)](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2024/01/10/president-biden-names-forty-fourth-round-of-judicial-nominees/)). The software-engineering-to-federal-judge pipeline is not the career path Waterloo's co-op brochures advertise — but it is the thread that ties this campus to the center of the American separation-of-powers fight.

## History: an engineering school built around work

Waterloo's founding premise was practical: train engineers by putting them to work. The university's own account says it opened in 1957 to 74 engineering students "with co-operative education as its cornerstone" ([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/about/)). The Higher Education Quality Council of Ontario — an agency of the provincial government — confirms that co-op education was "a defining characteristic when it opened in 1957" ([Higher Education Quality Council of Ontario](https://heqco.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/HEQCO_Waterloo_PD_Summary.pdf)). Britannica lists its faculties today as applied health sciences, arts, engineering, environmental studies, mathematics, and science, alongside schools of accounting, architecture, optometry, and urban and regional planning ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/637478)).

The campus sits at 200 University Avenue West in Waterloo, Ontario ([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/about/)). From that single engineering cohort, the institution grew into one of Canada's major research universities — and into a startup factory. Its Velocity incubator, the university says, has created more than 10,000 jobs and is "Canada's most productive startup incubator by private investment" ([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/about/)).

## The co-op machine

The co-op program is Waterloo's signature, and the independent evidence for its scale is unusually strong. At the time of the HEQCO study, the university had 4,500 employer partners and offered alternating academic and workplace terms to more than 16,500 students across 120 academic programs — "making Waterloo's the world's largest postsecondary coop program," in the government agency's words ([Higher Education Quality Council of Ontario](https://heqco.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/HEQCO_Waterloo_PD_Summary.pdf)). The university's current figure is 8,000+ active co-op employers ([University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/about/)).

HEQCO also studied whether the program actually works, rather than just being large. Its evaluation of Waterloo's professional-development courses (WatPD, initiated in 2006) drew on more than 24,000 student end-of-course surveys, focus groups, and over 96,000 employer work-term evaluations ([Higher Education Quality Council of Ontario](https://heqco.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/HEQCO_Waterloo_PD_Summary.pdf)). Of the surveyed students, 61% rated the courses "very good" or "good," 27% "satisfactory," and 12% "poor" or "very poor," while testing showed a 23% increase in scores on course material from pretest to posttest ([Higher Education Quality Council of Ontario](https://heqco.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/HEQCO_Waterloo_PD_Summary.pdf)). The key finding was from the employer side: at every work-term level, students enrolled in the WatPD program performed slightly better than their pre-WatPD peers — a rare independent confirmation that the co-op machinery teaches something, not just places students ([Higher Education Quality Council of Ontario](https://heqco.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/HEQCO_Waterloo_PD_Summary.pdf)). The Chronicle of Higher Education, describing the scene, wrote that "every four months thousands of University of Waterloo students leave their classrooms for offices, factories, and hospitals," and that the co-op idea, though born in America, "has blossomed at Canada's Waterloo" ([Chronicle of Higher Education](https://www.chronicle.com/article/students-at-canadian-university-get-extensive-real-world-experience/)).

## A Nobel laureate and a judge

Waterloo's most decorated scholar is Donna Strickland, a professor in its Department of Physics and Astronomy and one of the recipients of the 2018 [Nobel Prize](/events/nobel-prize/) in Physics, awarded for developing chirped pulse amplification with Gérard Mourou — research they published in 1985 when Strickland was a PhD student at the University of Rochester ([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/events/nobel-week-dialogue/2020/panellists/donna-strickland/)). Strickland joined Waterloo in 1997, where her ultrafast laser group builds high-intensity laser systems for nonlinear optics research; she had previously worked at the National Research Council Canada, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, and Princeton ([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/events/nobel-week-dialogue/2020/panellists/donna-strickland/)).

The alumnus who matters most to this series, though, is [Amir Ali](/people/amir-ali/). The White House announcement of his judicial nomination records the essentials: B.S.E. from the University of Waterloo in Ontario, Canada, in 2008; J.D., magna cum laude, from Harvard Law School in 2011; clerkships for Judge [Raymond C. Fisher](/people/raymond-c-fisher/) on the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit (2011–2012) and Justice [Marshall Rothstein](/people/marshall-rothstein/) on the [Supreme Court of Canada](/organizations/supreme-court-of-canada/) (2012–2013); then private practice at Jenner & Block in Washington, D.C., before he became president and executive director of the [MacArthur Justice Center](/organizations/macarthur-justice-center/) ([The White House (archived)](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2024/01/10/president-biden-names-forty-fourth-round-of-judicial-nominees/)). That is the résumé of a software engineer who became the federal judge the Trump administration has faced across the courtroom more than once. Before the bench, Ali made his name at the MacArthur Justice Center, where he became president and executive director ([The White House (archived)](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2024/01/10/president-biden-names-forty-fourth-round-of-judicial-nominees/)), arguing civil-rights cases in the federal appellate courts — including a Supreme Court win in *Thompson v. Clark* and an en banc victory in the Fifth Circuit in the *Cole* case ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/theyve-got-next-the-40-under-40-amir-ali-of-macarthur-justice-center)). He also sat on the board of the Appellate Project and counted his former boss, Judge Fisher, as a mentor ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/theyve-got-next-the-40-under-40-amir-ali-of-macarthur-justice-center)). The path from a Waterloo engineering degree to the D.C. district court runs straight through the apprenticeship system of American appellate law — a long way from the co-op placements the university is known for.
