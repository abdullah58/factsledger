## Overview

The Massachusetts Institute of Technology is a private research university in Cambridge, Massachusetts, founded in 1861 by geologist William Barton Rogers to advance "the development and practical application of science in connection with arts, agriculture, manufactures and commerce" ([MIT News, 1999](https://news.mit.edu/1999/factsmit)). Classes began in Boston in 1865, and in 1916 the Institute moved to its newly constructed campus on the Cambridge side of the Charles River, financed by gifts from industrialists T. C. du Pont and George Eastman ([MIT News, 1999](https://news.mit.edu/1999/factsmit)).

Two institutions-within-the-institution define MIT's distinctive footprint more than its age does. The first is MIT OpenCourseWare (OCW), a free publishing project through which MIT puts virtually all of its course materials online for anyone to use ([MIT News, 2003](https://news.mit.edu/2003/ocw)). The second is MIT Lincoln Laboratory, a U.S. Department of Defense research center that MIT has operated in the national interest since 1951, for no fee ([MIT News, 2015](https://news.mit.edu/2015/air-force-renew-contract-mit-lincoln-laboratory-0428)).

Note: MIT is not the [California Institute of Technology](/universities/caltech) (Caltech) in Pasadena, California — a separate private research university that happens to share the "institute of technology" name and a comparable scientific reputation.

## What it is known for

MIT is known for applied science and engineering education organized around five schools — Architecture and Planning, Engineering, Humanities and Social Science, Management (Sloan), and Science — and for a research enterprise built substantially on federal sponsorship ([MIT News, 1999](https://news.mit.edu/1999/factsmit)). Financially, it is anchored by a $27.4 billion endowment (as of June 30, 2025), which supports 50 percent of undergraduate tuition, underpinning a need-blind admissions policy ([MIT News, 2025](https://news.mit.edu/2025/mit-releases-financials-and-endowment-figures-1010)).

## OpenCourseWare

OCW's origin story runs against the grain of typical university web strategy: a faculty committee formed in 1999 considered and discarded revenue-generating web ideas before settling on giving everything away, influenced by the open-source software model ([ZDNet](https://www.zdnet.com/article/mit-curriculum-goes-open-course/)). Free was not costless — the pilot, begun April 2001, was supported by two $5.5 million grants from the Hewlett and Mellon foundations, and MIT budgeted $20 million to the project over 20 years ([ZDNet](https://www.zdnet.com/article/mit-curriculum-goes-open-course/)).

Announced in April 2001, the OCW pilot site opened publicly in September 2002 with 32 courses; by September 2003 it had published its 500th course, spanning all 33 of MIT's disciplines and all five schools, with materials contributed by almost 600 of the Institute's 950 faculty members ([MIT News, 2003](https://news.mit.edu/2003/ocw)). OCW's stated dual mission was free searchable access to virtually all MIT course materials, plus a standards-based publishing model other universities could emulate ([MIT News, 2003](https://news.mit.edu/2003/ocw)). Materials may be reused by anyone, anywhere, noncommercially, with attribution and share-alike terms ([MIT News, 2003](https://news.mit.edu/2003/ocw)).

## Research funding model

MIT's research funding rests on two pillars: campus sponsored research, dominated by federal agencies, and the separately operated Lincoln Laboratory. Historically, MIT's five major campus sponsors were industry and four federal sources — the Departments of Energy, Defense, and Health and Human Services, plus NASA ([MIT News, 1999](https://news.mit.edu/1999/factsmit)). Under a 2015 contract, the Air Force renewed MIT's operation of Lincoln Laboratory — a Department of Defense Federally Funded Research and Development Center (FFRDC) — strictly on a cost-reimbursement, no-loss, no-gain basis ([MIT News, 2015](https://news.mit.edu/2015/air-force-renew-contract-mit-lincoln-laboratory-0428)). It is the sole national defense lab pairing R&D with rapid hardware prototyping; once developed, capabilities and hardware are turned over to industry for commercialization at scale ([MIT News, 2015](https://news.mit.edu/2015/air-force-renew-contract-mit-lincoln-laboratory-0428)).

In 2026, President Sally Kornbluth reported campus research funded by federal money down more than 20 percent year over year, with overall sponsored-research activity 10 percent smaller, and new graduate entrants down close to 20 percent (roughly 500 fewer students outside Sloan); she called it "a loss for the nation," noting federal funding was "not actually flowing to MIT the way it typically has" ([Financial Advisor/Bloomberg](https://www.fa-mag.com/news/mit-warns-of--striking-loss--from-funding--grad-enrollment-drops-87031.html?section=104)). She said MIT is pursuing corporate partnerships and new master's-only programs as alternative revenue, while a federal levy on university endowments rises from 1.4 percent to as much as 8 percent of net investment gains ([Financial Advisor/Bloomberg](https://www.fa-mag.com/news/mit-warns-of--striking-loss--from-funding--grad-enrollment-drops-87031.html?section=104)).

## Governance and leadership

MIT's governing body is the MIT Corporation, which elected Sally A. Kornbluth as MIT's 18th president in October 2022; she took office January 1, 2023, succeeding L. Rafael Reif after his 10-year tenure, and had previously served since 2014 as provost of Duke University ([MIT News, 2022](https://news.mit.edu/2022/sally-kornbluth-named-MIT-president-1020)). MIT enrolls almost 12,000 students — about 40 percent undergraduates — with roughly 30 percent international, coming from 137 countries ([Financial Advisor/Bloomberg](https://www.fa-mag.com/news/mit-warns-of--striking-loss--from-funding--grad-enrollment-drops-87031.html?section=104)).
