## Overview

Stanford University is a private research university in Stanford, California, founded in 1885 by railroad magnate Leland Stanford and his wife, Jane Lathrop Stanford in memory of their only child, Leland Stanford Jr., who had died the year before ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). But what distinguishes Stanford from every other elite research university is what it deliberately built around itself: the institution that commercialized academic research at scale — creator of the world's first university research park, pioneer of the modern technology-licensing office, and the campus whose graduates turned student projects into some of the largest companies in the world ([Stanford Research Park](https://stanfordresearchpark.com/about/); [Science|Business](https://sciencebusiness.net/news/74363/A-licence-to-print-money)).

Its scale is matched by its wealth: the university's endowment stood at $40.8 billion at the close of fiscal year 2025, the third-largest academic endowment in the United States, behind only Harvard and Yale ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/10/report-investment-portfolio-value-endowment); [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/business/financial-health/2026/02/12/endowment-returns-held-stable-fiscal-25)). As of Autumn Quarter 2025, Stanford enrolls 17,314 students across seven schools on an 8,353-acre campus nicknamed "the Farm" ([Stanford Facts](https://facts.stanford.edu/); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)).

## What it is known for

The university's areas of excellence span seven schools: the Graduate School of Business, the Doerr School of Sustainability, the School of Engineering, the Graduate School of Education, the School of Humanities and Sciences, the Law School, and the School of Medicine ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). Enrollment breaks down to 7,289 undergraduates and 10,025 graduate students ([Stanford Facts](https://facts.stanford.edu/)), and the student-to-faculty ratio is 6 to 1 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)).

The campus spans 8,353 acres with 630 buildings ([Stanford Facts](https://facts.stanford.edu/)). Stanford maintains study centers in France, Italy, Germany, England, Argentina, Mexico, Chile, Japan, and Russia, and roughly one-third of its undergraduates study abroad ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). Landmarks on the main campus include the Stanford Memorial Church, the Cantor Center for Visual Arts with its Rodin sculpture garden, and the Hanna House, completed in 1937 to a Frank Lloyd Wright design ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)).

The Hoover Institution was founded in 1919 by Stanford alumnus and future U.S. president Herbert Hoover to preserve documents relating to World War I ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). Its collection has since grown to about 1.6 million volumes and 50 million documents ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)).

The Stanford Linear Accelerator Center, known as SLAC, was established in 1962 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). The university also hosts the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, the Hopkins Marine Station at Pacific Grove, and the Jasper Ridge Biological Preserve ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). Across the institution, the university reports more than 7,500 externally sponsored research projects and 15 independent laboratories, centers, and institutes ([Stanford Facts](https://facts.stanford.edu/)).

Stanford's own count puts 20 living Nobel laureates on its roster, with 16 additional Stanford laureates deceased ([Stanford Report](https://news.stanford.edu/university-news/topic/awards-and-honors/nobel-prize)). The university is careful about how it claims laureates: it lists but does not claim winners who are not on the faculty, even those with a significant Stanford connection, and it omits winners whose connection is fleeting or tenuous ([Stanford Report](https://news.stanford.edu/university-news/topic/awards-and-honors/nobel-prize)).

The most famous omission is John Steinbeck, the 1962 literature laureate, who attended Stanford but did not make the institution's own list ([Stanford Report](https://news.stanford.edu/university-news/topic/awards-and-honors/nobel-prize)). Steinbeck earned a "C" in freshman English in 1919, dropped out in 1921, re-entered as a journalism major in 1923, and dropped out again in 1925 ([Stanford Report](https://news.stanford.edu/university-news/topic/awards-and-honors/nobel-prize)).

Recent laureates include chemist Carolyn Bertozzi, who won the 2022 Nobel Prize in chemistry for her development of bioorthogonal reactions, which allow scientists to explore cells and track biological processes without disrupting the normal chemistry of the cell ([Stanford Report](https://news.stanford.edu/university-news/topic/awards-and-honors/nobel-prize)).

Other notable Stanford alumni include writers Ken Kesey and Jeffrey Eugenides, artist Robert Motherwell, U.S. Supreme Court Justices William Rehnquist and Sandra Day O'Connor, astronaut Sally Ride, and golfer Tiger Woods ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). Nobel-winning faculty named by Britannica include economist Milton Friedman, biochemist Arthur Kornberg, and physicist Burton Richter ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)).

At the 2024 Paris Olympics, 59 Stanford-affiliated athletes won 39 medals ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). Among the university's alumni in science and technology is [Dario Amodei](/people/dario-amodei), who enrolled at [Caltech](/universities/caltech) to study physics before transferring to Stanford, where he earned his bachelor's degree in physics in 2006; he went on to a Princeton doctorate in physics in 2011 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Dario-Amodei)).

## The Silicon Valley relationship

Stanford's entanglement with Silicon Valley was not an accident of geography — it was a program. In the 1930s, Frederick Terman, then a professor in Stanford's Department of Electrical Engineering, was concerned that his graduates had to leave for the East Coast to find good jobs, and he decided to try to spawn a local industry in radio engineering. He brought together two of his former students, William Hewlett and David Packard, and convinced them to start Hewlett-Packard ([Science|Business](https://sciencebusiness.net/news/74363/A-licence-to-print-money)).

In the 1950s, Terman — by then Provost and Dean of Engineering — created the Stanford Industrial Park, leasing offices on Stanford property to technology companies ([Science|Business](https://sciencebusiness.net/news/74363/A-licence-to-print-money)). The park was his brainchild: he saw the potential of a university-affiliated business park focused on research and development that would generate income for the university and the community ([Stanford Research Park](https://stanfordresearchpark.com/about/)). In a pioneering partnership, leaders within the City of Palo Alto and Stanford agreed to annex 700 acres of Stanford land into the City of Palo Alto, generating tax revenue for the county, the city, and the school district ([Stanford Research Park](https://stanfordresearchpark.com/about/)). The renamed Stanford Research Park is the world's first university research park.

Its tenant lineage reads as a history of Silicon Valley itself: Varian Associates, the first tenant, developed the microwave tube behind satellite technology and particle accelerators; Hewlett-Packard developed electronic measuring instruments that led to medical equipment, hand-held calculators, mainframe computers, and laser printers; Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center created the personal workstation, Ethernet cabling, and the computer mouse; Steve Jobs's NeXT Computer pushed graphics and audio in personal computing; Lockheed Martin's space and missile division developed critical components for the International Space Station; and Facebook grew its social network from 20 million to 750 million users while headquartered in the Park ([Stanford Research Park](https://stanfordresearchpark.com/about/)). Today, Tesla's engineering headquarters develops and assembles electric-vehicle and battery prototypes there, and electric-vehicle maker Rivian occupies the buildings of the park's first tenant, Varian ([Stanford Research Park](https://stanfordresearchpark.com/about/)). The park now houses more than 150 companies across 10 million square feet in 140 buildings ([Stanford Research Park](https://stanfordresearchpark.com/about/)).

Terman also lured William Shockley, co-inventor of the transistor, to the office park and a Stanford professorship; most of the chip companies in Silicon Valley trace their roots to Shockley Semiconductor ([Science|Business](https://sciencebusiness.net/news/74363/A-licence-to-print-money)).

The second institutional pillar was technology transfer. Niels J. Reimers joined Stanford in 1968 as Associate Director of its Sponsored Projects Office and proposed an Office of Technology Licensing (OTL), replacing a system that relied on an outside consulting firm and had netted the school just $5,000 over 15 years; the pilot program brought in $55,000 in its first year ([Science|Business](https://sciencebusiness.net/news/74363/A-licence-to-print-money)). Reimers's model — marketing inventions rather than merely patenting them, staffed by people with science degrees and industry experience — became the template for university licensing offices worldwide. After taking a 15 percent fee, Stanford's OTL gives a third of the stock or royalties it receives to the inventors, with the remainder split between the inventor's department and school to fund other research ([Science|Business](https://sciencebusiness.net/news/74363/A-licence-to-print-money)). Its biggest early hit was the Cohen–Boyer "gene splicing" patent, developed jointly by Stanley Cohen of Stanford and Herbert Boyer of the University of California: by the time the patent expired in 1997, the universities' share of the royalties had reached $255 million, and the technique was licensed to more than 400 companies ([Science|Business](https://sciencebusiness.net/news/74363/A-licence-to-print-money)).

The model's most famous payoff was [Google](/organizations/google). Sergey Brin and Larry Page met as graduate students at Stanford University and began working from Page's dormitory room on a new search technology they dubbed BackRub ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). When the pair took a leave of absence and incorporated Google in 1998, Stanford received nearly two million shares in exchange for an exclusive licence to the software patents they had developed — the largest university technology-licensing gain of its era ([Science|Business](https://sciencebusiness.net/news/74363/A-licence-to-print-money)).

The startup pipeline formalized further with StartX, founded in 2009 by Cameron Teitelman as a student-run organization. It is a nonprofit accelerator for Stanford-affiliated founders — deliberately structured as an organization separate from the university so Stanford avoids intellectual-property ownership conflicts — and it takes no equity from the companies it admits ([BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/startx-celebrates-10-years-helping-stanford-s-top-tech-and-medical-entrepreneurs-solve-real-world-problems)). Per its tenth-anniversary release, approximately 700 companies had gotten their start in the StartX community, with a combined valuation exceeding $19 billion and $7.9 billion raised ([BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/startx-celebrates-10-years-helping-stanford-s-top-tech-and-medical-entrepreneurs-solve-real-world-problems)).

## Endowment and finances

Stanford's endowment was valued at $40.8 billion on August 31, 2025, the close of its fiscal year ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/10/report-investment-portfolio-value-endowment)). In the National Association of College and University Business Officers' FY25 study, Stanford's endowment ranked as the third-largest academic endowment in the United States at $40.7 billion, behind Harvard's $55.6 billion and Yale's $44.1 billion ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/business/financial-health/2026/02/12/endowment-returns-held-stable-fiscal-25)).

The university's principal investment vehicle is the Merged Pool, which was valued at $47.7 billion on June 30, 2025 ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/10/report-investment-portfolio-value-endowment)). The Merged Pool returned 14.3 percent for the year ending June 30, 2025, with five- and ten-year annualized returns of 11.7 and 9.4 percent respectively — ahead of the median U.S. college endowment over the same periods ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/10/report-investment-portfolio-value-endowment)). Stanford Management Company, the university's investment office, manages the Merged Pool under fiduciary law and Stanford's Ethical Investment Framework ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/10/report-investment-portfolio-value-endowment)).

The endowment is a major pillar of the operating budget: it disbursed $1.9 billion in fiscal year 2025 — nearly double the $1.1 billion disbursed in 2015 — funding academic programs and financial aid, and $2.0 billion is expected to flow to the operating budget in fiscal year 2026 ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/10/report-investment-portfolio-value-endowment)). Approximately 75 percent of the payout is restricted to specific purposes by donors; the unrestricted remainder helps cushion fiscal shocks ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/10/report-investment-portfolio-value-endowment)). Stanford News cites the federal excise tax on endowments rising from 1.4 to 8.0 percent, along with uncertainty around federal research funding, as reasons the endowment "remains a critical part of Stanford's operating budget" ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/10/report-investment-portfolio-value-endowment)).

## Founding and history

Stanford was founded in 1885 by Leland and Jane Stanford in memory of their only child, Leland Stanford Jr., who had died the year before ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). The university opened on October 1, 1891, with 555 registered students ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). It was coeducational from its opening — at a time when most schools accepted only men — and it carried no religious affiliation when most other universities did ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)).

The campus was built on the Stanfords' Palo Alto stock farm, which gave rise to its enduring nickname, "the Farm" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). The land is the ancestral territory of the Muwekma Ohlone Tribe ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)). The Main Quad's buildings were conceived by landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted and designed by architect Charles Allerton Coolidge in the Richardsonian Romanesque and Mission Revival styles ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Stanford-University)).
