## Overview

Western Carolina University is a public university in Cullowhee, Jackson County, North Carolina, set in the Blue Ridge Mountains of the state's far west — and it exists because the mountains needed teachers. Founded in 1889 as a small school to train educators for the region, it became a constituent institution of the University of North Carolina System in 1972 and today also operates a satellite campus at Biltmore Park in Asheville ([Sylva Herald](https://www.thesylvaherald.com/history/article_35260c74-82b3-11e7-bf5e-dfe0d645a23b.html)). Its athletic teams are the Catamounts, competing in the Southern Conference ([ETSU Athletics](https://etsubucs.com/news/2026/9/26/football-bucs-roll-past-western-carolina-in-socon-opener-58-31.aspx)).

What distinguishes WCU from other regional publics is not its rankings but its relationship with the Cherokee people: the campus sits on former Cherokee land, and the university has spent decades embedded in Cherokee language revitalization — a partnership formalized in a 2021 agreement with all three federally recognized Cherokee tribes ([One Feather](https://theonefeather.com/2021/07/25/tribes-enter-into-moa-with-western-carolina-univ-for-language-preservation/), [EdNC](https://www.ednc.org/preserving-cherokee-language-ten-books-at-a-time/)).

## From Cullowhee Academy to a regional university

The university's own origin story, as told by Jackson County's local newspaper, begins in 1888, when Cullowhee-area leaders formed a board of trustees to establish a school first known as Cullowhee High School ([Sylva Herald](https://www.thesylvaherald.com/history/article_35260c74-82b3-11e7-bf5e-dfe0d645a23b.html)). The state recognized the school in 1893 with its first appropriation, explicitly to educate teachers; in 1905 North Carolina took over the grounds and buildings and renamed it Cullowhee Normal and Industrial School ([Sylva Herald](https://www.thesylvaherald.com/history/article_35260c74-82b3-11e7-bf5e-dfe0d645a23b.html)). By the mid-1920s the coeducational institution had trained more than 2,000 teachers ([Sylva Herald](https://www.thesylvaherald.com/history/article_35260c74-82b3-11e7-bf5e-dfe0d645a23b.html)).

The modern names arrived in sequence: the N.C. Legislature chartered a four-year program in 1929 and the school became Western Carolina Teachers College (its first bachelor's graduate in 1931, first master's in 1952), then Western Carolina College in 1953, and finally Western Carolina University in 1967 after the General Assembly designated it a regional university ([Sylva Herald](https://www.thesylvaherald.com/history/article_35260c74-82b3-11e7-bf5e-dfe0d645a23b.html)). It joined the UNC System in 1972 ([Sylva Herald](https://www.thesylvaherald.com/history/article_35260c74-82b3-11e7-bf5e-dfe0d645a23b.html)).

## The Cherokee partnership: language, curriculum, and land

WCU's deepest institutional relationship is with the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, whose Qualla Boundary lies about twenty miles from campus. The university runs a Cherokee Studies Program that develops and coordinates Native American and Cherokee courses and is heavily involved in language revitalization, plus a Cherokee Center aimed at improving educational opportunities for Qualla Boundary residents ([One Feather](https://theonefeather.com/2016/06/27/wcu-renews-agreement-to-continue-support-of-cherokee-language-history-culture/)).

That relationship was formalized at the highest level in July 2021. A little more than two years after the three federally recognized Cherokee tribes declared a state of emergency for the Cherokee language, the Eastern Band, the Cherokee Nation, and the United Keetoowah Band signed a "Memorandum of Agreement for Cherokee Language, History, and Culture" with WCU at the Kituwah Celebration, held at the Kituwah Mound — the Mother Town of the Cherokee ([One Feather](https://theonefeather.com/2021/07/25/tribes-enter-into-moa-with-western-carolina-univ-for-language-preservation/)). Principal Chief Richard Sneed publicly thanked the university for "recognizing that it is sacred ground" and for making Cherokee culture and language "a key part of the curriculum and a priority at the university" ([One Feather](https://theonefeather.com/2021/07/25/tribes-enter-into-moa-with-western-carolina-univ-for-language-preservation/)). The agreement calls for a joint strategic plan, shared curriculum and assessments, quarterly meetings, an annual public report, and a nine-person "Teach What You Know, Share What You Learn" delegation ([One Feather](https://theonefeather.com/2021/07/25/tribes-enter-into-moa-with-western-carolina-univ-for-language-preservation/)).

The work is concrete, not ceremonial. In 2024 the Cherokee One Feather's reporter sat in on WCU's Cherokee Language Certificate Program — a hybrid course equivalent to college-level introductory Cherokee, free for members of federally recognized tribes and Eastern Band employees — taught by Jack "Tsegi" Cooper, himself a second-language learner, alongside fluent speaker Gilliam Jackson ([One Feather](https://theonefeather.com/2024/09/18/wcu-cherokee-language-certificate-program-is-in-action/)). And WCU printmaking students, led by professor Tatiana Potts, hand-produce limited-edition Cherokee-language picture books for the New Kituwah Academy, the tribe's preschool-through-6th-grade immersion school — books whose subjects and syllabary text are decided by adult Cherokee language learners, with Potts noting pointedly that "the university is placed on former Cherokee land" and that she wanted "a real connection," not just a land acknowledgement ([EdNC](https://www.ednc.org/preserving-cherokee-language-ten-books-at-a-time/)). With only about 200 first-language Cherokee speakers remaining, the stakes of that connection are existential ([EdNC](https://www.ednc.org/preserving-cherokee-language-ten-books-at-a-time/)).

## Growth, records, and a hurricane

WCU's enrollment story is one of steady, deliberate growth. In fall 2019, total enrollment crossed 12,000 for the first time — 12,167 students, up 4.54% — powered by distance education (up over 10% to 2,451 students), graduate enrollment (1,698, up 5.33%), and a surge of returning undergraduates; it was the eighth increase in nine years ([One Feather](https://theonefeather.com/2019/09/03/wcu-enrollment-hits-12000-for-new-record-driven-by-gains-in-distance-graduate-populations/)). Chancellor Kelli R. Brown called the slightly smaller freshman class "by design," part of an effort to grow "strategically and sustainably" ([One Feather](https://theonefeather.com/2019/09/03/wcu-enrollment-hits-12000-for-new-record-driven-by-gains-in-distance-graduate-populations/)).

That trajectory was interrupted by Hurricane Helene in September 2024, which killed more than 100 people statewide and forced WCU and the UNC System's other two mountain campuses to suspend operations ([Reflector](https://www.reflector.com/apgstate/unc-system-campuses-in-western-nc-rebound-with-spring-enrollment-gains/article_efb274fc-9f4e-5431-ab82-1103c14a8ab1.html)). The rebound came quickly: spring 2025 enrollment rose 1.7% over spring 2024 (3.3% over spring 2023), with emergency funding from the General Assembly repairing damaged infrastructure and boosting financial aid — and all three campuses back fully operational ([Reflector](https://www.reflector.com/apgstate/unc-system-campuses-in-western-nc-rebound-with-spring-enrollment-gains/article_efb274fc-9f4e-5431-ab82-1103c14a8ab1.html)).
