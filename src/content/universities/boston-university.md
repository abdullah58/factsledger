## Overview

Boston University in 2026 is a study in contrasts. In July 2025, the university announced it would cut its budget by an average of 5% for fiscal 2026, laying off about 120 people and eliminating another 120 vacant positions — steps four senior leaders called "a day of loss for all of us" but "a necessary step in ensuring our future" ([BU Today](https://www.bu.edu/articles/2025/university-announces-budget-cuts-layoffs-financial-pressures/)). Barely a year later, starting with the 2026–27 academic year, the same university began covering full tuition for students whose families earn up to $200,000 — the BU Promise — with family contributions capped at $20,000 and families earning up to $75,000 getting all costs covered ([Inside Higher Ed](http://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/08/10/bu-offers-free-tuition-families-earning-200k)). Austerity and generosity, in the same institution, in the same year.

The generosity is the older thread. BU began as a Methodist theological seminary — the Newbury Biblical Institute, founded in Newbury, Vermont, on April 24–25, 1839, after Methodist ministers and laymen meeting at Boston's Old Bromfield Street Church voted to establish it ([wiki.Alumni.NET](https://wiki.alumni.net/mediawiki/index.php?title=North_America/United_States_of_America/Massachusetts/Boston/Boston_University/&)). But its 1869 charter was strikingly nonsectarian for its era: no instructor could be required to profess particular religious opinions, no student could be refused admission on account of religious opinions, and every department was open to all regardless of sex, race, or religion — the School of Theology excepted ([wiki.Alumni.NET](https://wiki.alumni.net/mediawiki/index.php?title=North_America/United_States_of_America/Massachusetts/Boston/Boston_University/&)). The BU Promise is arguably that 1869 clause expressed in 2026 dollars.

## A Vermont seminary becomes a Boston university

The institute spent its first decades migrating: to Concord, New Hampshire, in 1847 as the Methodist General Biblical Institute, then to Boston in 1867, where it was rechartered as the Boston Theological Seminary on February 27 ([wiki.Alumni.NET](https://wiki.alumni.net/mediawiki/index.php?title=North_America/United_States_of_America/Massachusetts/Boston/Boston_University/&); [BU Timeline](https://www.bu.edu/timeline/?p=352)). In 1869, three trustees — Boston businessmen and Methodist laymen Isaac Rich, Lee Claflin, and Jacob Sleeper — obtained a Massachusetts Legislature charter for a university to be called "Boston University," which Governor William Claflin (Lee Claflin's son) signed on May 26, 1869 ([wiki.Alumni.NET](https://wiki.alumni.net/mediawiki/index.php?title=North_America/United_States_of_America/Massachusetts/Boston/Boston_University/&)). On February 27, 1871, the seminary was renamed the School of Theology — the university's first school ([BU Timeline](https://www.bu.edu/timeline/1871/02/27/first-school-named/)).

The young university's finances nearly collapsed before it began. Isaac Rich died in January 1872, leaving the bulk of his estate — appraised at more than $1.5 million, mostly Boston real estate — to BU. But the Great Boston Fire of December 1872 destroyed nearly all of the bequeathed buildings, and the insurance companies that had covered them went bankrupt; the bequest, delivered in 1882, was worth about half its original value ([wiki.Alumni.NET](https://wiki.alumni.net/mediawiki/index.php?title=North_America/United_States_of_America/Massachusetts/Boston/Boston_University/&)).

## Built partly on Harvard's defectors

BU's law school owes an early debt to its rival across the river. When Christopher Columbus Langdell became dean of [Harvard Law School](https://factsledger.com/organizations/harvard-law-school) in 1870, he revolutionized legal education — introducing the case method and the Socratic approach, standardizing the curriculum, and ranking students by class. The methods were, in Harvard historian Daniel Coquillette's telling, "so shocking at the time" that there was an exodus of Harvard students, "many of whom fled to the newly founded Boston University Law School" ([Harvard Law School](http://hls.harvard.edu/today/talking-about-a-revolution/)). BU's legal education tradition was, in part, built by students Harvard could not keep.

Today BU enrolls 36,977 students — 18,289 undergraduates and 18,688 graduate students — as a private nonprofit university headquartered at One Silber Way in Boston ([Boston University Common Data Set](https://www.bu.edu/asir/files/2026/07/CDS-2025-2026-updated.pdf)). It is selective: for fall 2025, 76,776 students applied for first-time first-year admission and 9,853 were admitted, about a 12.8% admit rate ([Boston University Common Data Set](https://www.bu.edu/asir/files/2026/07/CDS-2025-2026-updated.pdf)).

## Free tuition and a 5% cut: 2025–26

The budget cuts announced in July 2025 were part of a sector-wide retrenchment: Harvard, Northwestern, Cornell, Stanford, the University of Oregon, Temple, and the University of Maryland had all announced reduced budgets or layoffs in the preceding months, all citing some mix of federal funding uncertainty, inflation, and demographic shifts ([BU Today](https://www.bu.edu/articles/2025/university-announces-budget-cuts-layoffs-financial-pressures/)). At BU, the reductions would be spread across the university — some areas cut less than 5%, others more — with around 20 additional positions seeing schedule changes; the layoffs affected about 1% of employees, who were offered separation packages and career transition services ([BU Today](https://www.bu.edu/articles/2025/university-announces-budget-cuts-layoffs-financial-pressures/)).

The BU Promise, announced in August 2026, moves in the opposite direction on affordability. For domestic first-year students from families earning up to $200,000 with typical assets, BU now covers full tuition — just over $73,000 for 2026–27 — plus a portion of housing and dining, capping family contributions at $20,000; standard room and board runs about $22,000 ([Inside Higher Ed](http://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/08/10/bu-offers-free-tuition-families-earning-200k)). It joins Swarthmore, Yale, Harvard, and Rice among selective institutions that have raised the free-tuition threshold to $200,000 ([Inside Higher Ed](http://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/08/10/bu-offers-free-tuition-families-earning-200k)). "No family should rule out Boston University because they assume it's beyond their reach," said Chris Sedore, vice president and associate provost for enrollment and student administration ad interim ([Inside Higher Ed](http://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/08/10/bu-offers-free-tuition-families-earning-200k)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim BU has no sectarian character — only that its 1869 charter barred religious tests for instructors and religious bars on admission, a provision reported on a tier3 source (see openItems). It does not resolve the discrepancy over the name of the 1867 Boston charter (Boston Theological Seminary vs. Boston Theological Institute). It does not state BU's endowment or any university rankings, since no fully-read source confirmed them. It does not claim the BU Promise covers international students or continuing students — the announcement summarized here describes eligibility in terms of household income and typical assets only. It does not claim the July 2025 layoffs are complete or final; it reports only the announced actions.
