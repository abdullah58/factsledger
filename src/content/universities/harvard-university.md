## Overview

Harvard University is a private research university in Cambridge, Massachusetts, founded October 28, 1636 by vote of the Great and General Court of the Massachusetts Bay Colony — the first college in the American colonies, per Harvard's own account ([Harvard University](https://harvard.edu/about)). It is best understood as two coupled machines: a $56.9 billion endowment run by a dedicated investment company — the largest of any university in the world — and the institution whose undergraduate admissions system triggered the 2023 Supreme Court decision that ended race-conscious college admissions nationwide ([Reuters](https://theprint.in/world/harvard-endowment-swells-to-nearly-57-billion-donations-reach-a-record/2765631/)).

## What it is known for

In fiscal 2025 the endowment grew nearly $4 billion to $56.9 billion on an 11.9% return, beating Harvard's 8% target (fiscal 2024: +9.6% to $53.2 billion). The portfolio favors alternatives: 41% private equity, 31% hedge funds, 14% public equities, per Harvard Management Company chief executive N.P. Narvekar. A record $600 million in unrestricted gifts was also reported that year ([Reuters](https://theprint.in/world/harvard-endowment-swells-to-nearly-57-billion-donations-reach-a-record/2765631/)).

## Institutional role and schools

Harvard counts 24,317 undergraduate and graduate students and more than 400,000 alumni worldwide ([Harvard University](https://harvard.edu/about)). It comprises Harvard College — the original undergraduate college — plus twelve degree-granting graduate and professional schools across three greater-Boston campuses: Cambridge (most schools, including Law, the Kennedy School, and Design), Allston (Business School), and Longwood in Boston (Medical School, Dental Medicine, Public Health) ([Harvard University](https://hr.harvard.edu/harvards-schools-and-departments)).

The "College" vs "University" distinction matters legally: the respondent in the 2023 Supreme Court case was the "President and Fellows of Harvard College" — the Harvard Corporation, the university's senior governing body — and the challenged program was Harvard College's undergraduate admissions system, not graduate admissions ([Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2023/6/supreme-court-decides-students-for-fair-admissions-inc-v-president-and-fellows-of-harvard-college-and-students-for-fair-admissions-inc-v-university-of-north-carolina-et-al)).

## Governance/leadership

Harvard is governed by two bodies: the Corporation (President and Fellows) and the Board of Overseers ([The Harvard Crimson](https://thecrimson.com/article/2025/12/16/garber-to-be-extended/)). Alan M. Garber became interim president in January 2024 after Claudine Gay, the university's 30th president, stepped down, and was named president that August ([Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2024/01/harvard-president-claudine-gay-steps-down/)). In December 2025 the governing boards extended his presidency indefinitely amid intensifying political pressure from the Trump administration ([The Harvard Crimson](https://thecrimson.com/article/2025/12/16/garber-to-be-extended/)).

## Finances/endowment (verified only)

The endowment is not a bank account. In fiscal 2025 it distributed $2.5 billion — 37% of operating revenue. Its ~14,765 funds are overwhelmingly donor-directed to specific programs; only about 20% is unrestricted. The general payout rate is 5.0 to 5.5% of market value, and each of the twelve schools "owns" its share ([Harvard University](https://finance.harvard.edu/endowment)). The endowment is managed by the Harvard Management Company, a nonprofit wholly-owned subsidiary formed in 1974, governed by a board appointed by the President and Fellows ([HMC](https://www.hmc.harvard.edu/about/)). A forward-looking risk: a new federal bill passed in 2025 increases the excise "endowment tax" on net investment income for institutions like Harvard ([Harvard University](https://finance.harvard.edu/endowment)).

## The 2023 admissions case and aftermath

On June 29, 2023, the Supreme Court struck down Harvard's and UNC's race-conscious admissions programs (Nos. 20-1199/21-707) as violations of the Equal Protection Clause and Title VI: 6–2 in the Harvard case (Justice Jackson took no part, having served on Harvard's Board of Overseers) and 6–3 in the UNC case ([Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2023/6/supreme-court-decides-students-for-fair-admissions-inc-v-president-and-fellows-of-harvard-college-and-students-for-fair-admissions-inc-v-university-of-north-carolina-et-al)).

The Roberts majority held that a race-conscious program must (1) satisfy strict scrutiny, (2) never use race as a stereotype or negative, and (3) eventually end — and that both programs failed all three: the asserted interests (training leaders, diverse outlooks, marketplace of ideas, engaged citizens) were not "sufficiently measurable to permit judicial review"; zero-sum admissions meant race operated as a "negative"; and tying the programs' end to demographic representation was racial balancing by another name. Harvard's own system was unusually detailed in the record: every application went to a "first reader" scoring six categories — academic, extracurricular, athletic, school support, personal, overall — with race considered at every stage, proving "a determinative factor for a significant percentage of all admitted African American and Hispanic students" ([Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2023/6/supreme-court-decides-students-for-fair-admissions-inc-v-president-and-fellows-of-harvard-college-and-students-for-fair-admissions-inc-v-university-of-north-carolina-et-al)).

The first post-ruling class (2028) was 14% Black, down from 18%; the Hispanic share rose 14% to 16%; the Asian American share held steady at 37%. MIT saw a steeper drop; Yale and Princeton showed little change ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/harvards-black-enrollment-dips-after-us-supreme-court-bars-affirmative-action/)). BET reported, citing the New York Times, that the class of 2029 moved further to 11.5% Black and 11% Latino, with Asian American enrollment at a record 41% ([BET](https://www.bet.com/article/e04w4b/harvard-reports-fewer-black-and-latino-students-this-year)).
