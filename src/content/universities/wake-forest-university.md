## Overview

Wake Forest University is a private university in Winston-Salem, North Carolina, founded in 1834 near Raleigh on a plantation; it moved to Winston-Salem in 1956 ([The Journal of Blacks in Higher Education](http://jbhe.com/2020/02/wake-forest-university-apologizes-for-its-historical-ties-to-slavery/)). The university's distinctive thread is a long, documented arc from slavery to integration: Ed Reynolds, from Ghana, became the first Black full-time student when he enrolled in 1962 and graduated in 1964, and the university observed the 50th anniversary of racial integration in 2012 ([The Journal of Blacks in Higher Education](https://jbhe.com/2012/09/wake-forest-university-celebrates-50-years-of-racial-integration/)). In 2020, Wake Forest formally apologized for its historical ties to slavery ([The Journal of Blacks in Higher Education](http://jbhe.com/2020/02/wake-forest-university-apologizes-for-its-historical-ties-to-slavery/)). Its motto is "Pro Humanitate" — "for humanity" ([Expert Admissions](http://expertadmissions.com/spotlight-on-wake-forest-university/)).

## A plantation college reckons with its past

Wake Forest was founded in 1834 near Raleigh, on a plantation, and remained there for more than a century before moving to its Reynolda campus in Winston-Salem in 1956 ([The Journal of Blacks in Higher Education](http://jbhe.com/2020/02/wake-forest-university-apologizes-for-its-historical-ties-to-slavery/)). Racial integration came in 1962, when Ed Reynolds, a student from Ghana, enrolled as the university's first Black full-time student; he graduated in 1964 ([The Journal of Blacks in Higher Education](https://jbhe.com/2012/09/wake-forest-university-celebrates-50-years-of-racial-integration/)). The university marked the 50th anniversary of that integration in 2012 — half a century after Reynolds arrived on campus ([The Journal of Blacks in Higher Education](https://jbhe.com/2012/09/wake-forest-university-celebrates-50-years-of-racial-integration/)).

That history sits inside a wider regional struggle. Baptist News Global has documented the fight to integrate even progressive Baptist institutions in the 1960s, reporting on the desegregation struggle at Baptist institutions including Wake Forest ([Baptist News Global](https://baptistnews.com/article/remembering-the-struggle-to-integrate-even-progressive-baptist-churches-in-the-1960s/)). The arc continued into the present: in February 2020, the university apologized for its historical ties to slavery — an institutional acknowledgment coming nearly two centuries after its founding on plantation land ([The Journal of Blacks in Higher Education](http://jbhe.com/2020/02/wake-forest-university-apologizes-for-its-historical-ties-to-slavery/)). From plantation origins in 1834, to a first Black student in 1962, to a formal apology in 2020, the institution's own record traces one of the longer documented reckonings in American higher education.

## The 2008 test-optional move

Apart from its historical reckoning, Wake Forest made a distinctive mark on American admissions policy. In June 2008, the university stopped requiring applicants to submit SAT or ACT scores, "joining the ranks of test-optional colleges," in the words of Chronicle of Higher Education reporters Eric Hoover and Beckie Supiano ([The Chronicle of Higher Education](http://fs.ncaa.org/Docs/DII_Acad_Req/2009/February/S9a.pdf)). The Chronicle called Wake Forest "one of the most prominent institutions with a 'test optional' admissions policy," and noted that the university — located in Winston-Salem, N.C. — had accepted 42 percent of undergraduate applicants the previous year ([The Chronicle of Higher Education](http://fs.ncaa.org/Docs/DII_Acad_Req/2009/February/S9a.pdf)).

The decision gave the university a second identity in the higher-education press: an institution simultaneously excavating a difficult 19th-century past and reshaping 21st-century admissions practice. The test-optional move broadened the profile of Wake Forest beyond its Southern Baptist and regional roots, placing it in a national conversation about access — while its integration history and 2020 apology anchored a separate conversation about institutional accountability.
