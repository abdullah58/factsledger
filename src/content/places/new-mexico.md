## Overview

New Mexico, nicknamed the Land of Enchantment, is a southwestern state of the [United States](/places/united-states/) admitted as the 47th state on 6 January 1912, with Santa Fe as its capital, about 2.1 million residents, and a landscape that runs from Great Plains to high desert to the Rocky Mountains ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico); [US Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/NM/PST045224)).

New Mexico ranks fifth among the 50 states in total area, at 121,590 square miles, and is bounded by Colorado to the north, Oklahoma and [Texas](/places/texas/) to the east, Texas and the Mexican states of Chihuahua and Sonora to the south, and Arizona to the west ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). At its northwestern corner it joins Arizona, Utah and Colorado in the only four-way meeting of states in the United States ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). The Census Bureau's July 2025 population estimate is 2,125,498, up just 0.4 per cent from the April 2020 base — a stat that, combined with a land area of 121,312.75 square miles, makes New Mexico one of the most thinly settled states in the country, at 17.5 people per square mile in 2020 ([US Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/NM/PST045224)). Its capital, Santa Fe, was founded in 1610 and is the oldest continuously used seat of government in North America, while about two-fifths of residents live in [Albuquerque](/places/albuquerque/), founded in 1706, and surrounding Bernalillo County ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)).

## From Spanish province to 47th state

The area that is now New Mexico was claimed by Spain in the 16th century, became part of Mexico in 1821, and was ceded to the United States in 1848 through the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). Spanish explorers led by Francisco Vásquez de Coronado first entered the area in 1540, and permanent settlement followed in the early 17th century, with Santa Fe founded in 1610 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). From 1821 to 1880, Santa Fe was the terminus of the Santa Fe Trail, a wagon trail that served as a major commercial and migration route from Missouri to the Southwest until the railroad was completed ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)).

Britannica frames the state's modern identity as a fusion of Spanish American, Native American and Anglo-American cultures, with Pueblo, Navajo and Apache peoples inhabiting the land for thousands of years before Europeans arrived ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). That fusion is still visible in the numbers: 49.4 per cent of residents are Hispanic or Latino, 11.6 per cent are American Indian and Alaska Native alone, and 31.3 per cent speak a language other than English at home ([US Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/NM/PST045224)). More than two-fifths of the population is of Hispanic heritage, tracing back to original Spanish settlers who intermarried with Native Americans ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). The state motto, "Crescit Eundo" — it grows as it goes — and the yucca flower and the song "O, Fair New Mexico" complete the official identity Britannica records ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)).

## [Chaco Canyon](/places/chaco-canyon/): the Pueblo world at the state's heart

The information most state profiles miss is that New Mexico's deepest history is not Spanish at all — it is Pueblo, and its centre was Chaco Canyon in the state's northwest. The Chacoan cultural flowering began in the mid-800s and lasted more than 300 years, producing massive stone great houses of multiple storeys containing hundreds of rooms, planned from the start rather than added to room by room ([US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/index.htm)). The earliest great houses — Pueblo Bonito, Una Vida and Peñasco Blanco — went up in the middle and late 800s, followed by Hungo Pavi, Chetro Ketl, Pueblo Alto and others, many oriented to solar, lunar and cardinal directions ([US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/index.htm)). [UNESCO](/organizations/unesco/) records that the Chacoan society reached its height between about 1020 and 1110, when its sites were a focus for ceremonies, trade and political activity, linked by an elaborate system of engineered roads, many of which can still be traced ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/353/)).

By 1050, Chaco had become the ceremonial, administrative and economic centre of the San Juan Basin, its roads connecting dozens of great houses in the canyon to more than 150 great houses throughout the region ([US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/index.htm)). The Park Service stresses a point that overturns the usual picture of an ancient city: the great houses were probably not traditional farming villages occupied by large populations, but public architecture used periodically, when temporary populations came to the canyon for ceremony, commerce and trading ([US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/index.htm)). In the 1100s and 1200s construction slowed and Chaco's role as a regional centre shifted to Aztec, Mesa Verde, the Chuska Mountains and other centres, while its people migrated, reorganised and eventually became the modern Southwest Indian peoples ([US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/index.htm)). Pueblo descendants say Chaco was a special gathering place where many peoples and clans converged to share ceremonies, traditions and knowledge, and it remains central to the origins of several Navajo clans and ceremonies ([US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/index.htm)).

That living connection is why Chaco still makes news. The [National Park Service](/organizations/national-park-service/) lists 26 traditionally associated peoples for the park, from the Hopi Tribe and the Navajo Nation to the pueblos of Acoma, Taos, Zuni, Ohkay Owingeh, Cochiti, Isleta, Jemez, Laguna, Nambe, Picuris, Pojoaque, San Felipe, San Ildefonso, Sandia, Santa Ana, Santa Clara and Tesuque, plus Kewa, the Jicarilla Apache Nation, the Mescalero Apache Tribe, the Southern Ute and Ute Mountain Ute tribes, and Ysleta del Sur Pueblo of Texas ([US National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/index.htm)). And UNESCO flags a vulnerability standard profiles ignore: the property has no buffer zone, and threats to its integrity from adjacent development, energy exploration and extraction, and transportation projects have increased since inscription ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/353/)). It is that gap — a World Heritage site with no buffer against drilling at its edges — that the [All Pueblo Council of Governors](/organizations/all-pueblo-council-of-governors/), the Albuquerque-based body representing 20 Pueblo Nations in New Mexico and one in Texas, is campaigning to close with a permanent protective buffer around the Greater Chaco region (All Pueblo Council of Governors).

## A high, dry, federally-shaped economy

New Mexico's geography explains much of its economy. More than four-fifths of the state sits higher than 4,000 feet above sea level, with average elevation ranging from 5,000 to 8,000 feet in the northwest to less than 4,000 feet in the southeast, and its highest peaks — Wheeler Peak at 13,161 feet and Truchas Peak at 13,103 feet — in the Sangre de Cristo Mountains ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). The eastern third is an extension of the Great Plains including the Llano Estacado, the Rocky Mountains extend into the north-central part, the southwest is Basin and Range country, and the northwest, part of the Colorado Plateau, holds volcanic formations ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). Five river systems drain the state — the Rio Grande, which virtually bisects it from north to south, plus the Pecos, Canadian, San Juan and Gila — and its most distinctive natural features are the caverns near Carlsbad and the gypsum sand dunes at White Sands ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)).

The economy rests on services and extractive industries: oil and natural gas account for about half of the state's income from natural resources, mining extracts copper, gold, silver, iron ore, lead, zinc, manganese and molybdenum, and the state produces over four-fifths of US potash and is a leading producer of perlite ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). Government spending accounts for nearly a quarter of the state's economy, and high-technology industries contribute through nuclear weapons and energy research — a federal footprint that makes the state unusually dependent on federal programme expenditure ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). Tourism is also a leading industry, with attractions such as the Albuquerque International Balloon Fiesta drawing many visitors ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Mexico)). The implication of the Census figures is sobering: a median household income of $64,059 and a poverty rate of 16.4 per cent sit alongside a resource-rich, federally funded economy, suggesting the wealth generated by extraction and laboratories does not spread evenly ([US Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/NM/PST045224)).
