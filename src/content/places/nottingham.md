## Overview

Nottingham is a city and unitary authority in Nottinghamshire, [England](/places/england/), standing on the River Trent in the East Midlands of the [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/). Its site, a sandstone hill commanding a Trent crossing, was occupied by Anglo-Saxons in the 6th century and named Snotingaham, meaning "the ham, or village, of Snot's people," and it later became one of the five towns of the Danelaw before Norman rulers founded a second borough beside the older Saxon town ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)).

The city is known for the Robin Hood legend, for a lace industry that was once the centre of the world's, and as a UNESCO City of Literature ([UK National Commission for UNESCO](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/4864/ukncfscc11v1_1_nottingham_city_of_literature.pdf)). In 2021 its population was around 323,600 ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E06000018)).

Nottingham's modern identity rests on that layered past: a medieval market square and a castle on Standard Hill, two universities, and an economy that moved from hosiery and lace into services ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). It is also the origin point of one of Britain's most investigated railway accidents: the 06:13 Nottingham to [Norwich](/places/norwich/) service that derailed at Barrow upon Soar in 2008, an event recorded in the Barrow upon Soar rail crash profile ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar)).

## From Snotingaham to an industrial city

### Saxon, Danish and Norman layers

Nottingham began as a river-crossing settlement. Anglo-Saxons occupied the sandstone hill above the Trent in the 6th century and called it Snotingaham, and Danes peaceably occupied it in the 9th century, making it one of the five towns of the Danelaw ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). After the Norman Conquest of 1066, new rulers founded a borough that existed side by side with the older Saxon town, and the two communities were separately administered until about 1300 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). In 1449 Henry VI confirmed all previous privileges, instituted the office of sheriff, and granted that the town, excepting the castle and jail, should function as a county in itself ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). A marketplace mentioned in the charter of 1155 sat in the small valley between the Norman and Saxon settlements, and its successor, the old market square of 5.5 acres, remains a major feature of central Nottingham ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)).

### Lace, hosiery and the industrial city

Nottingham's prosperity in the Industrial Revolution was founded on textiles, and the city became an internationally important centre of lace manufacture ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). The UK National Commission for UNESCO describes it as once the centre of the world's lace industry, adding that it still creates and sells world-famous lace, including royal baby shawls made on historic hand-knitting frames ([UK National Commission for UNESCO](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/4864/ukncfscc11v1_1_nottingham_city_of_literature.pdf)). The city was also traditionally associated with the hosiery trade, and later developed pharmaceutical and tobacco industries, though a bicycle-manufacturing plant closed in the early 2000s ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). Nottingham lies at the heart of the East Midlands coalfield and is a major transport centre; its diversified economic structure has produced sustained prosperity, and the service sector now provides the major proportion of employment ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). The first railway train into [Derby](/places/derby/) ran from Nottingham on 30 May 1839, an early sign of the city's role as a regional transport hub ([Discover Derby](https://discover-derby.co.uk/railway-quarter-trails-midland-road/)).

## Castle, market square and the Robin Hood legend

### Castle and Market Square

The old Saxon town is now marked by Nottingham Castle on Standard Hill, so named because Charles I raised his standard there in 1642 at the outbreak of the English Civil Wars ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). The present castle, renovated by the corporation in 1875–78, houses a museum and art gallery ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). The UK National Commission for UNESCO lists Nottingham Castle, situated on the hill above the city, among the city's iconic landmarks, along with the Council House in Market Square with its lions, and the caves honeycombing the ground underneath the castle ([UK National Commission for UNESCO](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/4864/ukncfscc11v1_1_nottingham_city_of_literature.pdf)). The old market square is dominated on its eastern side by the Council House, opened in 1928, flanked by shops, with the central area reserved for pedestrians ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). The marketplace itself was recorded in a charter of 1155, and the market square measures about 5.5 acres ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)).

### Robin Hood and the caves

Nottingham's most famous association is a legend, not a documented resident: the link between the city and the outlaw Robin Hood is commemorated by a statue on Castle Green ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). The UNESCO factsheet calls the city most famous for the legend of Robin Hood, which has prompted numerous films, plays, books and poems ([UK National Commission for UNESCO](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/4864/ukncfscc11v1_1_nottingham_city_of_literature.pdf)). Beneath the castle hill, caves honeycomb the ground, and the sandstone that made the hill a defensible crossing point also made it easy to carve ([UK National Commission for UNESCO](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/4864/ukncfscc11v1_1_nottingham_city_of_literature.pdf)). The legend, the castle and the caves together form the core of the city's visitor identity, distinct from its industrial record in lace and hosiery ([UK National Commission for UNESCO](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/4864/ukncfscc11v1_1_nottingham_city_of_literature.pdf)). The factsheet also notes the city's thriving literary and arts scene, with a poetry network and festivals ([UK National Commission for UNESCO](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/4864/ukncfscc11v1_1_nottingham_city_of_literature.pdf)). This profile treats Robin Hood as a cultural association, not as a verified historical figure.

## Nottingham in 2021: a young, dense city

### Census 2021

Between the 2011 and 2021 censuses, Nottingham's population increased by 5.9%, from just under 305,700 to around 323,600, passing 320,000 for the first time ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E06000018)). That growth was slower than the East Midlands as a whole at 7.7% and slower than England at 6.6% ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E06000018)). The median age rose by one year, from 30 to 31, leaving Nottingham with the lowest average age in the East Midlands and a much lower average than England's 40 years ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E06000018)). In housing, Nottingham saw England's largest percentage-point fall in social renting, from 29.7% of households in 2011 to 25.5% in 2021, though it remained in the highest 10% of English local authorities for the share of households in the sector ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E06000018)). The census therefore describes a young, dense, student-influenced city growing more slowly than its region.

### Two universities and a literary city

Nottingham's higher-education history began with University College, opened in 1881 and moved to its present site west of the city in 1928 on land given by Lord Trent, otherwise Jesse Boot, founder of Boots Company, who subsequently financed much of its development ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). It was incorporated as the University of Nottingham in 1948, while Nottingham Trent University was established as a polytechnic in 1970 and gained university status in 1992 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). The city's literary ties include the poet Lord Byron and the novelist D.H. Lawrence, and visitors can see Byron's ancestral home and Lawrence's birthplace ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795); [UK National Commission for UNESCO](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/4864/ukncfscc11v1_1_nottingham_city_of_literature.pdf)). Its two theatres are the Theatre Royal, dating from 1865, and the Playhouse, opened in 1963 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/420795)). The UNESCO City of Literature designation recognises this combination of literary heritage, two universities and a thriving arts scene ([UK National Commission for UNESCO](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/4864/ukncfscc11v1_1_nottingham_city_of_literature.pdf)).

## The 2008 derailment of the Nottingham–Norwich service

On 1 February 2008, the 06:13 Nottingham to Norwich train collided with debris from a footbridge that had been knocked down by the raised body of a tipper lorry a short time earlier; the train derailed and continued for 170 metres ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar)). The train derailed but remained upright, and the [Midland Main Line](/places/midland-main-line/) remained blocked, with London services terminating at [Leicester](/places/leicester/) ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/News-Robot/Train-strikes-footbridge)). Two of the four lines were reopened by 04:00 the following day ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/News-Robot/Train-strikes-footbridge)). The full account, including the investigation's findings, is in the [Barrow upon Soar rail crash](/events/barrow-upon-soar-rail-crash/) profile, which this profile links to rather than repeating.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does the name Nottingham mean?**
Nottingham comes from Snotingaham, meaning "the ham, or village, of Snot's people," the name Anglo-Saxon settlers gave their 6th-century settlement on a sandstone hill above a Trent crossing. The name survived Danish occupation, Norman rule and industrial growth, and the city still carries the Saxon root in its modern form.

**Was Robin Hood a real person from Nottingham?**
This profile does not claim that. Nottingham is associated with the legend of Robin Hood, commemorated by a statue on Castle Green, and the legend has prompted numerous films, plays, books and poems. The association is cultural and literary, and the profile records it as a legend rather than as verified biography.

**Why is Nottingham a UNESCO City of Literature?**
The designation recognises the city's literary heritage and living scene: ties to Lord Byron and D.H. Lawrence, two universities bringing UK and international students, a flourishing poetry network, festivals and events, and landmarks such as Nottingham Castle and the Council House. The UNESCO factsheet presents literature as part of the city's present cultural life, not only its past.

**How fast is Nottingham growing?**
Between 2011 and 2021 its population grew 5.9%, from just under 305,700 to around 323,600, passing 320,000. That was slower than the East Midlands (7.7%) and England (6.6%). Its median age of 31 was the lowest in the East Midlands, reflecting a young, student-influenced population.

**What happened to the 06:13 Nottingham to Norwich train in 2008?**
On 1 February 2008 it collided with debris from a footbridge knocked down by a tipper lorry's raised body near Barrow upon Soar, derailed, and ran on for 170 metres. The driver, the guard and one passenger were taken to hospital. The Midland Main Line was blocked, with London services terminating at Leicester until lines reopened.
