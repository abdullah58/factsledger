## Overview

Ireland — Éire in Irish, the Republic of Ireland in its official description — is a parliamentary republic in north-western Europe occupying 26 of the 32 counties of the island of Ireland, with a population of about 5.5 million and its capital at Dublin ([Central Statistics Office](https://www-cloud.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-pme/populationandmigrationestimatesapril2026/keyfindings/); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)). It is a young state with an old history: incorporated into the [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/) in 1801, it won dominion status as the Irish Free State in 1922, became a sovereign country under a new constitution in 1937, and formally became a republic in 1949 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Ireland)). The six north-eastern counties that stayed in the United Kingdom as Northern Ireland remain the island's unfinished business, settled into a fragile peace by the 1998 Good Friday Agreement ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Ireland)). Since joining the [European Union](/organizations/european-union/) in 1973, Ireland has remade itself from one of Europe's poorest regions into one of its richest per head — a transformation built on foreign investment, nearly broken by a banking crash and an €85 billion bailout, and still running on the same low-tax model that built it ([European Union](https://european-union.europa.eu/principles-countries-history/eu-countries/ireland_en); [European Commission Representation in Ireland](https://ireland.representation.ec.europa.eu/news-and-events/news/circumstances-behind-irelands-bailout-late-naughties-2019-04-06_en)).

## From Free State to Republic

Ireland's independence came in instalments, not in a single moment. The Easter Rising of 1916 was followed by the Anglo-Irish War of 1919–21, and the Anglo-Irish Treaty signed on 6 December 1921 granted the 26 southern counties dominion status as the Irish Free State — while the island was partitioned, with six north-eastern counties remaining in the United Kingdom as Northern Ireland ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Ireland)). The Treaty split the independence movement itself, and the Irish Civil War of 1922–23 followed between its supporters and opponents ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Ireland)). Over the next decade and a half the new state steadily cut its remaining ties to Britain: in 1937 it adopted a new constitution that renamed the state Éire (Ireland) and made it a sovereign independent country, and in 1948 the Dáil passed the Republic of Ireland Act, which took effect in April 1949, declaring Ireland a republic and removing it from the British Commonwealth ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Ireland)). Britain recognised the new status but declared that unity with the six counties of Northern Ireland could not occur without the consent of Northern Ireland's parliament ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Ireland)).

Partition cast a long shadow. In the last decades of the 20th century the political status of Northern Ireland prompted hostilities between Catholics and Protestants in the north and deep concern in the south, and the Irish government played a pivotal role in negotiating and winning public support in 1998 for the Good Friday Agreement, under which Ireland agreed to remove from its constitution its claim to the territory of the entire island — a change enacted in 1999 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Ireland)). The settlement did not end the argument over unity, but it moved it from violence to consent: any change now depends on the agreement of the people of Northern Ireland. Ireland's government today is a unitary parliamentary republic with two legislative houses — the Dáil and the Seanad — a largely ceremonial president as head of state and a Taoiseach (prime minister) as head of government ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland); [European Union](https://european-union.europa.eu/principles-countries-history/eu-countries/ireland_en)). The president is Catherine Connolly ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)), and the Taoiseach is Micheál Martin, elected by the Dáil 95–76 on 23 January 2025 at the head of a Fianna Fáil–Fine Gael coalition, having previously held the office from 2020 to 2022 ([Al Jazeera](https://1-e8259.azureedge.net/amp/news/2025/1/23/micheal-martin-returns-as-irish-prime-minister)).

## Geography and people

Ireland occupies five-sixths of the westernmost major island of the British Isles, separated from Great Britain by the North Channel, the Irish Sea and St. George's Channel ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)). The republic is a broad, undulating central plain ringed almost completely by coastal highlands, with large areas of peat bog, many lakes and low ridges; its highest point is Carrauntoohill, at 1,041 metres in the Macgillycuddy's Reeks in the south-west ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)). Washed by abundant rain, the country's grasslands create the green-hued landscape behind the popular name Emerald Isle ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)). Dublin, on the east coast, is the capital and by far the largest city; Cork in the south-west is the second city, with Limerick and Galway in the west and Waterford, Wexford and Drogheda on the east coast among the other principal centres ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)).

The population story of the last decade is one of rapid, migration-driven growth. The Central Statistics Office estimated the usually resident population at 5,525,600 in April 2026, up 66,900 (+1.2%) in a year — with net inward migration of 48,100 accounting for 72% of that growth and natural increase for the rest ([Central Statistics Office](https://www-cloud.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-pme/populationandmigrationestimatesapril2026/keyfindings/)). The population has risen by 786,000 people (+16.6%) since 2016, the third-highest percentage increase in the European Union between 2015 and 2025, after Malta and Luxembourg ([Central Statistics Office](https://www-cloud.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-mip/measuringirelandsprogress2026/society/)). Dublin concentrates that growth: 1,597,700 people, or 28.9% of the state, lived in Dublin in April 2026, up from a 27.6% share in 2011 ([Central Statistics Office](https://www-cloud.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-pme/populationandmigrationestimatesapril2026/keyfindings/)). At the same time the population is ageing — 891,100 people, or 16.1%, were aged 65 and over in April 2026, up from 14.4% in 2020, while the number of children under 15 fell below one million for the first time since 2014 ([Central Statistics Office](https://www-cloud.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-pme/populationandmigrationestimatesapril2026/keyfindings/)).

## The FDI economy: boom, bailout, and recovery

Ireland's modern economy is a deliberate construction, and its foundation is tax. The 12.5% corporation tax rate on trading income has been a cornerstone of the country's economic model since the early 2000s, and it is one of the few policies that unites all shades of Irish political opinion ([ACCA AB Magazine](https://abmagazine.accaglobal.com/global/articles/2021/nov/business/ireland-s-seismic-tax-shift.html)). The rate, an English-speaking workforce, and access to the EU single market drew in foreign multinationals at a scale unmatched in Europe: some 14,500 foreign multinationals are based in Ireland, accounting for 32% of all jobs, and in 2020 corporation tax supplied 21% of the state's total tax revenue, with just ten multinationals accounting for over half of it ([ACCA AB Magazine](https://abmagazine.accaglobal.com/global/articles/2021/nov/business/ireland-s-seismic-tax-shift.html)). That concentration is the model's known vulnerability — and it is why Ireland's 2021 agreement to apply a 15% minimum rate to large groups (those with turnover above €750 million) mattered so much, even though the 12.5% rate survives for the vast majority of companies ([ACCA AB Magazine](https://abmagazine.accaglobal.com/global/articles/2021/nov/business/ireland-s-seismic-tax-shift.html)).

The foreign-investment pipeline is still running hot. In 2024 Ireland secured 234 investment wins projected to create 13,500 jobs, including 69 from first-time investors, and companies backed by the state agency IDA Ireland employed 302,566 people — about 11% of national employment, above 300,000 for a third consecutive year — across more than 1,800 companies ([IDA Ireland](https://www.idaireland.fr/latest-news/press-release/record-r-d,-strong-capital-investment,-and-high-number-of-new-investors)). A record €1.9 billion of research and development investment was approved across 64 projects in the same year, and 59% of investments landed outside Dublin ([IDA Ireland](https://www.idaireland.fr/latest-news/press-release/record-r-d,-strong-capital-investment,-and-high-number-of-new-investors)). Ireland ranked fourth of 67 economies in the 2024 IMD World Competitiveness Ranking, and its GDP per capita of €99,000 is the second-highest in the EU, well above the EU average of €41,600 ([IDA Ireland](https://www.idaireland.fr/latest-news/press-release/record-r-d,-strong-capital-investment,-and-high-number-of-new-investors); [European Union](https://european-union.europa.eu/principles-countries-history/eu-countries/ireland_en)). The same model that draws the giants also draws smaller ones: [ClickUp](/organizations/clickup/) chose Ireland for its European headquarters and a 600-job expansion, funded by its October 2021 Series C, when 40% of its business was already outside the [United States](/places/united-states/).

The boom that built this economy nearly destroyed it. During the late-1990s and 2000s "Celtic Tiger" years, Irish banks lent hugely to property developers until their balance sheets had grown to four times Irish GDP; when the global crisis hit in 2008, the state compounded the problem with a blanket guarantee of the banks ([European Commission Representation in Ireland](https://ireland.representation.ec.europa.eu/news-and-events/news/circumstances-behind-irelands-bailout-late-naughties-2019-04-06_en)). In November 2010 the government formally requested international financial assistance, and an €85 billion EU–IMF package was agreed the following month, primarily to bail out the banks ([European Commission Representation in Ireland](https://ireland.representation.ec.europa.eu/news-and-events/news/circumstances-behind-irelands-bailout-late-naughties-2019-04-06_en)). Ireland exited the three-year programme on 15 December 2013 and its economy grew faster than the EU average in the years that followed, with unemployment falling from a peak of around 16% in 2012 to 5.4% by March 2019 ([European Commission Representation in Ireland](https://ireland.representation.ec.europa.eu/news-and-events/news/circumstances-behind-irelands-bailout-late-naughties-2019-04-06_en)). High technology, tourism and other service industries are now pivotal to the economy, while agriculture employs only a small percentage of the workforce ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)).

## Culture, language, and the diaspora

Ireland's culture retains characteristics that its history explains. Both Irish and English are official languages, but English is spoken by most of the population; Irish, the national language, declined sharply in the 19th century and is now a compulsory school subject, with organised efforts to revive it ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)). The shamrock is the national emblem, associated with St. Patrick — credited with Christianising the country in the 5th century — who is said to have used it to explain the Trinity, and the flag's green, white and orange stripes symbolise Catholics, Protestants and the peace between them ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)). Traditional music, often performed in pubs with the fiddle, tin whistle and uilleann pipes, and St. Patrick's Day on 17 March remain the most visible expressions of that heritage ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)). The literary tradition runs through William Butler Yeats, described as perhaps Ireland's best-known poet, whose work evokes an idealised countryside central to the memories of the country's millions of expatriates ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)).

The diaspora is part of the story, not a footnote to it. Some 40 million Americans trace their ancestry to Ireland, the result of mass migration especially during the cycle of famine in the 19th century, when as many as two million people emigrated and about one million died from starvation or famine-related disease ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/summary/Ireland)). Every year members of that diaspora visit their ancestral homeland — a reversal of the emigration that defined Ireland for a century and a half, now that the flow of people runs the other way, into the country rather than out of it ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ireland)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Ireland the same as Northern Ireland?**
No. Ireland (the Republic of Ireland) is a sovereign state of 26 counties. Northern Ireland is the six north-eastern counties of the island that remain part of the United Kingdom. The island of Ireland is the geographic whole; the state of Ireland is five-sixths of it.

**When did Ireland become a republic?**
The Republic of Ireland Act was passed in 1948 and took effect in April 1949, declaring Ireland a republic and removing it from the British Commonwealth. The state had already become sovereign under its 1937 constitution, and a self-governing dominion as the Irish Free State in 1922.

**When did Ireland join the EU?**
Ireland joined the European Economic Community, the forerunner of the European Union, on 1 January 1973, and has been a member of the euro area since 1 January 1999. It has negotiated an opt-out from the Schengen area.

**Why do so many tech companies have headquarters in Ireland?**
The combination is a 12.5% corporation tax rate on trading income, an English-speaking and highly educated workforce, and access to the EU single market. IDA Ireland's client companies employ over 300,000 people, about 11% of national employment — but just ten multinationals supply over half of all corporation tax, so the model is concentrated.

**What is Ireland's population?**
The Central Statistics Office estimated it at 5,525,600 in April 2026, up 66,900 in a year, with net migration driving 72% of the growth. Dublin alone holds 1,597,700 people, 28.9% of the state.
