## Overview

Warsaw is the capital and largest city of [Poland](/places/poland/), a city on the Vistula River in east-central Poland that was deliberately razed in 1944 and then rebuilt by its returning inhabitants, its Old Town so faithfully reconstructed that [UNESCO](/organizations/unesco/) listed it as a World Heritage site in 1980 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw); [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/30)). It is Poland's seat of government and its centre of social, political, economic, scientific and cultural life ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw)). Warsaw lies on the Vistula, about 240 miles southeast of Gdańsk, divided into right- and left-bank portions by the river, with a population of 1,860,281 in 2021 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw)). Britannica calls it a phoenix city: damaged in the Swedish and Prussian occupation of 1655–56 and assaulted in 1794, it was razed in 1944 and rebuilt after the war, resuming its role as the capital ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw)). Its motto is "Contemnit procellas" — "It defies the storms" — and its emblem is a mermaid with sword and shield ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw)). UNESCO records that 85% of the historic city was destroyed in 1944, so much of the Old Town visitors see today is a deliberate post-war reconstruction — the rebuilding itself is what UNESCO honoured ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/30)). The city's story reaches beyond Poland: the [Copernicus Foundation](/organizations/copernicus-foundation/) of [Chicago](/places/chicago/) raised $150,000 to replicate a Warsaw original for its [Nicolaus Copernicus monument](/places/nicolaus-copernicus-monument/), dedicated in 1973.

## Destruction and reconstruction

In 1944, after the Warsaw Uprising failed, Warsaw was deliberately annihilated as repression of Polish resistance to the Nazi German occupation, with the intention of obliterating the centuries-old tradition of Polish statehood ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/30)). UNESCO records that 85% of the historic city was destroyed, and the rebuilding that followed was the result of the determination of the inhabitants and the support of the whole nation ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/30)). The reconstruction of the Old Town was devised at the Warsaw Reconstruction Office in the years 1945–1951, using surviving structures from the 14th to 18th centuries and the late-medieval network of streets and squares, aiming to recreate the city's late-18th-century appearance ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/30)). The project recreated the urban plan, the Old Town Market, the townhouses, the circuit of the city walls, the Royal Castle and important religious buildings ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/30)). Rebuilding continued until the mid-1960s and was completed with the Royal Castle, opened to visitors in 1984 ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/30)).

## The Warsaw Uprising

On 1 August 1944 the Polish Home Army, a non-Communist underground resistance movement, began the Warsaw Uprising to liberate the city from German occupation and reclaim Polish independence ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-warsaw-polish-uprising)). About 45,000 Home Army members under General Antoni Chruściel, known as "Monter", took part, supported by 2,500 soldiers from other resistance movements, yet only a quarter of the partisans had weapons, facing 25,000 German soldiers equipped with artillery, tanks and air forces ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-warsaw-polish-uprising)). The revolt, planned to last a few days, lasted 63 days, and it was the single largest military effort undertaken by resistance forces against German occupation in the Second World War ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-warsaw-polish-uprising)). By the time it ended on 2 October 1944, the number of victims exceeded 180,000 people, and more than 11,000 Home Army soldiers were taken prisoner ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-warsaw-polish-uprising)). In mid-September the Red Army took Praga, on the east bank of the Vistula, but did not cross the river to intervene, and Soviet troops took the devastated city on 17 January 1945 ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/the-warsaw-polish-uprising)).

## Government, economy and culture

As the capital of the Republic of Poland, Warsaw houses all the central institutions of the national government, including the residence of the Polish president, the Presidential Palace, while the Sejm, Poland's national legislature, sits near the crossroads of Nowy Świat and Aleje Jerozolimskie ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw)). The city is run by an elected City Council headed by a city president ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw)). Since the 1990s, after the collapse of communism, Warsaw's economy has changed rapidly, with services, notably banking and insurance, growing in importance, and the Warsaw Stock Exchange was reopened in 1991 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw)). Warsaw is the hub of the main rail, road and air routes of importance to Eastern Europe, and its international gateway is Warsaw Frédéric Chopin Airport in Okęcie, south of the centre ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw)). The city is also a cultural centre, home to the National Museum, the Zachęta National Gallery of Art and the POLIN Museum of the History of Polish Jews, and to the National Philharmonic Orchestra and the National Opera ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Warsaw)).

## FAQ

**Why is Warsaw called a phoenix city?**
Britannica calls Warsaw a phoenix because it has repeatedly risen from the ashes of war: it was damaged in the Swedish and Prussian occupation of 1655–56, assaulted in 1794, and razed in 1944 after the Warsaw Uprising failed. Each time its people returned and rebuilt it, restoring streets, buildings and churches to their original forms.

**What happened in the Warsaw Uprising?**
On 1 August 1944 the Polish Home Army rose against the German occupation to liberate the city and reclaim Polish independence. The fighting lasted 63 days, ending on 2 October 1944, with more than 180,000 victims and over 11,000 Home Army soldiers taken prisoner, after which German forces systematically destroyed most of the city.

**Why is Warsaw's Old Town a UNESCO World Heritage site?**
UNESCO listed the Historic Centre of Warsaw in 1980, not because the Old Town survived the war, but because it was comprehensively rebuilt after 85% of the historic city was destroyed in 1944. The reconstruction, devised by the Warsaw Reconstruction Office in 1945–1951, recreated the urban plan, market square, townhouses, walls and Royal Castle.

**What is Warsaw's connection to Copernicus and Chicago?**
A statue of Nicolaus Copernicus stands on Krakowskie Przedmieście in Warsaw. The Copernicus Foundation of Chicago raised $150,000 to replicate that Warsaw original for a monument dedicated in Chicago in 1973, a direct link between the Polish capital and the city's Polish-American community.
