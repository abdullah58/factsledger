## Overview

Jefferson Park is Community Area 11 of [Chicago](/places/chicago/), a Northwest Side neighborhood of 26,649 people (2024) built around one of the city's busiest transit hubs and home to the [Copernicus Foundation](/organizations/copernicus-foundation/)'s [Copernicus Center](/organizations/copernicus-center/). Named for [Thomas Jefferson](/people/thomas-jefferson/) and nicknamed the "Gateway to Chicago," it was an independent township until Chicago annexed it in 1889, and it remains a predominantly middle-class, heavily Polish-American community of bungalows and two-flats.

The community area covers about 2.3 square miles on the city's far Northwest Side, bordered by Norwood Park, Forest Glen, and Portage Park, with the suburb of Harwood Heights to the west ([Are You Chi](https://areyouchi.com/neighborhoods/community-areas/jefferson-park/)). It contains the Jefferson Park neighborhood around the transit center and Gladstone Park to the northwest, whose Dutch Colonial homes earned it the nickname "Little Rotterdam" ([Are You Chi](https://areyouchi.com/neighborhoods/community-areas/jefferson-park/)). This profile is about Chicago's Jefferson Park — not the Jefferson Park neighborhood of Los Angeles or the one in Portland, Oregon, which share only the name.

## From tavern crossroads to Chicago township

Settlement began in the early 1830s, when John Kinzie Clark built a cabin and Elijah Wentworth staked a claim near today's Jefferson Park Metra station, running a tavern and inn ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)). After 1849, produce moved faster on two plank roads — the North West Plank Road, now Milwaukee Avenue, and the Lower Road, now Elston Avenue ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)). In 1850 the state formed Jefferson Township, named for President Thomas Jefferson; five years later residents platted a village near Milwaukee Avenue and Higgins Road, and by 1855 the town counted about 50 buildings ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)). The railroad built a depot at the town's center and drew Polish and German immigrants; by 1884 about 500 people lived in the township ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)). The town incorporated in 1872 and Chicago annexed it in 1889 ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)). Street railways and a 1920s building boom filled the area with graystones, A-frames, and bungalows, and the population reached 20,532 by 1930 ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)).

## The Gateway to Chicago: a century of transit

The nickname is literal: Jefferson Park has been a transport crossroads since the plank-road era, and the Kennedy Expressway, completed in 1959, slices diagonally through it ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)). In 1970 the CTA built a terminal joining bus routes, a commuter rail station, and the "L" ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)); the Blue Line station at 4917 N. Milwaukee Avenue opened on February 1, 1970, as the terminus of the $50 million Kennedy Extension, its white steel-and-glass station designed by Skidmore, Owings & Merrill under Myron Goldsmith ([Chicago-'L'.org](https://www.chicago-l.org/stations/jefferson_pk.html)). The line ran on to River Road in 1983 and to O'Hare in 1984 ([Chicago-'L'.org](https://www.chicago-l.org/stations/jefferson_pk.html)). Greyhound coaches once stopped here too, moving to Cumberland when the airport extension opened ([Chicago-'L'.org](https://www.chicago-l.org/stations/jefferson_pk.html)). The hub kept evolving: an elevator and renovation in 2000–01, a $4 million upgrade and a donated Jefferson statue in the plaza in 2005, and a $25 million renovation begun in October 2018 ([Chicago-'L'.org](https://www.chicago-l.org/stations/jefferson_pk.html)). In the late 1990s the Metra station served 10,000 commuters a day ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)).

## A Polish-American anchor in a diversifying community

Nearly half of Jefferson Park's 23,649 residents were of Polish descent in 1990, and the community's cultural anchor is the Copernicus Center in the former Gateway Theatre ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)). The 1930 movie palace, designed by Mason Rapp of Rapp & Rapp, is Chicago's last atmospheric theater, seats 1,800, and is owned by the Copernicus Foundation ([Gateway Theater](https://gatewaytheater.org/)). The Foundation has served as the city's Polish cultural center for over thirty years, hosting the [Taste of Polonia](/events/taste-of-polonia-festival/) festival ([National Park Service](https://archive.org/stream/NationalRegisterNominationsForChicago/JeffersonPkNrNom_djvu.txt)). The other anchor is Thomas Jefferson Memorial Park, whose 1930 Hatzfeld fieldhouse and Esdohr House appear in a 2005 National Register nomination ([National Park Service](https://archive.org/stream/NationalRegisterNominationsForChicago/JeffersonPkNrNom_djvu.txt); [Chicago Park District](https://www.chicagoparkdistrict.com/parks-facilities/jefferson-thomas-memorial-park?page=4)). The population has since diversified: the White non-Hispanic share fell from 81.6% in 2000 to 56.1% in 2020–24, while Hispanic and Asian shares rose to 26.9% and 11.9% ([CMAP](https://cmap.illinois.gov/download/6116/?tmstv=1791575057)).

## FAQ

**Where is Jefferson Park in Chicago?**
Jefferson Park is Community Area 11 on Chicago's far Northwest Side, about 10 miles northwest of the Loop, in the [United States](/places/united-states/) ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)). It borders Norwood Park, Forest Glen, and Portage Park, with Harwood Heights to the west, and its edges follow Foster, Austin, Cicero, Montrose, Nagle, and Bryn Mawr avenues ([Are You Chi](https://areyouchi.com/neighborhoods/community-areas/jefferson-park/)).

**Why is Jefferson Park called the Gateway to Chicago?**
Because of its long history as a transportation hub, now centered on the Jefferson Park Transit Center, where the CTA Blue Line, Metra's Union Pacific Northwest Line, and CTA buses meet ([Encyclopedia of Chicago](http://encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/667.html)). The Blue Line station opened there in 1970, and the Kennedy Expressway runs diagonally through the area ([Chicago-'L'.org](https://www.chicago-l.org/stations/jefferson_pk.html)).

**What is the Copernicus Center?**
The Copernicus Center is the Polish cultural center of Chicago, housed in the former Gateway Theatre, a 1930 movie palace designed by Mason Rapp of Rapp & Rapp ([Gateway Theater](https://gatewaytheater.org/)). Owned and operated by the Copernicus Foundation, it seats up to 1,800 and hosts concerts, performances, classes, and the annual Taste of Polonia festival ([Gateway Theater](https://gatewaytheater.org/); [National Park Service](https://archive.org/stream/NationalRegisterNominationsForChicago/JeffersonPkNrNom_djvu.txt)).

**How diverse is Jefferson Park today?**
The 2020–2024 American Community Survey counted 26,649 residents: 56.1% White (non-Hispanic), 26.9% Hispanic, 11.9% Asian, and 2.8% Black, with 26.6% foreign-born ([CMAP](https://cmap.illinois.gov/download/6116/?tmstv=1791575057)). The median household income was $96,870, well above the city figure of $77,902, and the median age was 42.3 years against 35.8 citywide ([CMAP](https://cmap.illinois.gov/download/6116/?tmstv=1791575057)).

**Is this the same Jefferson Park as in Los Angeles?**
No. This profile covers Chicago's Jefferson Park, Community Area 11 on the Northwest Side — not the Jefferson Park neighborhood of [Los Angeles](/places/los-angeles/), California, nor the one in Portland, Oregon. The three places share only a name, all honoring Thomas Jefferson, and have separate histories, boundaries, and communities.
