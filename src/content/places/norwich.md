## Overview

Norwich is a cathedral city and district in Norfolk, [England](/places/england/), and the county town of Norfolk, standing on the River Wensum above its confluence with the River Yare, about 100 miles (160 kilometres) northeast of [London](/places/london/) in the [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/).

Its site seems not to have been occupied until Saxon times, when the village of Northwic was founded on a gravel terrace above the Wensum, and by 1004, when it was sacked by the Danes, it had become an important market centre ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). Norwich grew rich on wool, kept 30 medieval churches, and in 2012 became England's first UNESCO City of Literature ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England); [National Centre for Writing](https://nationalcentreforwriting.org.uk/norwich-unesco-city-of-literature/why-norwich/)). In 2021 its population was around 143,900 ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E07000148/)).

Norwich is also the destination named in one of Britain's most investigated railway accidents: the 06:13 [Nottingham](/places/nottingham/) to Norwich service that derailed at [Barrow upon Soar](/places/barrow-upon-soar/) in 2008, an event recorded in the Barrow upon Soar rail crash profile ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar)).

## From Northwic to a prosperous medieval city

### Saxon, Danish and Norman layers

Norwich's story starts with a gravel terrace above the Wensum. The village of Northwic was founded there in Saxon times, and by 1004 it was already an important market centre when the Danes sacked it ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). Shortly after the Norman Conquest of 1066, a cathedral church and a Benedictine monastery were founded near the river, anchoring the medieval city ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). The first charter dates from 1158, giving the town formal self-government in the century when its market, churches and walls took shape ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). Norwich's medieval prosperity rested on wool: the city remained one of England's most prosperous provincial towns until the late 18th century ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). The cathedral's Norman apse and nave survive, and its 15th-century stone spire rises to 315 feet, one of the tallest in England, above cloisters that are the largest in England ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)).

### Wool, weavers and strangers

Until the late 18th century Norwich was one of the most prosperous of English provincial towns, challenged only by Bristol and York, and its prosperity rested on wool ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). Edward III aided the trade by inducing Flemish weavers to settle in Norwich in 1336, and a second influx of immigrants, mainly from the Low Countries, arrived during the reign of Elizabeth I ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). In 1579 nearly a third of the town's population of 16,000 were immigrants, a measure of how much the city's wealth depended on strangers bringing skills ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). The prosperity of the medieval town is reflected in the number of churches from the period, 30 of which still exist, and in the flint Guildhall built in the 15th century overlooking the market square ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). During the English Civil Wars of 1642–51 the population stood predominantly behind the Parliamentary cause, and the town saw little strife ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)).

## Cathedral, castle and the medieval streetscape

### Cathedral and castle

Norwich's two great Norman monuments face each other across the medieval city. The cathedral, founded shortly after the Conquest, pairs a Norman apse and nave with Perpendicular Gothic additions, including the 15th-century spire and the largest cloisters in England ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). The Norman castle, built in the 12th century east of the town's principal market, has served since 1894 as the city's main museum and art gallery, with an archaeological collection and paintings of the Norwich school, which flourished in the 19th century ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). Together they compress nine centuries into one walkable centre: monastery and fortress, then museum, then the civic buildings around the market ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). The castle's conversion to a museum in 1894 came at the moment when Victorian Norwich was reimagining its medieval past as public heritage rather than as fortification or prison ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)).

### Churches, Guildhall and market

The medieval town's wealth is still legible in its buildings. Thirty churches from the medieval period still exist, a survival unmatched among English provincial cities, and the 15th-century flint Guildhall overlooks the market square ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). From the 18th century Norwich declined in relation to the new industrial centres of the north, yet it developed as one of England's largest centres of footwear manufacturing, and although some of that manufacturing has remained, much of the focus has shifted toward footwear wholesaling ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). Engineering, printing and food processing, notably mustard, are also important, but services including tourism now constitute much of the local economic activity ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). Norwich remains one of England's major agricultural and livestock markets and serves as a shopping and entertainment centre for an extensive area ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)). Its football club, Norwich City, plays at Carrow Road stadium ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)).

## A literary city in 2021

### UNESCO City of Literature

Norwich has been a literary city for over 900 years, a place where the power of words has changed lives, promoted parliamentary democracy, fomented revolution, fought for the abolition of slavery and transformed literature ([National Centre for Writing](https://nationalcentreforwriting.org.uk/norwich-unesco-city-of-literature/why-norwich/)). In 2012 it consolidated that standing by becoming England's first UNESCO City of Literature, joining an international network that includes Edinburgh, Melbourne, Iowa City, Dublin, Reykjavik, Kraków, Nottingham, Dunedin, Granada, Manchester and Prague ([National Centre for Writing](https://nationalcentreforwriting.org.uk/norwich-unesco-city-of-literature/why-norwich/)). It is the birthplace of world-renowned writers and thinkers including Anna Sewell, Harriet Martineau and Thomas Browne ([National Centre for Writing](https://nationalcentreforwriting.org.uk/norwich-unesco-city-of-literature/why-norwich/)). The city hosts the oldest city arts festival in the country, the Norfolk & Norwich Festival, and a year-round programme of literary and translation events ([National Centre for Writing](https://nationalcentreforwriting.org.uk/norwich-unesco-city-of-literature/why-norwich/)). The University of East Anglia, founded in 1964, includes the Centre of East Anglian Studies at Earlham Hall, long associated with the Gurney family ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Norwich-England)).

### Census 2021

Between the 2011 and 2021 censuses, Norwich's population increased by 8.6%, from just over 132,500 to around 143,900, passing 140,000 ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E07000148/)). That growth was slightly faster than the East of England's 8.3% and faster than England's 6.6% ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E07000148/)). The median age remained 34 years, the joint second-lowest in the East of England and well below England's 40 ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E07000148/)). Norwich saw the East of England's joint largest rise in the share of adults who had never married or been in a civil partnership, from 46.9% to 52.4% ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E07000148/)). In housing, it saw the region's largest percentage-point fall in social renting, from 32.7% to 30.3%, yet remained in the highest 3% of English local authorities for the share of households in the sector ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/censusareachanges/E07000148/)).

## The 2008 derailment of the Nottingham–Norwich service

On 1 February 2008, the 06:13 Nottingham to Norwich train collided with debris from a footbridge that had been knocked down by the raised body of a tipper lorry a short time earlier; the train derailed and continued for 170 metres ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar)). The train derailed but remained upright, and the [Midland Main Line](/places/midland-main-line/) remained blocked, with London services terminating at Leicester ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/News-Robot/Train-strikes-footbridge)). Two of the four lines were reopened by 04:00 the following day ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/News-Robot/Train-strikes-footbridge)). The full account, including the investigation's findings, is in the [Barrow upon Soar rail crash](/events/barrow-upon-soar-rail-crash/) profile, which this profile links to rather than repeating.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does the name Norwich mean?**
Norwich began as Northwic, a Saxon village on a gravel terrace above the River Wensum. The city grew from that riverside market into a Norman cathedral city, a medieval wool centre and, in Britannica's phrase, the traditional regional capital of East Anglia. The Saxon root survives in the modern name.

**Why does Norwich have so many medieval churches?**
Its medieval prosperity, based on the wool trade and boosted by Flemish weavers from 1336 and Low Countries immigrants under Elizabeth I, funded church building across the city. Thirty churches from the medieval period still exist, alongside the flint Guildhall and the Norman cathedral and castle, giving Norwich an unusually complete medieval streetscape.

**Why is Norwich a UNESCO City of Literature?**
Norwich has been a literary city for over 900 years, and in 2012 it became England's first UNESCO City of Literature. It is the birthplace of Anna Sewell, Harriet Martineau and Thomas Browne, home to the University of East Anglia's Creative Writing MA and the British Centre for Literary Translation, and host of the country's oldest city arts festival.

**How fast is Norwich growing?**
Between 2011 and 2021 its population grew 8.6%, from just over 132,500 to around 143,900, slightly faster than the East of England (8.3%) and England (6.6%). Its median age stayed at 34, the joint second-lowest in the East of England, reflecting a young, university-influenced population.

**What happened to the 06:13 Nottingham to Norwich train in 2008?**
On 1 February 2008 it collided with debris from a footbridge knocked down by a tipper lorry's raised body near Barrow upon Soar, derailed, and ran on for 170 metres. The driver, the guard and one passenger were taken to hospital. The Midland Main Line was blocked, with London services terminating at Leicester until lines reopened.
