## Overview

The West Bank is a territory of roughly 3 million people — about 500,000 Jews and 2.5 million Palestinians — lying west of the Jordan River and east of Israel, about 9 miles wide at its narrowest point ([BICOM](https://www.bicom.org.uk/the-west-bank-and-settlements/)). Internationally it is called the West Bank; in Israel it is often called Judea and Samaria, and described as the areas beyond the Green Line ([BICOM](https://www.bicom.org.uk/the-west-bank-and-settlements/)). Its modern history is a sequence of three ruptures: Jordan annexed it in 1950, Israel took control of it in 1967, and Jordan formally walked away from it in 1988. The map drawn by the 1995 Oslo II accords — Areas A, B, and C — still governs daily life there.

## Nineteen years of Jordan: annexation to disengagement

The chapter most summaries skip is the Jordanian one. On 24 April 1950, Jordan formally annexed the West Bank and East Jerusalem, declaring a union of the two banks with 30 parliamentary representatives from each side and granting Jordanian citizenship to the territory's residents ([Center for Israel Education](https://israeled.org/timeline/jordan-annexes-the-west-bank-and-east-jerusalem/)). The Arab League declared the annexation illegal, and only three countries — Britain, Iraq, and Pakistan — ever recognized it ([Center for Israel Education](https://israeled.org/timeline/jordan-annexes-the-west-bank-and-east-jerusalem/)).

Jordan's rule ended in two stages. First came the June 1967 war, in which Israel took control of the West Bank including East Jerusalem; settlement building began that September under a Labour government ([The Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/region/west-bank-main-dates-since-1967)). Then, two decades later, Jordan itself cut the knot. In July 1988, King Hussein dismantled the relationship in three moves: on 28 July he ended a $1.3 billion West Bank development program, on 30 July he dissolved Parliament, and on 31 July he announced the severing of all of Jordan's administrative and legal ties to the territory — retaining only the Hashemite guardianship of Muslim and Christian holy sites in Jerusalem ([kinghussein.gov.jo](http://kinghussein.gov.jo/his_periods9.html)). With that, the West Bank's Jordanian chapter closed, and the question of who would speak for its Palestinian residents passed to the PLO.

## Oslo's map: Areas A, B, and C

The first Palestinian intifada (1987–1993) led to the Oslo accords, which granted the Palestinians limited self-government ([The Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/region/west-bank-main-dates-since-1967)). The Palestinian Authority was established in 1994 with its seat in Ramallah ([The Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/region/west-bank-main-dates-since-1967)). Then Oslo II, in 1995, drew the map that still applies: Area A — including Ramallah, Jenin, Nablus, Jericho, Bethlehem, and part of Hebron — under PA control; Area B under mixed Palestinian-Israeli control; and Area C, about 60% of the territory, under Israeli control ([The Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/region/west-bank-main-dates-since-1967)).

The second intifada (2000–2005) hardened that map physically: Israel began building a separation barrier in 2002 ([The Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/region/west-bank-main-dates-since-1967)). The result is a patchwork in which a Palestinian resident of Ramallah (Area A) and a Palestinian farmer a few miles away (Area C) live under substantially different authorities.

## 2020–2026: annexation plans and escalation

The most recent chapter has been one of accelerating Israeli government moves and escalating violence. In 2020, a Trump administration plan to annex parts of the West Bank was suspended in exchange for Israel's normalization of relations with the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain ([The Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/region/west-bank-main-dates-since-1967)). After [Hamas](/organizations/hamas/) launched the October 7, 2023 attacks on Israel, hundreds of Hamas operations were launched from the West Bank, killing more than 550 Israelis, according to BICOM ([BICOM](https://www.bicom.org.uk/the-west-bank-and-settlements/)).

In 2025, the [Government of Israel](/organizations/government-of-israel/) intensified its West Bank campaign: it launched Operation Iron Wall on 21 January, approved 22 new settlements on 29 May and a 3,400-home settlement project on 20 August, and more than 70 members of Israel's parliament backed a motion to apply Israeli sovereignty to the territory ([The Jordan Times](https://jordantimes.com/news/region/west-bank-main-dates-since-1967)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not take a position on the legal status of the territory or the settlements; it reports what its sources report and attributes contested descriptions. Population figures (~500,000 Jews, 2.5 million Palestinians) come from a single source and are approximate. The casualty and operations figures for the post–October 7 period are those of the cited sources. The 2025 settlement approvals, Operation Iron Wall, and the parliamentary sovereignty motion are reported from the Jordan Times chronology, and developments after that chronology are not covered here. Nothing in this profile predicts the outcome of annexation debates or future negotiations over the territory.
