## Overview

Chaco Canyon is a canyon in northwestern [New Mexico](/places/new-mexico/) whose great houses were the centre of an Ancestral Puebloan civilisation that flourished from about AD 850 to 1250, and it is preserved today as Chaco Culture National Historical Park, a [UNESCO](/organizations/unesco/) World Heritage site since 1987 whose wider landscape — the Greater Chaco region — is the subject of a 2025–2026 campaign for permanent protection from oil and gas leasing ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chaco-Culture-National-Historical-Park); [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/353/); [Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)).

Three names describe one subject, and the distinctions matter. "Chaco Canyon" is the canyon itself, the geographic and cultural heart of the place ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chaco-Culture-National-Historical-Park)). "Chaco Culture National Historical Park" is the federally managed park unit preserving the canyon's core sites, established in 1907 as Chaco Canyon National Monument and redesignated and renamed in 1980 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chaco-Culture-National-Historical-Park)). "Greater Chaco" is the wider landscape beyond the park boundary — outlier great houses, roads, shrines and sacred places across the San Juan Basin — and it is the landscape at stake in the current protection fight ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). The UNESCO listing, titled "Chaco Culture," sits between the two: it covers the park plus Aztec Ruins and five additional protected archaeological areas, but UNESCO states plainly that the property has no buffer zone ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/353/)).

Chaco Culture National Historical Park lies in a long, shallow canyon atop the Colorado Plateau, centrally located within the San Juan Basin of northwestern New Mexico ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). Britannica places it some 45 miles south of Bloomfield and about 55 miles northeast of Gallup, occupying 53 square miles of canyon dissected by the Chaco and Gallo washes ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chaco-Culture-National-Historical-Park)). The park unit is located in Nageezi, New Mexico ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). People have occupied this high desert landscape, with its long winters, short growing seasons and limited rainfall, for over 10,000 years ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)).

## A ceremonial, commercial and political centre built in stone

About 1,000 years ago, a remarkable urban centre of politics, economy, ceremony and culture evolved in this desert landscape, built by the ancestral Pueblo people ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). This pre-Columbian cultural complex dominated and influenced much of the southwestern [United States](/places/united-states/) from the mid-9th to the mid-13th centuries ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). UNESCO dates the society's height to between about 1020 and 1110, when its sites were a focus for ceremonies, trade and political activity ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/353/)). The site was an administrative, economic and ceremonial centre of the Ancestral Pueblo people from AD 850 to 1250, and the Ancestral Pueblos were ancestors of the modern Pueblo Indians ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chaco-Culture-National-Historical-Park)).

The building achievement is the reason for the World Heritage listing. From 850 AD to approximately 1250 AD, ancestral Puebloans constructed buildings and managed an extensive cultural system throughout the area, and at its peak a few thousand people may have lived in Chaco Canyon, with thousands more drawn to ceremonial gatherings and trading events ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). The ancestral Puebloans created specific styles of masonry, unique for their time, that allowed them to build multi-storied stone structures on mesa tops and canyon floors, quarrying sandstone blocks and hauling timber from great distances ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). The great houses and other village clusters feature this masonry, and the high concentration of these buildings suggests the area served as a regional religious, administrative, commercial and trading centre ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)).

Pueblo Bonito was the centre of the Chacoan world ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). The enormous, well-planned, D-shaped building took decades to build and at its peak stood four to five stories tall, covered 3 acres, and contained over 600 rooms, 40 kivas and two plazas, serving as the focus of ceremonial functions, administration, trading, storage, hospitality, communications, astronomy and burial of the honored dead ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). Britannica gives a different count — about 800 rooms and 39 kivas for Pueblo Bonito, built mainly in the 10th century — and both figures are reported here rather than reconciled ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chaco-Culture-National-Historical-Park)). Construction began in the mid to late 800s on Pueblo Bonito, Una Vida and Penasco Blanco, followed later by Hungo Pavi, Chetro Ketl, Pueblo Alto, Casa Chiquita, Kin Kletso, Pueblo del Arroyo, Tsin Kletsin, Casa Rinconada and Wijiji ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)).

The system extended far beyond the canyon walls. Many buildings were oriented to solar, lunar and cardinal directions, and astronomical markers, communication features, catch basins, canals, check dams and ditches surrounded clusters of buildings connected by over 400 miles of prehistoric roadways, some as much as 30 feet wide, linking dozens of Chaco Canyon's great houses to over 150 great houses in the region ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). Britannica likewise records some 400 miles of engineered roads radiating outward like spokes on a wheel, once connecting dozens of settlements ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chaco-Culture-National-Historical-Park)). The road system carried trade: Chacoans traded pottery, turquoise, seashells, copper bells, macaws and parrots among themselves and with groups as far south as Mexico, and turquoise jewelry, obsidian blades and macaw feathers from Mesoamerica suggest Chaco lay along an important trade route extending far southward ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chaco-Culture-National-Historical-Park)). The [National Park Service](/organizations/national-park-service/) adds that chemical testing on pot sherds from cylinder jars indicates the Puebloans even imported chocolate from Mexico ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/worldheritage.htm)).

The UNESCO inscription itself reflects that reach: it includes sites outside the park, some owned by the Bureau of Land Management, the State of New Mexico and the Navajo Nation, including Aztec Ruins, Pierre's site, Halfway House, Twin Angels, Salmon Ruins, Kin Nizhoni and Casamero ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/worldheritage.htm)). The United States [Department of the Interior](/organizations/us-department-of-the-interior/) nominated Chaco Culture under criterion (iii), to bear unique or exceptional testimony to a cultural tradition or civilisation, and the park's statement of significance points to its monumental architecture, sophisticated astronomical understanding and complex road system ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/chcu/learn/historyculture/worldheritage.htm)). After prevailing for 300 years, new construction slowed at Chaco and its influence as a regional centre began to decline around 1100 or 1200, and the people eventually shifted away and migrated to new areas in the Southwest ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)). Their descendants are the modern Native Americans of the Southwest, including the Hopi, the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico and the Navajo Nation, and Chaco Canyon and its great houses remain a sacred and special place for many southwestern Indian peoples ([U.S. National Park Service](https://home.nps.gov/articles/chaco.htm)).

## The 2025–2026 fight over Greater Chaco

The Greater Chaco landscape is protected, for now, by a federal mineral withdrawal rather than by its World Heritage status. Public Land Order No. 7923 established a 10-mile buffer around the park and closed federally managed lands within that area to new mineral leasing and mining claims for 20 years, covering approximately 336,404 acres ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). The 2023 order remains subject to valid existing rights and does not apply to state, private, Navajo Nation trust or individual allotment lands ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). The [All Pueblo Council of Governors](/organizations/all-pueblo-council-of-governors/), representing the twenty sovereign Pueblo nations of New Mexico and Texas, has supported permanent protection of the Greater Chaco landscape ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)).

In July 2026 the Bureau of Land Management released a draft environmental assessment proposing the full revocation of that protection, opening a 14-day public comment period closing July 29, 2026 ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). The draft considered three outcomes — leaving the withdrawal in place, revoking it entirely, or retaining protections within five miles while reopening lands beyond that distance — and identified full revocation as its proposed action ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). The review followed President Donald Trump's Executive Order 14154 and Interior Secretary's Order 3418, which directed federal officials to review restrictions affecting domestic energy and mineral development ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). The All Pueblo Council of Governors responded with deep concern on July 15, 2026, calling the proposal an attempt to erase protection built over more than a decade by Pueblo nations acting together alongside the Hopi Tribe, the Navajo Nation and the State of New Mexico ([All Pueblo Council of Governors](https://www.archaeologysouthwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2026-07-15_APCG-Pr_Chaco-Draft-EA-1.pdf)).

The Council's central objection is that the landscape has not been fully understood, and the Bureau's own document concedes the point: it reports at least 7,552 known historic properties and at least 145 traditional cultural properties within the ten-mile area, then states those figures represent only a fraction of what is present because large portions of the region have never been formally inventoried ([All Pueblo Council of Governors](https://www.archaeologysouthwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2026-07-15_APCG-Pr_Chaco-Draft-EA-1.pdf)). The Bureau's own analysis also projects that full revocation would yield fourteen new wells over twenty years, with no new wells anticipated within five miles of the park under any alternative considered ([All Pueblo Council of Governors](https://www.archaeologysouthwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2026-07-15_APCG-Pr_Chaco-Draft-EA-1.pdf)). The Pueblo of Acoma's governor, Charles Riley, put the trade in blunt terms: "We are being asked to accept the permanent loss of a landscape our ancestors built, walked and prayed in, in exchange for fourteen wells" ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). Riley added that approximately 90% of the San Juan Basin is already leased, disputing the idea that the Chaco withdrawal prevents the country from meeting its broader energy needs ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)).

The dispute is not a simple two-sided fight, and that complexity is itself part of the story. The Navajo Nation has taken a different position in federal court, challenging the process used to create the 10-mile withdrawal and arguing that Interior did not adequately account for the economic effect on Navajo allottees who receive oil and gas royalty payments, and the Nation has advocated consideration of a smaller five-mile buffer ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). The dispute reflects the checkerboard pattern of land ownership around Chaco, where federal, state, private, Navajo Nation and individually allotted parcels interlock across the San Juan Basin ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). Both Acoma and Laguna pueblos have supported maintaining the withdrawal and previously intervened in litigation to defend the Chaco protections ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)).

Meanwhile the landscape gained a different kind of recognition: on May 20, 2026, the Greater Chaco Cultural Landscape was named to the National Trust for Historic Preservation's 2026 list of America's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places, nominated by the Pueblo of Acoma with support from the All Pueblo Council of Governors and other tribal and non-tribal partners, the second time in 15 years the landscape appeared on the list ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)). The National Trust found that federal and state management has historically treated Chaco as an isolated archaeological park while ignoring the broader landscape and the living Indigenous communities who hold cultural responsibilities to these places ([All Pueblo Council of Governors](https://www.archaeologysouthwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2026-07-15_APCG-Pr_Chaco-Draft-EA-1.pdf)). The Council continues to call on Congress to enact the Chaco Cultural Heritage Area Protection Act, which would make the ten-mile withdrawal permanent, and to maintain the appropriations moratorium in the interim ([All Pueblo Council of Governors](https://www.archaeologysouthwest.org/wp-content/uploads/2026-07-15_APCG-Pr_Chaco-Draft-EA-1.pdf)). Pueblo leaders had travelled to Washington, D.C., in September 2025 and later gathered in Albuquerque to press federal officials to retain the protections and meet with tribal leaders at Chaco ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news-news-alert-newsletters/blm-proposes-full-revocation-chaco-protections-opens-14-day-public)).

It does not claim the Chaco Cultural Heritage Area Protection Act has passed — sources support only that it has been reintroduced and advocated. It does not treat the 10-mile federal mineral withdrawal as a UNESCO buffer zone — UNESCO states the property has no buffer zone. It does not state visitor numbers, entrance fees, or current park operating details, none of which appear in the sources read in full for this profile, and it does not attribute sacred significance for specific sites to specific Pueblo nations beyond the National Park Service's general statement that Chaco Canyon is sacred to many southwestern Indian peoples.
