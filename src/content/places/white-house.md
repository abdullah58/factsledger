## Overview

The White House is the color of paint, and that is not a small detail. The building's walls are porous sandstone, and from the start they were covered with lime-based whitewash to keep them from cracking in winter freezes — which is why it is called the White House, not the other way around ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)). The white has had to be stripped and renewed for two centuries: by Jimmy Carter's administration the paint had built up so thickly that visitors could no longer see the carving details above the windows and doors or any of the beautiful molding, and a major exterior restoration to peel the layers back took twenty years, not finishing until Bill Clinton's administration — when more than 45 coats of paint had been removed from the outside walls ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)).

Beyond its paint, the White House at 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue NW in Washington, D.C., is the official residence and workplace of the president of the United States, and it has housed every president since John Adams moved in during 1800 ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/)). It is also a name the world uses for the presidency itself — the subject of a separate profile, [The White House](https://factsledger.com/organizations/the-white-house/), which treats the institution rather than the building.

## Built by many hands

George Washington selected the site for the President's House in 1791; the cornerstone was laid in 1792, and a design submitted by the Irish-born architect James Hoban was chosen ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/)). After eight years of construction, President John Adams and his wife Abigail moved into the still-unfinished residence — Adams was the second man elected president, but the first to live there ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/), [HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)). Washington, who chose the site, never lived in the house.

Hoban designed the mansion, but hundreds of workers built it — free and enslaved African-Americans and white wage laborers, sawyers, carpenters, stone workers, and bricklayers. Some of the enslaved workers were owned by the city commissioners overseeing the project, or by Hoban himself; most were hired out from their owners in Washington, D.C., Virginia, and Maryland, who pocketed the enslaved workers' wages ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)). They cleared land, quarried and transported stone, and raised the walls over eight years.

Inside, one feature survived nearly everything the house endured. Carved into the stone fireplace of the State Dining Room is John Adams's 1800 prayer from a letter to Abigail: "I pray Heaven to bestow the best of blessings on this house and all that shall hereafter inhabit it. May none but honest and wise men ever rule under this roof." It was engraved there in 1945 ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)).

## Fire, rebuilding, and reinvention

During the War of 1812 the British set fire to the President's House, and Hoban — the original architect — was appointed to rebuild it. James Monroe moved into the building in 1817, and the South Portico was constructed during his administration ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/)). In 1829, Andrew Jackson oversaw the addition of the North Portico ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/)). One source consulted here dates the North Portico's completion to 1830 rather than 1829 ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)) — the sources disagree, so this profile states the disagreement rather than choosing.

The twentieth century remade the building twice. In 1902 Theodore Roosevelt began a major renovation that relocated the president's offices from the second floor of the Residence to the newly constructed West Wing, planned and carried out by the New York firm McKim, Mead and White; his successor William Howard Taft had the Oval Office built within an enlarged office wing in 1909 ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/)). The first Oval Office stood inside that wing from 1909, and it was moved to its present location during Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)). Less than fifty years after the Roosevelt work, the house was showing serious structural weakness: Harry S. Truman began a renovation in which everything but the outer walls was dismantled, the reconstruction overseen by architect Lorenzo Winslow, and the Truman family moved back in 1952 ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/)). The East Wing, developed in 1942, completed the complex's working footprint ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)).

The name itself is a twentieth-century formality. At various times the building was known as the "President's Palace," the "President's House," and the "Executive Mansion"; Theodore Roosevelt officially gave it its current name in 1901 ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/)).

## The grounds

The house looks two stories from the street, but there are six floors — three above ground (the State Floor and two residence floors) and three below ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)). The Residence holds 132 rooms, 35 bathrooms, 412 doors, 147 windows, 28 fireplaces, 8 staircases, and 3 elevators; its kitchen can serve dinner to 140 guests and hors d'oeuvres to more than 1,000 ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/)).

The grounds around it have shrunk from open land to a closed compound. When Adams moved in on November 1, 1800, there was no fence or gate and the grounds were open to pedestrians; Thomas Jefferson fenced the grounds to about nine acres and scaled back the idea of a working farm to vegetable and flower gardens. In 1873 Ulysses S. Grant began closing the grounds at sunset for security; in 1893 Grover Cleveland closed the South Grounds after strangers tried to photograph his young daughter; and the North and South Grounds were closed permanently during Calvin Coolidge's presidency at the Secret Service's recommendation ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)).

The house keeps adding to itself. According to the White House's own account, President [Donald Trump](https://factsledger.com/people/donald-trump/) — who returned to the White House after the 2024 election — announced a State Ballroom project of approximately 90,000 square feet with seating for 650, replacing the East Wing (built in 1902 and extensively modified, including a 1942 second-story addition), with construction commencing in September 2025 and privately donated funds of $250 million ([whitehouse.gov](https://www.whitehouse.gov/about/)). [Abraham Lincoln](https://factsledger.com/people/abraham-lincoln/) met his cabinet in the second-floor office in the house's southeast corner — the room now known as the Lincoln Bedroom ([HistoryExtra](https://www.historyextra.com/period/21st-century/white-house-facts-president-usa-american-history-when-was-built-washington-rooms-donald-trump-fire/)).

## What this profile does not claim

It does not resolve the North Portico's date — whitehouse.gov says 1829, BBC History Magazine says 1830 — and it states the conflict rather than choosing. It describes the State Ballroom only as the White House itself announced it; the physical demolition of the East Wing and any legal challenge are outside this profile's full-read sources, so the profile takes no position on them. It makes no claim about the National Park Service's stewardship of the grounds or the wider President's Park, which was not verified from a full-read source. And it covers the building and grounds only — the presidency-as-institution, including the 2026 press-credential litigation, belongs to the separate [White House](https://factsledger.com/organizations/the-white-house/) organization profile.
