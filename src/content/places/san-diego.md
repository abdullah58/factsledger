## Overview

San Diego is a city in southern [California](/places/california/), [United States](/places/united-states/), on the Pacific coast immediately north of the border with [Mexico](/places/mexico/) — the site of California's first Spanish mission and presidio, founded July 16, 1769, and today the state's second-largest city, with an estimated 1,404,452 residents as of July 1, 2024 ([City of San Diego](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/fy26ab_v1cityprofile.pdf)). The city calls itself "America's Finest City," and it is the eighth-largest city in the United States, covering 326 square miles of land and more than 70 miles of Pacific coastline ([City of San Diego](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/fy26ab_v1cityprofile.pdf)). Its modern identity rests on three pillars that all trace back to the same bay: the U.S. Navy, which has been its largest single economic contributor; the Torrey Pines research mesa, which turned a marine station into a biotech cluster; and Balboa Park, whose zoo grew out of a world's fair ([U.S. Naval Institute](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2007/october/interwined-heritage); [American Association of Immunologists](https://www.aai.org/About/History/History-Articles-Keep-for-Hierarchy/Immunology-in-San-Diego-Staking-Claims-on-Torrey); [San Diego History Center](https://sandiegohistory.org/journal/2016/april/san-diego-zoo-100-years/)).

## From Kumeyaay homeland to Spanish mission

San Diego's written history begins with people who were already there. The area belonged to the region's first Americans, and the Kumeyaay arrived around 1000 B.C. and mixed with the existing inhabitants; when the Spanish mission was established in 1769 there were roughly 25,000 to 30,000 Indians living in the area, and today about 20,000 Kumeyaay descendants live in San Diego County on its 18 reservations — more than in any other county in the United States ([NOAA Fisheries](https://permanent.fdlp.gov/websites/www.nwfsc.noaa.gov/research/divisions/cb/ecosystem/humandim/communityprofiles/California/SanDiego_CA.pdf)). The first European to see the bay was Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo, who sailed in for Spain in 1542 and named it San Miguel; sixty years later Sebastián Vizcaíno renamed it San Diego, on the feast day of San Diego de Alcalá ([NOAA Fisheries](https://permanent.fdlp.gov/websites/www.nwfsc.noaa.gov/research/divisions/cb/ecosystem/humandim/communityprofiles/California/SanDiego_CA.pdf)).

Permanent colonization came in 1769, and it nearly failed on arrival. The "Sacred Expedition" under Gaspar de Portolà marched 51 days from Baja California; the first land party reached San Diego on May 14, Father Junípero Serra arrived July 1, and of more than 300 men who set out, only 126 survived the journey ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/san-diego-presidio/)). On July 16, 1769, Serra founded the mission on the hill north of the encampment — now Presidio Hill — christening the enterprise "San Diego de Alcalá." It was the first of the 21 Franciscan missions in Alta California and, with the soldiers' garrison beside it, the first military post: the foundation of Western European colonization in California, sometimes called "the Plymouth Rock of the Pacific Coast" ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/san-diego-presidio/)). The site is the first Spanish settlement in Upper California ([1911 Encyclopædia Britannica](http://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/San_Diego)).

The mission's early years were violent and unsettled. Within a month of the presidio's founding the Kumeyaay staged their first attack on the fort, and in 1774 the mission church was moved about five miles northeast — where it remains today — to find better water and distance from the soldiers ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/san-diego-presidio/)). In November 1775 the Kumeyaay attacked, looted and burned the relocated mission, killing its head, Father Luis Jaime; the church was rebuilt, and the presidio served as the Spanish civil and political center for the region ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/san-diego-presidio/)). Mexico won independence in 1821, Spain officially abandoned the Presidio on April 20, 1822, and the missions were secularized; in 1833 the Mexican government began parceling out mission property, and by 1835 San Diego was a civil pueblo of more than 400 people ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/san-diego-presidio/); [NOAA Fisheries](https://permanent.fdlp.gov/websites/www.nwfsc.noaa.gov/research/divisions/cb/ecosystem/humandim/communityprofiles/California/SanDiego_CA.pdf)).

## A city incorporated twice

The American period began from the sea. At dawn on July 29, 1846, the U.S. Navy sloop Cyane arrived off Point Loma during the Mexican War; sailors and Marines went ashore and raised the Stars and Stripes in the village plaza — the first raising of the U.S. flag in Southern California, commemorated by a plaque in Old Town today — and a small fortification, Fort Stockton, rose above the plaza ([U.S. Naval Institute](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2007/october/interwined-heritage)). The town was occupied by American forces in July 1846 and reoccupied in November after a temporary dispossession, "no blood being shed" ([1911 Encyclopædia Britannica](http://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/San_Diego)).

What followed was a strange civic history: San Diego was incorporated as a city in 1850, then lost its charter in 1852 for lack of growth, and by 1867 had only a dozen inhabitants ([1911 Encyclopædia Britannica](http://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/San_Diego)). The Gold Rush of 1849 and the first rail link had both bypassed the town ([NOAA Fisheries](https://permanent.fdlp.gov/websites/www.nwfsc.noaa.gov/research/divisions/cb/ecosystem/humandim/communityprofiles/California/SanDiego_CA.pdf)). Rescue came from a speculator: in 1867 Alonzo Horton bought 960 acres of waterfront and laid out a "New Town" about three miles south of the old settlement; its population rose to 2,300 by 1870, and this new San Diego was incorporated in 1872 ([1911 Encyclopædia Britannica](http://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/San_Diego); [NOAA Fisheries](https://permanent.fdlp.gov/websites/www.nwfsc.noaa.gov/research/divisions/cb/ecosystem/humandim/communityprofiles/California/SanDiego_CA.pdf)). A crash in 1873–74 stalled it again until the Santa Fé railway arrived in 1884, and after 1900 growth was rapid: 17,700 people in 1900, 39,578 in 1910, 147,995 in 1930, 573,224 in 1960, and 1,223,400 in 2000 ([1911 Encyclopædia Britannica](http://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/San_Diego); [NOAA Fisheries](https://permanent.fdlp.gov/websites/www.nwfsc.noaa.gov/research/divisions/cb/ecosystem/humandim/communityprofiles/California/SanDiego_CA.pdf)).

One visible fact about the city is younger than it looks. The Mediterranean-style, Mission architecture and Spanish street names seen in San Diego today were built by developers for the Panama-California Expositions of 1915–1916, "rather than remnants of the city's colonial past" ([NOAA Fisheries](https://permanent.fdlp.gov/websites/www.nwfsc.noaa.gov/research/divisions/cb/ecosystem/humandim/communityprofiles/California/SanDiego_CA.pdf)). The old town at the foot of Presidio Hill survives separately as Old Town San Diego State Historic Park, established in 1969 for California's bicentennial, while the 1929 Serra Museum on the hill is a deliberate re-creation, not a surviving mission building ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/military-history-and-science/san-diego-presidio/)).

## The Navy's city

No story of San Diego's development from the mid-1800s to the present can be told without the Navy — its maritime tradition is, as the Naval Institute puts it, "tightly intertwined with the history of the city itself" ([U.S. Naval Institute](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2007/october/interwined-heritage)). The Navy's permanent return began modestly: the coaling station at La Playa, commissioned in 1905, was its first permanent base in the city. Then Congressman William Kettner, elected in 1912, lobbied relentlessly for naval investment, dangling offers of free land; the city won harbor dredging contracts in 1913, a second naval communications station in 1914, an initial naval training station in 1917, a naval supply depot in 1921, and a naval hospital in 1922 ([U.S. Naval Institute](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2007/october/interwined-heritage)). The permanent Naval Training Station was commissioned in 1923, and over 1.75 million recruits passed through it before it closed in 1997; the site is now the Liberty Station community of parks, homes, offices and shops ([U.S. Naval Institute](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2007/october/interwined-heritage)).

San Diego is also where American naval aviation was born. In 1911 North Island was undeveloped scrub, and on January 26, 1911, Glenn Curtiss flew his "hydroaeroplane" from Spanish Bight — the first flight of a seaplane in the United States — and within a year the entire flying Navy, three aircraft and four pilots, was stationed there ([U.S. Naval Institute](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2007/october/interwined-heritage)). A pier built in 1924 received the Navy's first aircraft carrier, the Langley, and the base became the center for carrier doctrine; nearly every carrier that has served in the U.S. Navy has visited the city. The city's largest museum is itself a carrier: the USS Midway (CV-41), the Navy's longest-serving carrier, opened on the downtown waterfront in 2004 ([U.S. Naval Institute](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2007/october/interwined-heritage)). War made the relationship permanent: in 1941, after Pearl Harbor, headquarters of the U.S. Pacific Fleet moved to San Diego ([NOAA Fisheries](https://permanent.fdlp.gov/websites/www.nwfsc.noaa.gov/research/divisions/cb/ecosystem/humandim/communityprofiles/California/SanDiego_CA.pdf)). The Naval Institute's history calls the Navy "the largest single contributor to the city's economy," and the Balboa Park training station it built soon eclipsed the Navy's primary West Coast training facility in [San Francisco](/places/san-francisco/) Bay ([U.S. Naval Institute](https://www.usni.org/magazines/naval-history-magazine/2007/october/interwined-heritage)).

## Science on the mesa, and the modern economy

The city's second great transformation began on Torrey Pines Mesa, on land the city owned as pueblo lands. Bioscience research started in 1909, when newspaper magnate and philanthropist Ellen Browning Scripps donated $150,000 to support the Marine Biological Laboratory in La Jolla, the western hemisphere's first permanent marine science center; in 1924, inspired by the discovery of insulin, she gave a further $300,000 toward a hospital and clinic to investigate diabetes ([American Association of Immunologists](https://www.aai.org/About/History/History-Articles-Keep-for-Hierarchy/Immunology-in-San-Diego-Staking-Claims-on-Torrey)). The University of California, San Diego was officially established on November 18, 1960, as a school focused on "mathematics, physics, chemistry, and the earth and biological sciences," and Jonas Salk chose the mesa over the San Francisco Bay area for his institute: construction of the Salk Institute for Biological Studies began in 1962, and its first laboratory opened in 1963 ([American Association of Immunologists](https://www.aai.org/About/History/History-Articles-Keep-for-Hierarchy/Immunology-in-San-Diego-Staking-Claims-on-Torrey)). In 1978 a UCSD professor, Ivor Royston, launched Hybritech, the region's first biotech company, and the alumni of that company went on to found dozens of other firms; immunologists alone have founded or conducted research at more than 100 biotech companies in the area ([American Association of Immunologists](https://www.aai.org/About/History/History-Articles-Keep-for-Hierarchy/Immunology-in-San-Diego-Staking-Claims-on-Torrey)).

That research base now shows up in the city's accounts. UC San Diego received $1.73 billion in new research funding in Fiscal Year 2024, and its Scripps Institution of Oceanography is one of the world's largest and most renowned centers for ocean, earth and atmospheric science research ([City of San Diego](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/fy26ab_v1cityprofile.pdf)). In 2024 the county's life-science workforce was over 71,000 direct employees paid an average of $172,000 a year — a regional economic impact of $54.1 billion, with biotechnology at 42 percent of life-sciences employment — and San Diego took in $5.8 billion in venture capital funding in 2024, behind only four other U.S. cities ([City of San Diego](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/fy26ab_v1cityprofile.pdf)). The defense economy is just as large: a San Diego Military Advisory Council study estimated that in 2024 defense activities generated about $63.2 billion of gross regional product for San Diego County — 23.7 percent of the total — and about 369,000 jobs, 24.2 percent of all employment in the region ([City of San Diego](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/fy26ab_v1cityprofile.pdf)). Tourism completes the picture: about 32.2 million visitors spent approximately $14.8 billion in 2024, and Comic Con International's 2023 event drew about 135,000 attendees for a $161.1 million regional impact; the convention has committed to staying through 2027 ([City of San Diego](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/fy26ab_v1cityprofile.pdf)). The city is also a working tech address: productivity-software company [ClickUp](/organizations/clickup/) is headquartered in San Diego.

The city stays connected by air, rail and trolley: San Diego International Airport at Lindbergh Field handled 25.2 million passengers in 2024, Amtrak runs intercity rail from downtown to [Los Angeles](/places/los-angeles/) and north to San Luis Obispo, and the trolley's Mid-Coast extension to University City, next to UC San Diego, entered service on November 21, 2021 ([City of San Diego](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/fy26ab_v1cityprofile.pdf)). Tourism has rebounded strongly since the height of the [COVID-19 pandemic](/events/covid-19-pandemic/), with the third-highest hotel occupancy in the country in 2024 at 74.3 percent ([City of San Diego](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/fy26ab_v1cityprofile.pdf)).

Balboa Park ties the old and new city together: a 1,200-acre urban park with 21 museums and cultural institutions, and the home of the San Diego Zoo, which grew out of the animals left over from the 1915–1916 Panama-California Exposition ([City of San Diego](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/2025-08/fy26ab_v1cityprofile.pdf); [San Diego History Center](https://sandiegohistory.org/journal/2016/april/san-diego-zoo-100-years/)). The zoo's own origin is a local legend with a paper trail: after hearing a lion roar at the exposition, Dr. Harry Wegeforth organized the Zoological Society of San Diego, incorporated November 17, 1916; the zoo opened in 1923, survived funding fights that were only settled when a two-cent property-tax allotment passed in 1934, pioneered moated enclosures — the first giraffe moat anywhere, in 1962 — and now cares for more than 6,000 animals across two sites, with a "Frozen Zoo" banking genetic material from 10,000 individual animals of 1,000 species ([San Diego History Center](https://sandiegohistory.org/journal/2016/april/san-diego-zoo-100-years/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**When was San Diego founded?**
July 16, 1769, when Father Junípero Serra founded Mission San Diego de Alcalá beside the Presidio of San Diego. It was the first mission and the first military post in Alta California, making San Diego the first European settlement in what is now California.

**Is San Diego the oldest city in California?**
In the sense that matters: it is the site of California's first Spanish mission and presidio, founded in 1769. But the present downtown is not the original settlement — that was at Presidio Hill and Old Town, and the modern city center is Horton's 1867 "New Town."

**How big is the military presence in San Diego?**
Very large. Defense activities generated about $63.2 billion of gross regional product for San Diego County in 2024 — 23.7 percent of the total — and about 369,000 jobs, nearly a quarter of all employment in the region.

**What is Torrey Pines?**
A mesa in northern San Diego that holds the University of California, San Diego (founded 1960), the Salk Institute (construction began 1962) and the Scripps research institutions — the core of a biotech cluster of more than 100 companies.

**What is Balboa Park?**
A 1,200-acre urban park with 21 museums and cultural institutions, built up around the 1915–1916 Panama-California Exposition. It is also home to the San Diego Zoo, which began with animals left over from that exposition.

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not smooth over the incorporation history: San Diego was incorporated in 1850, lost its charter in 1852, and was reincorporated in 1872 — a federal profile's claim of an 1868 incorporation does not fit that sequence and is flagged, not followed. It does not present the city's Spanish look as colonial survival: the Mission-style architecture largely dates to the 1915–1916 expositions, and the Serra Museum is a 1929 re-creation. It does not state Census Bureau demographics (median income, foreign-born share) because the Census QuickFacts page could not be fetched for this profile. It does not claim the 1833 secularization year on more than one source, and it does not cover the Hotel del Coronado, Lindbergh and the Spirit of St. Louis, the current mayor, or the San Diego–Tijuana metro population, none of which was verified from a fully-read source.
