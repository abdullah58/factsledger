## Overview

**Oklahoma** is a south-central state of the [United States](/places/united-states/) admitted on November 16, 1907, as the 46th state — a place whose modern identity is shaped by three forces: its origin as Indian Territory, its oil-and-agriculture economy, and some of the most violent tornadoes ever recorded, including the F5 that struck [Blackwell](/places/blackwell-oklahoma/) on May 25, 1955 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state); [National Weather Service](https://preview.weather.gov/oun/events-19550525)).

The state borders Colorado and [Kansas](/places/kansas/) to the north, Missouri and Arkansas to the east, Texas to the south and west, and New Mexico to the west of its Panhandle ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state)). Its capital and largest city is Oklahoma City, near the center of the state ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state)). The Census Bureau counted 3,959,353 residents in 2020 and estimated 4,123,288 by July 1, 2025 ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/OK/NES010221)). The name comes from two Choctaw words, *okla* ("people") and *humma* ("red") ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state)).

This profile is about Oklahoma the state — not Oklahoma City, its capital, and not the musical *Oklahoma!*, whose title song is the state song ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state)).

## From Indian Territory to the 46th state

Oklahoma began as Indian Territory, the destination for tribes removed from the Southeast under the Indian Removal Act of 1830 ([National Park Service](http://home.nps.gov/trte/learn/historyculture/index.htm)). The next opening came by land run, not treaty: in 1889, Congress authorized President Benjamin Harrison to proclaim two million acres of the Unassigned Lands open for settlement, and at noon on April 22, an estimated 50,000 people rushed to claim homesteads under the Homestead Act's 160-acre rule ([Oklahoma Historical Society](https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry?entry=LA014); [HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/November-16/oklahoma-enters-the-union)). About 11,000 homesteads were claimed that day, and those who had entered early to beat the starting gun were called "sooners" — hence the nickname ([Oklahoma Historical Society](https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry?entry=LA014)). The run led quickly to Oklahoma Territory under the Organic Act of 1890, and in 1907 Congress admitted Indian Territory and Oklahoma Territory together as a single state ([Oklahoma Historical Society](https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry?entry=LA014); [HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/November-16/oklahoma-enters-the-union)).

Statehood did not bring an easy century. In 1921, a white mob destroyed Tulsa's Greenwood district — "Black Wall Street," a community of 10,000 founded in 1906 on former Indian Territory — killing hundreds of Black residents and destroying more than 1,000 houses and businesses; rebuilding began almost immediately ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/articles/black-wall-street-tulsa-race-massacre)). In the 1930s, drought made Oklahoma part of the Dust Bowl, and poor tenant farmers known as "Okies" traveled west seeking work; oil brought a major economic boom in the 1970s ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/November-16/oklahoma-enters-the-union)).

## Native nations and the McGirt ruling

Oklahoma is home to 38 federally recognized tribal nations, including the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek), Seminole, Osage, Comanche, Kiowa, and Cheyenne and Arapaho nations ([Oklahoma Historical Society](https://www.okhistory.org/research/tribalnations)). The deep Native presence is a direct result of forced removal: after the Indian Removal Act of 1830, the Choctaw, Muscogee Creek, Seminole, and Chickasaw nations were relocated to Indian Territory, and the Cherokee were forced west after a minority faction signed the Treaty of New Echota in 1835 ([National Park Service](http://home.nps.gov/trte/learn/historyculture/index.htm)). In May 1838, U.S. troops evicted more than 16,000 Cherokee from homelands in [Tennessee](/places/tennessee/), Alabama, North Carolina, and Georgia; fourteen detachments traveled west between August and December 1838 under Principal Chief John Ross, and more than a thousand Cherokee died on the journey — with perhaps several thousand more perishing from its consequences ([National Park Service](http://home.nps.gov/trte/learn/historyculture/index.htm)).

That history is still live law. On July 9, 2020, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled 5–4 in *McGirt v. Oklahoma* that the Muscogee (Creek) Nation's reservation, promised as a "permanent home" by an 1833 treaty, was never disestablished by Congress — so nearly half of Oklahoma remains Indian land outside state jurisdiction for major crimes ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/july-9/scotus-mcgirt-v-oklahoma)). The case began with Jimcy McGirt, convicted in state court in 1997 for crimes committed on tribal land; after the ruling he was retried and convicted in federal court, and Oklahoma has since acknowledged that the reservations established in 1833 continue to exist within its borders ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/july-9/scotus-mcgirt-v-oklahoma)).

## Land, weather, and the 1955 Blackwell tornado

Oklahoma's terrain runs from rolling, timbered hills in the east to treeless high plains in the Panhandle, with three mountain subregions — the Ouachita, Arbuckle, and Wichita mountains — in the south ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state)). Drainage follows the Arkansas and Red rivers; the Red forms nearly all of the southern border with Texas, and the land slopes from 4,973 feet at Black Mesa in the northwest to about 300 feet on the Red River in the southeast ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state)). Most lakes are man-made, created by damming rivers; the largest body of water by volume is Lake Texoma, on the Texas–Oklahoma border ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state)).

The same geography puts Oklahoma in the path of the continent's most violent storms. The [National Weather Service](/organizations/national-weather-service/) records repeated F5 tornadoes: Snyder in 1905 (97 killed), Antlers in 1945 (69 killed, 353 injured), Woodward in 1947 — the state's deadliest, with 116 killed in Oklahoma — and Blackwell in 1955 ([National Weather Service](https://preview.weather.gov/oun/tornadodata-ok-f5tornadoes)). The Woodward disaster, with other devastating tornadoes of the late 1940s and early 1950s, pushed the Weather Bureau to begin a tornado watch and warning program in 1953 ([National Weather Service](https://preview.weather.gov/oun/tornadodata-ok-f5tornadoes)).

The Blackwell tornado shows why. It touched down about 8 miles west of [Marland](/places/marland-oklahoma/) around 9:00 p.m., passed east of Tonkawa — where its parent storm dropped baseball-sized hail — and struck the east side of Blackwell at 9:27 p.m. local time, killing 19 people in town and one more to the northeast, and injuring 280 ([National Weather Service](https://preview.weather.gov/oun/events-19550525)). About 80 blocks were damaged or destroyed; 400 homes were destroyed and 500 damaged, and the [Hazel Atlas Glass Company](/organizations/hazel-atlas-glass-company/) plant and the [Acme Foundry](/organizations/acme-foundry/) were destroyed ([National Weather Service](https://preview.weather.gov/oun/tornadodata-ok-f5tornadoes); [National Weather Service](https://preview.weather.gov/oun/events-19550525)). The [Blackwell Journal-Tribune](/organizations/blackwell-journal-tribune/) surveyed the damage building by building, counting about 190 homes and buildings completely destroyed ([National Weather Service](https://preview.weather.gov/oun/events-19550525)). The same supercell then produced a second F5 that killed 80 people in and near Udall, Kansas — covered in the profile of the [1955 Udall tornado](/events/1955-udall-tornado/) and the wider [Great Plains outbreak](/events/great-plains-tornado-outbreak-may-1955/) ([National Weather Service](https://preview.weather.gov/oun/tornadodata-ok-f5tornadoes)). The full Blackwell story is told in the profile of the [1955 Blackwell Oklahoma tornado](/events/1955-blackwell-oklahoma-tornado/).

## An energy and agriculture economy

Oklahoma was once basically agricultural — the Dust Bowl locale of John Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath* — and it now has an increasingly diversified economy in which the service sector employs the greatest number of people ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state)). The state still ranks high nationally in mineral production, including petroleum, natural gas, and coal, and in agriculture livestock ranks first, followed by wheat, dairy products, cotton, and soybeans ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Oklahoma-state)). Oklahoma's energy sector employed 139,809 people in 2024 — 8.2% of all state employment — with fuels alone accounting for 57,907 of those jobs ([U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/media/348936)). That fuels-heavy mix means energy prices and drilling activity still move Oklahoma's job market more than in most states. The Census Bureau puts median household income at $65,039 and the poverty rate at 14.9% ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/OK/NES010221)).

## Frequently asked questions

**When did Oklahoma become a state?**
Oklahoma was admitted on November 16, 1907, as the 46th state, when Congress joined Indian Territory and Oklahoma Territory into a single state. Voters of the two territories approved a constitution on September 17, 1907, and President Theodore Roosevelt welcomed the new state in November.

**How many tribal nations are in Oklahoma?**
The Oklahoma Historical Society lists 38 federally recognized tribal nations, including the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek), and Seminole nations. Many secondary pages still say 39; this profile follows the Historical Society and the U.S. Department of Justice, which both give 38.

**What happened in Blackwell, Oklahoma, in 1955?**
On the evening of May 25, 1955, an F5 tornado struck the east side of Blackwell, killing 20 people and injuring 280, destroying 400 homes and damaging 500 more across about 80 blocks. The same supercell then produced a second F5 that killed 80 people in and near Udall, Kansas.

**Is Oklahoma in Tornado Alley?**
Oklahoma is commonly counted as part of Tornado Alley, the central-Plains region known for violent tornadoes. The National Weather Service record supports the reputation: F5 tornadoes struck Snyder in 1905, Antlers in 1945, Woodward in 1947, and Blackwell in 1955, and the 1953 warning program grew out of that history.

**What is Oklahoma's economy based on?**
Energy and agriculture remain central — petroleum, natural gas, and coal production, plus livestock and wheat — but the service sector now employs the most people. Energy alone employed 139,809 Oklahomans in 2024, about 8.2% of all state employment, so drilling cycles still swing the job market.
