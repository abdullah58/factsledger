## Overview

Ottawa is the capital of [Canada](/places/canada/), a bilingual city of 1,017,449 people in southeastern [Ontario](/places/ontario/) on the south bank of the Ottawa River, across from Gatineau, Quebec. Chosen by Queen Victoria in 1857 over larger rivals such as [Toronto](/places/toronto/) and [Montreal](/places/montreal/), it has been the seat of Canada's federal government since Confederation in 1867, and it holds Parliament Hill, the [Supreme Court of Canada](/organizations/supreme-court-of-canada/), the Bank of Canada and the Royal Canadian Mint ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa)).

This profile is about Ottawa the city, not the Ottawa River that gives it its name, and not the Ottawa (Outaouais) people, who settled in the area only briefly in the mid-1600s. The city stands at the meeting point of the Ottawa, Gatineau and Rideau rivers, on a river about 790 miles (1,270 km) long whose watershed carried furs, timber and minerals toward the St. Lawrence ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa)). Its name is believed to come from an Algonquian word meaning "to trade", a legacy of the Algonquin First Nation traders of the Ottawa River valley ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa)).

## From Bytown to the capital

The future capital began as a construction camp. Samuel de Champlain first described the site in 1613; after France ceded New France to Britain in 1763, timber drew Philemon Wright, who founded Wrightsville, the first permanent town, in 1800. The War of 1812 exposed the St. Lawrence as vulnerable, so Britain built a canal: Lt. Col. John By of the Royal Engineers built the more than 126-mile (203-km) Rideau Canal in 1826–32, and the workers' town he laid out became Bytown — a town in 1850, the city of Ottawa in 1855 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa); [Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-services-procurement/services/infrastructure-buildings/parliamentary-precinct/history.html)).

The canal was never used militarily, but it made Ottawa a timber town — and then, improbably, a capital. The 1837 rebellion forced Upper and Lower Canada into one Province of Canada in 1841, and the rival claims of Quebec City, Toronto, Montreal and Kingston deadlocked politics until leaders asked Queen Victoria to choose. She chose Ottawa in late 1857: defensible, far from the American border, set on a cliff and reachable by rail. Ottawa was declared the capital on 17 February 1858 and kept the role when the Dominion was formed in 1867 ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-services-procurement/services/infrastructure-buildings/parliamentary-precinct/history.html); [Parks Canada](https://parks.canada.ca/culture/designation/lieu-site/capitale-capital)).

## Parliament Hill: built, burned and rebuilt

The new capital needed a parliament, and it got one by competition. In May 1859 the Department of Public Works invited designs; 298 proposals arrived, and Thomas Fuller and Chilion Jones won the Centre Block while Thomas Stent and Augustus Laver won the East and West Blocks ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-services-procurement/services/infrastructure-buildings/parliamentary-precinct/history.html)). Ground was broken that December on 25 acres of limestone cliff above the Ottawa River, the Prince of Wales laid the cornerstone in September 1860, and costs running to two-and-a-half times the plan halted work until 1863. The buildings were still unfinished when the Dominion was born on 1 July 1867, but Parliament held its first session there that November, and the Library of Parliament was completed in 1876 ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-services-procurement/services/infrastructure-buildings/parliamentary-precinct/history.html)).

Fire then remade the Hill. On 3 February 1916, while the House of Commons was sitting, a blaze consumed the Centre Block, killing seven people; the Library survived only because librarian Alpheus Todd had insisted on iron fire doors and clerk "Connie" MacCormac ordered them shut. Parliament sat temporarily in the Victoria Memorial Museum while a larger Centre Block was rebuilt; it opened in February 1920, and the Peace Tower was dedicated in 1927 as a monument to Canada's war dead ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-services-procurement/services/infrastructure-buildings/parliamentary-precinct/history.html)).

## A capital remade: Gréber, the NCC and the Greenbelt

For decades the capital still looked like the mill town it had been: a fire in 1900 razed much of Ottawa and destroyed Hull, and smokestacks and railways dominated the waterfront even as the civil service grew. ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa)). The remaking had begun in 1899 with the Ottawa Improvement Commission, created to acquire and beautify parks, streets and drives; its successor, the Federal District Commission, followed in 1927. In 1936 Prime Minister William Lyon Mackenzie King invited the French architect Jacques Gréber to plan the city; his 1950 plan called for parkland, a greenbelt to control growth, and railways and industry removed from the centre ([National Capital Commission](https://ncc-ccn.gc.ca/blog/stories-of-our-past)).

Parliament gave the plan teeth: the National Capital Commission was created under the 1958 National Capital Act, coming into being in 1959, the National Capital Region grew to 4,660 square kilometres on both sides of the river, and a 37,500-acre Greenbelt was assembled around the city ([National Capital Commission](https://ncc-ccn.gc.ca/blog/stories-of-our-past)). The Commission also became the capital's gardener: in 1946 it received 20,500 tulip bulbs from Princess Juliana of the Netherlands in thanks for wartime refuge, and its landscape architects still plan the Tulip Festival gardens ([National Capital Commission](https://ncc-ccn.gc.ca/blog/stories-of-our-past)). Today the federal government is the major employer, the technology sector provides many jobs, and the University of Ottawa and the National Gallery mark the city's bilingual character ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa)).

Ottawa counted 1,017,449 residents in the 2021 census, up from 883,391 in 2011 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa)) — growth of more than 130,000 people in a decade inside a city whose Greenbelt was drawn precisely to contain sprawl.

## FAQ

### Why is Ottawa the capital of Canada?

Queen Victoria chose Ottawa in 1857 after the rival claims of Quebec City, Toronto, Montreal and Kingston deadlocked the legislature of the Province of Canada. Ottawa sat on the boundary between English-speaking Ontario and French-speaking Quebec, was reachable by rail, and was far from the American border on a defensible cliff. ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa); [Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-services-procurement/services/infrastructure-buildings/parliamentary-precinct/history.html))

### When did Ottawa become the capital?

Ottawa was selected in late 1857 and officially declared the capital of the United Province of Canada on 17 February 1858. It retained the role when the Dominion of Canada was formed on 1 July 1867, and the Fathers of Confederation reaffirmed the choice that year. ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-services-procurement/services/infrastructure-buildings/parliamentary-precinct/history.html))

### What is the Rideau Canal?

The Rideau Canal is the more than 126-mile (203-km) waterway built between 1826 and 1832 under Lt. Col. John By, linking Ottawa with Lake Ontario at Kingston as a secure alternative to the St. Lawrence. Never used militarily, it drove Ottawa's early growth and is now a UNESCO World Heritage site used for boating and skating. ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa))

### Is Ottawa bilingual?

Ottawa is one of the most bilingual cities in Canada, a consequence of its position on the boundary between English-speaking Ontario and French-speaking Quebec and its role as the national capital. The University of Ottawa is a bilingual institution, and the National Gallery of Canada operates as a bilingual arts centre. ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ottawa))

### What happened to Parliament Hill in 1916?

On 3 February 1916 a fire destroyed the Centre Block while the House of Commons was sitting, killing seven people. The Library of Parliament survived behind iron fire doors. Parliament sat temporarily in the Victoria Memorial Museum while a larger Centre Block was rebuilt; it opened in 1920, and the Peace Tower was dedicated in 1927. ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-services-procurement/services/infrastructure-buildings/parliamentary-precinct/history.html))
