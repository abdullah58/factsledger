## Overview

Toronto is the capital of [Ontario](/places/ontario/) and [Canada](/places/canada/)'s most populous city, with 2,794,356 residents counted in the 2021 census — 7.6% of Canada's population — and it is the country's financial centre, its largest tech hub, and the home of the legal-tech company [Blue J](/organizations/blue-j/) ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto); [City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/92e3-City-Planning-2021-Census-Backgrounder-Population-Dwellings-Backgrounder.pdf)).

The city sits on the northwestern shore of Lake Ontario in southeastern Canada, on a site of 632 square kilometres, with a metropolitan area of 5,905 square kilometres and a 2021 metropolitan population of 6,202,225 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto)). Its position on the lake, with access to Atlantic shipping via the St. Lawrence Seaway and to major American industrial centres via the Great Lakes, helped make it an international trading centre — and its location gives it the greatest economic ties to, and influence from, the [United States](/places/united-states/) of any Canadian city ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto)). The skyline is dominated by the CN Tower, a communications and observation spire 553 metres high ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto)).

## From York to the megacity: history

Toronto's recorded history begins before the city itself. First Nations peoples inhabited the site, using the Toronto Passage — a portage from the mouth of the Humber River to Georgian Bay — as a trade route, and French fur traders built small posts in the area in the early 1700s before Fort Rouillé was burned in 1759 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/History)). In 1787 the British Crown opened negotiations with three Mississauga chiefs for the purchase of a site for the future capital, acquiring about 250,000 acres fronting the lake for £1,700 plus cloth, axes and other trading goods ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/History)).

In 1793 Lieutenant-Governor John Graves Simcoe selected the site as the capital of Upper Canada and changed its name from Toronto to York; two years later the capital consisted of only 12 cottages and a small military establishment ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/History)). The City of Toronto's own history records the same birth: a threatened American invasion led British officials to establish a naval arsenal, build Fort York, and move the colonial capital here from the exposed border town of Niagara ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/explore-enjoy/history-art-culture/museums/virtual-exhibits/textures-of-a-lost-toronto/institutional-toronto/)). The invasion Simcoe feared came anyway: during the War of 1812, York — population 700 — was taken by American forces in April 1813 and pillaged and occupied for 11 days ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/History)).

York recovered and grew fast, from 720 people in 1816 to more than 9,000 in 1834, when the town was incorporated and its former name of Toronto restored ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/History)). The exact date was March 6, 1834 ([Ontario Genealogical Society](https://torontofamilyhistory.org/researching-toronto-ancestors/chronology/)). At Confederation in 1867 Toronto was named the capital of the province of Ontario, adding administrative and public-service employment to its industrial base ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/History)). By the 1976 census it had passed [Montreal](/places/montreal/) to become the largest city in Canada ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/History)).

The modern city took its shape in two reorganizations. In 1953 the Corporation of Metropolitan Toronto federated the city with its surrounding municipalities, its council first meeting on January 1, 1954; in 1967 the thirteen municipalities were reduced to six ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/Administration-and-society)). In 1997 the Ontario legislature voted to combine those six into a single "megacity", effective January 1, 1998 — Toronto, Etobicoke, Scarborough, York, East York and North York becoming one City of Toronto ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/Administration-and-society); [Ontario Genealogical Society](https://torontofamilyhistory.org/researching-toronto-ancestors/chronology/)).

## Government: a mayor, 25 councillors, and one Act

Toronto is a single-tier municipality whose powers come principally from the City of Toronto Act, 2006, the provincial legislation that defines what the city can do ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/accountability-operations-customer-service/get-involved-how-government-works/?WT.rd_id=%2Fcity-government%2Faccountability-operations-customer-service%2Fget-involved-how-government-works%2Ftorontos-governance-system%2F)). City Council has 26 members: the Mayor, elected city-wide, and 25 councillors, each elected in one of the city's wards ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/accountability-operations-customer-service/get-involved-how-government-works/?WT.rd_id=%2Fcity-government%2Faccountability-operations-customer-service%2Fget-involved-how-government-works%2Ftorontos-governance-system%2F)). The Act also grants the Mayor special powers over council committees, city administration and the budget, including veto powers related to Provincial Priorities ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/city-government/accountability-operations-customer-service/get-involved-how-government-works/?WT.rd_id=%2Fcity-government%2Faccountability-operations-customer-service%2Fget-involved-how-government-works%2Ftorontos-governance-system%2F)). The ward count itself has been politically contested — it was set to rise to 47 before the provincial government cut it to 25 in 2018 — but the 26-member structure has held since ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Toronto/Administration-and-society)).

## A city of immigrants: people and diversity

Toronto's defining demographic fact is immigration. The 2021 census counted 1,286,140 immigrants in the city — 46.6% of the population, against 23.0% for Canada as a whole — and only 47.1% of residents were born in Canada ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/8ff2-2021-Census-Backgrounder-Immigration-Ethnoracial-Mobility-Migration-Religion-FINAL1.1-corrected.pdf)). More than half of residents, 52.9%, were first generation, meaning neither parent was known to have been born in Canada ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/8ff2-2021-Census-Backgrounder-Immigration-Ethnoracial-Mobility-Migration-Religion-FINAL1.1-corrected.pdf)). Statistics Canada notes the city is "often referred to as the most multicultural city in the world" ([Statistics Canada](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/o1/en/plus/6213-lets-get-local-exploring-municipal-diversity-dashboard)).

The census numbers behind that reputation: 1.54 million Torontonians — 55.7% of the city — belonged to a visible-minority group in 2021, more than twice Montreal's 0.67 million ([Statistics Canada](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/o1/en/plus/6213-lets-get-local-exploring-municipal-diversity-dashboard)). The largest visible-minority groups were South Asian (14.0% of the city), Chinese (10.7%) and Black (9.6%), and the most-reported ethnic origins were Chinese (297,725), English (244,995) and Irish (226,865) ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/8ff2-2021-Census-Backgrounder-Immigration-Ethnoracial-Mobility-Migration-Religion-FINAL1.1-corrected.pdf)). By religion, 46.2% identified as Christian and 30.6% reported no religion or secular perspectives, with 9.6% Muslim, 6.2% Hindu and 3.6% Jewish ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/8ff2-2021-Census-Backgrounder-Immigration-Ethnoracial-Mobility-Migration-Religion-FINAL1.1-corrected.pdf)). The population figure itself carries a caveat worth stating plainly: the census counted 2,794,356 people, but Statistics Canada's undercoverage-adjusted estimate for July 1, 2021 was 2,974,293 — the gap is undercounting, not growth, and this profile uses the census count throughout ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/92e3-City-Planning-2021-Census-Backgrounder-Population-Dwellings-Backgrounder.pdf)).

## Canada's financial centre — and its largest tech hub

Toronto is Canada's largest local economy, generating more than $180 billion in GDP as part of a region generating $358 billion — 50% of Ontario's and 20% of Canada's economic output ([Canadian Urban Institute](https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2024/ec/bgrd/backgroundfile-249433.pdf)). The implication is direct: a city holding 7.6% of Canada's people produces about a fifth of its economic output ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/92e3-City-Planning-2021-Census-Backgrounder-Population-Dwellings-Backgrounder.pdf); [Canadian Urban Institute](https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2024/ec/bgrd/backgroundfile-249433.pdf)).

Finance is the anchor. With nearly 210,000 workers — three-quarters of the entire region's financial-services workforce — Toronto is the largest financial centre in Canada, headquarters to the country's largest financial institutions, and the banking and stock exchange centre of Canada ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/business-economy/industry-sector-support/financial-services/)). The Toronto Stock Exchange is the ninth-largest stock exchange in the world by market capitalization and has more mining and oil and gas companies listed than any other exchange ([City of Toronto](https://www.toronto.ca/business-economy/industry-sector-support/financial-services/)). Alongside finance, the city's tech sector is recognized as the largest tech hub in Canada and the third largest in North America, with substantial recent job growth in software development, artificial intelligence and digital innovation ([Canadian Urban Institute](https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2024/ec/bgrd/backgroundfile-249433.pdf)).

## Toronto and Blue J: a legal-tech anchor

Toronto's tech story has a legal chapter, and it is the reason this profile exists in this batch. Blue J — the legal-technology company behind this batch's source article — was founded in 2015 and is headquartered in Toronto ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/companies/blue-j-legal/)). It originated from the collaboration of [University of Toronto](/universities/university-of-toronto/) law professors [Benjamin Alarie](/people/benjamin-alarie/), [Anthony Niblett](/people/anthony-niblett/) and [Albert Yoon](/people/albert-yoon/), together with software engineer Brett Janssen, and it builds AI-powered tax research and analysis platforms for tax and legal professionals, its flagship product being "Ask Blue J" ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/companies/blue-j-legal/)). In August 2025 the company raised a $122 million Series D funding round ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/companies/blue-j-legal/)). Blue J's Toronto base is therefore not incidental: the company grew out of the city's university legal scholarship and its AI research strength, and it remains headquartered in the city where it was founded.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Toronto the capital of Canada?**
No. Ottawa is Canada's capital. Toronto is the capital of the province of Ontario, a role it has held since Confederation in 1867.

**When was Toronto founded?**
The town was founded as York in 1793 by Lieutenant-Governor John Graves Simcoe, and it was incorporated as the City of Toronto on March 6, 1834, with its former name restored.

**How many people live in Toronto?**
The 2021 census counted 2,794,356 people in the city and 6,202,225 in the metropolitan area, making Toronto Canada's most populous city.

**Why is Toronto called Canada's financial centre?**
Because it hosts nearly 210,000 financial-services workers, the headquarters of the country's largest financial institutions, and the Toronto Stock Exchange — the ninth-largest stock exchange in the world by market capitalization.

**What is Blue J's connection to Toronto?**
Blue J is headquartered in Toronto, where it was founded in 2015 by University of Toronto law professors and a software engineer. It builds AI-powered tax research tools, and it raised a $122 million Series D round in August 2025.
