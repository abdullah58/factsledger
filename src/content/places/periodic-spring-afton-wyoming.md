## Overview

Periodic Spring — also widely called the Intermittent Spring — is a rhythmic spring in Swift Creek Canyon,
roughly four to five miles east of Afton, Wyoming, in Star Valley
([Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2024/05/04/why-the-heck-does-wyomings-rare-periodic-spring-turn-itself-on-and-off/);
[Wyoming Water Research Center](http://library.wrds.uwyo.edu/RB/RB91-01.html)). It is reported to be the
world's largest known spring of its kind — a cold-water spring whose discharge switches on and off at
fairly regular intervals rather than flowing steadily
([Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2024/05/04/why-the-heck-does-wyomings-rare-periodic-spring-turn-itself-on-and-off/)).
It was discovered in August 1894 by Bruce Gardner, the great-great-grandfather of Olympic wrestler Rulon
Gardner, while he was logging in the canyon
([Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2024/05/04/why-the-heck-does-wyomings-rare-periodic-spring-turn-itself-on-and-off/)).
The spring sits on Forest Service land within the Bridger-Teton National Forest
([Travel Wyoming](http://travelwyoming.com/blog/stories/post/15-family-friendly-hikes-in-wyoming/)), and it has
doubled as Afton's municipal water supply since 1958
([Wyoming Water Research Center](http://library.wrds.uwyo.edu/RB/RB91-01.html)).

## The siphon mechanism

The leading explanation for the on-off cycling is a natural siphon. Geologist William W. Rubey proposed the
model in 1972: a cave in the Madison Formation is connected to the spring orifice by a siphon-shaped
passage, and during low flow the cave fills with groundwater until water spills over the siphon's high
point, driving out the air and starting a siphon that drains the chamber; when the water level falls below
the siphon's intake, air rushes in and breaks the siphon, and the remaining water drains away through
cracks and talus until the cave refills and the cycle repeats
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)). Shreve and Rubey later built a physical
model of the system, and the treatment follows Peter Huntoon and James Coogan's 1987 paper, "The Strange
Hydrodynamics of Periodic Spring, Salt River Range, Wyoming"
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)).

University of Utah hydrologist Kip Solomon has said of the siphon account, "We can't think of another
explanation at the moment," and University of Utah studies in the mid-2000s found no gas content in the
spring's water — a result Solomon described as strong support for the siphon theory, since it shows the
water was exposed to air underground rather than driven by dissolved gas
([Cowboy State Daily](https://cowboystatedaily.com/2024/05/04/why-the-heck-does-wyomings-rare-periodic-spring-turn-itself-on-and-off/)).
That finding distinguishes the spring from cold-water geysers, which are driven by gas; the spring is
better understood as a non-geothermal intermittent spring
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)). The [Wyoming Water Research Center](https://factsledger.com/organizations/wyoming-water-research-center)'s
studies independently point the same way: water chemistry and temperature fluctuations indicate a karstic
conduit system in the Madison limestone, with some spring snowmelt moving completely through the system in
a matter of weeks — something the researchers say can only occur in a karstic system
([Wyoming Water Research Center](http://library.wrds.uwyo.edu/RB/RB91-01.html)).

## Geology and seasonal flow

The spring discharges from the upper part of the Mississippian Madison Formation, a regional aquifer of
limestones and dolomites whose dissolution has produced caves and solution-widened fractures
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)). Recharge comes from Madison outcrops
along a fold called the Periodic Anticline near the spring and from larger outcrops about four miles east,
with the Divide Anticline the probable major source because of its outcrop area, elevation head and
snowpack ([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)). The research center's sampling
found the discharge swinging from a wintertime low of about 5 cubic feet per second to a summertime peak
of up to 100 cubic feet per second, peaking between late June and mid-July, while releases from aquifer
storage keep the spring flowing year round
([Wyoming Water Research Center](http://library.wrds.uwyo.edu/RB/RB91-01.html)).

The intermittency visitors see is seasonal. From June through August the spring flows continuously at
roughly 65 million gallons per day, as snowmelt overwhelms the siphon; from late August through November
it runs about 15 minutes on and 15 minutes off, discharging about 13 million gallons per day; from
December through May it flows about 4 minutes on and 26 minutes off at about 1.8 million gallons per day
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)). The water is coldest — about 38°F —
during the June–July peak, when dissolved solids are lowest at roughly 80 parts per million, and slightly
warmer at about 43°F with roughly 120 parts per million in fall and spring
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)).

## A working water supply

The spring's orifice is no longer natural. In the late 1950s the town of Afton tapped the spring for a
water supply that needed no filtration or treatment; the small cave at the orifice was filled with blocks
of talus and capped with a cement intake structure, and the water is piped to town
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)). The University of Wyoming research
brief confirms the spring has served as Afton's water supply since 1958 and warns that contamination in
the recharge area — uplands about six miles east of the spring — would reach the spring in a matter of
weeks through the rapid conduit flow, while aquifer storage could keep the system from flushing clean for
years ([Wyoming Water Research Center](http://library.wrds.uwyo.edu/RB/RB91-01.html)). During the fall
viewing season the city collects roughly 7 of the spring's 13 million daily gallons, so a visitor watching
the spring "come back to life" is seeing about half of its total volume run down the creek
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)).

## Visiting the spring

The trail is an easy draw: about 1.5 miles round trip with roughly 209 feet of elevation gain on Forest
Service land, though the final section up to the spring's mouth is steeper
([Travel Wyoming](http://travelwyoming.com/blog/stories/post/15-family-friendly-hikes-in-wyoming/);
[Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)). The canyon road's first mile is paved
and the next four are unpaved, and the road gate closes for winter on December 1 and reopens May 1
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html)). Late August through November is the
best window to see the on-off cycling, when regional water tables are at their lowest
([Geology of Wyoming](https://www.geowyo.com/periodic-spring.html));
[Travel Wyoming](http://travelwyoming.com/blog/stories/post/15-family-friendly-hikes-in-wyoming/) and local
media likewise point to late summer as the most noticeable season
([My Country 95.5](http://mycountry955.com/did-you-know-wyoming-has-a-spring-that-breathes-video/)). The
spring's habit of stopping and restarting has earned it the nickname "the spring that breathes"
([My Country 95.5](http://mycountry955.com/did-you-know-wyoming-has-a-spring-that-breathes-video/);
[The Jerker](https://thejerker.com/intermittent-spring-a-geological-miracle-of-wyomings/)). Atlas Obscura
notes that the spring is said to flow for 18 minutes and stop for 18 minutes, and names the Gihon Spring
in Jerusalem as another of the few comparable rhythmic springs reported worldwide
([Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/14423)).

## What this profile does not claim

No precise count of comparable rhythmic springs worldwide is stated here: Atlas Obscura and Wyoming local
media say there are three, while Cowboy State Daily says "only a handful," and no primary inventory was
found to settle the figure. The "world's largest known rhythmic spring" ranking is widely repeated in
secondary reporting, but the specialist geology treatment hedges it as "purportedly" the largest, and no
primary measurement basis for the ranking was found. Cycle times are seasonal rather than fixed — roughly
15 minutes on and off in the fall, 4 on and 26 off in winter, with general tourism coverage reporting
18–20 minute cycles — so no single interval is asserted. Discovery details beyond Cowboy State Daily's
2024 account were not corroborated by a second source. A September 2022 Wyoming Business Report article on
Afton's water-supply concerns was found but sat behind a paywall and could not be read, so it was not
used. The spring's current flow regime beyond the May 2024 feature could not be confirmed from
independently accessible sources.
