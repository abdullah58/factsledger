## Overview

England is the predominant constituent unit of the [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/), occupying more than half of the island of Great Britain, with 58.8 million people and [London](/places/london/) as its capital ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England); [Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/populationestimates/bulletins/annualmidyearpopulationestimates/mid2025)). Unlike Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, which all have varying degrees of self-government, England no longer officially exists as a governmental or political unit: it has no parliament or government of its own, and is governed directly by the [UK Parliament](/organizations/uk-parliament/) and UK Government at Westminster ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England); [UK Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691afaec84a267da57d705ab/Devolution_factsheet.pdf)).

England is bounded on the north by Scotland; on the west by the Irish Sea, Wales and the Atlantic Ocean; on the south by the English Channel; and on the east by the North Sea ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)). It covers 130,278 square km, and no place in England is more than 75 miles (120 km) from the sea ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)). Formed from the union of small Celtic and Anglo-Saxon kingdoms in the early medieval period, it has long comprised several distinct regions — Yorkshire, the West Country, the Midlands among them — each different in dialect, economy, religion and disposition ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)).

## A kingdom made, absorbed and never quite replaced

England's creation has a date that most people have never heard of. In 927, Æthelstan acquired authority over the Viking stronghold at York and, in bringing Northumbria within his dominion, became the first to rule over an area recognisable as England ([ScienceDaily](http://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/09/250924012246.htm)). He had been crowned in 925, and in 937 he crushed a formidable Viking coalition, supported by Scots and the Strathclyde Welsh, at the Battle of Brunanburh ([ScienceDaily](http://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/09/250924012246.htm)). The case for calling him England's first king is not undisputed: historians have tended to dismiss his status on the grounds that the kingdom fragmented soon after his death in 939, and the focus has instead shifted to Edgar ([ScienceDaily](http://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/09/250924012246.htm)). But the counter-argument, made by the Cambridge historian David Woodman, is that a later breakdown does not undo a creation: Æthelstan's ways of governing and legislating continued to shape kingship for generations afterwards ([ScienceDaily](http://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/09/250924012246.htm)).

The next great rupture came in 1066. The Norman Conquest was the last military conquest of England, and the Tower of London — whose White Tower William the Conqueror built that year — stands as its symbol, representing the conquest's far-reaching significance for closer ties with Europe, for the English language and culture, and for creating one of the most powerful monarchies in Europe ([UNESCO](http://whc.unesco.org/en/list/488)). In 1215, rebel barons forced King John to grant Magna Carta, which dealt with grievances against his rule — his extortionate taxes, arbitrary conduct and denial of justice — and put into writing the principle that the king and his government were not above the law ([UK Parliament](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/2015-parliament-in-the-making/get-involved1/2015-banners-exhibition/ruth-ewan/1215-magna-carta-gallery/)). John soon renounced it, yet it was confirmed in shorter form by his son Henry III and written into law by his grandson Edward I, and it became one of the most important legal texts in the English-speaking world ([UK Parliament](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/2015-parliament-in-the-making/get-involved1/2015-banners-exhibition/ruth-ewan/1215-magna-carta-gallery/)). In 1707 the Act of Union joined England with Scotland, and England has seemingly been absorbed within the larger mass of Great Britain ever since ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)).

## Governed from Westminster, with no parliament of its own

Across the UK there are four law-making bodies and four executive bodies, and certain powers are devolved from the UK Parliament and Government to those in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland ([UK Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691afaec84a267da57d705ab/Devolution_factsheet.pdf)). England is the exception: the UK Parliament is sovereign and can make laws on any issue, and there is no English parliament to receive devolved law-making powers ([UK Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691afaec84a267da57d705ab/Devolution_factsheet.pdf)). The current form of devolution dates to the late 1990s, when voters chose to create a Scottish Parliament and the Senedd Cymru in 1997, and devolution in Northern Ireland became a key element of the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement, supported in a 1998 referendum ([UK Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691afaec84a267da57d705ab/Devolution_factsheet.pdf)).

What England has instead is devolution without law-making. The UK Government started to devolve powers within England in 2000 through the transfer of powers, budgets and responsibilities to mayors and through city deals, beginning with the establishment of the Greater London Authority ([UK Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691afaec84a267da57d705ab/Devolution_factsheet.pdf)). The main difference, the Cabinet Office states, is that English devolution does not involve law-making or tax-raising powers ([UK Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691afaec84a267da57d705ab/Devolution_factsheet.pdf)). Intergovernmental work runs through the Council of the Nations and Regions, which brings together the Prime Minister, the First Ministers of Scotland and Wales, the First and deputy First Minister of Northern Ireland, and the Mayor of London and the mayors of combined and combined county authorities ([UK Cabinet Office](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/691afaec84a267da57d705ab/Devolution_factsheet.pdf)). It is rare for institutions to operate for England alone; the notable exceptions are the Church of England and the sports associations for cricket, rugby and football ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)).

## People, landscape and economy

England's population reached an estimated 58,834,800 in mid-2025, up 227,600 (+0.39%) on the year before ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/populationestimates/bulletins/annualmidyearpopulationestimates/mid2025)). Net international migration was the largest contributor to that change, and the population is ageing: the median age was 40.6 years, the number of people aged 65 and over rose 2.0%, and the number aged 15 and under fell 0.9% ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/populationestimates/bulletins/annualmidyearpopulationestimates/mid2025)). The old-age-dependency ratio — the number of people of pensionable age for every 1,000 people of working age — stood at 280 for England ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/populationestimates/bulletins/annualmidyearpopulationestimates/mid2025)). Against the UK total of 69,483,900, England holds about 85% of the UK's people, a share obtained by dividing the two ONS mid-2025 estimates ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/populationestimates/bulletins/annualmidyearpopulationestimates/mid2025)).

The landscape is low in elevation but, except in the east, rarely flat, consisting largely of rolling hillsides with the highest ground in the north, north-west and south-west ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)). The Pennines form the spine of northern England, rising to 2,930 feet (893 metres) at Cross Fell, while the Cumbrian Mountains, which include the Lake District, reach 3,210 feet (978 metres) at Scafell Pike, the highest point in England ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)). The coastline, with its many indentations, runs some 2,000 miles (3,200 km), from the granite cliffs of Land's End in the extreme south-west to the white chalk from the Isle of Wight to Dover ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)). Laced by great rivers and small streams, England is a fertile land whose soil has supported a thriving agricultural economy for millennia ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)).

In the early 19th century, England became the epicentre of a worldwide Industrial Revolution and soon the world's most industrialised country ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)). Cities such as Manchester, Birmingham and Liverpool converted raw materials drawn from every settled continent into manufactured goods for a global market, while London emerged as one of the world's pre-eminent cities ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)). The modern economy remains the UK's engine: in 2024 England's GDP was £2,472,078 million, with GDP per head of £42,171 and real GDP growth of 1.3% ([Office for National Statistics](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/grossdomesticproductgdp/bulletins/regionaleconomicactivitybygrossdomesticproductuk/latest)). London's metropolitan area continues to serve as the financial centre of Europe and a centre of innovation, particularly in popular culture ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)). That culture reaches from the plays of William Shakespeare to the music of the Sex Pistols, and the novelist George Orwell — the "revolutionary patriot" who chronicled politics and society in the 1930s and 1940s — is quoted by Britannica as capturing the essence of Englishness as well as anyone ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/England)).

England's transport network still carries the consequences of single points of failure. In February 2008, a derailment at [Barrow upon Soar](/places/barrow-upon-soar/) in Leicestershire — the 06:13 [Nottingham](/places/nottingham/) to Norwich train striking debris from a footbridge demolished by a tipper lorry's raised body — blocked the [Midland Main Line](/places/midland-main-line/) and forced London services to terminate at Leicester ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar); [Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/News-Robot/Train-strikes-footbridge)). The episode is covered in full in the profile of the [Barrow upon Soar rail crash](/events/barrow-upon-soar-rail-crash/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is England a country?**
England is a constituent country of the United Kingdom, but it no longer officially exists as a governmental or political unit. It has no parliament or government of its own, unlike Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, which have varying degrees of self-government. England is governed directly by the UK Parliament and UK Government at Westminster.

**Why does England have no parliament of its own?**
Because devolution was designed for Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland only. England is governed by the sovereign UK Parliament, and English devolution — mayors and combined authorities, beginning with the Greater London Authority in 2000 — involves no law-making or tax-raising powers. No English parliament has been created.

**What is England's population?**
An estimated 58,834,800 in mid-2025, up 227,600 (+0.39%) on the year before, according to the Office for National Statistics. That is about 85% of the UK total of 69,483,900. Net international migration was the largest contributor to the increase, and the median age was 40.6 years.

**What is England's highest point?**
Scafell Pike in the Cumbrian Mountains, in the Lake District, at 3,210 feet (978 metres). The Pennines, the spine of northern England, rise to 2,930 feet (893 metres) at Cross Fell. England's coastline, with its many indentations, runs some 2,000 miles from Land's End to Dover.
