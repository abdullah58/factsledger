## Overview

Tallinn Old Town is the exceptionally complete and well-preserved medieval centre of [Tallinn](/places/tallinn/), the capital of [Estonia](/places/estonia/), inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 1997 ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). It is a medieval northern European trading city on the coast of the Baltic Sea that developed as a significant centre of the Hanseatic League during the 13th–16th centuries, and it survives with its street plan, building plots, and defences largely intact ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)).

The Old Town has two parts that have coexisted since the 13th century: the upper town of Toompea, on a high limestone hill with the castle and the cathedral, which has always been the administrative centre of the country, and the lower town at its foot, which preserves the medieval urban fabric — narrow winding streets, many retaining their medieval names, and fine public and burgher buildings ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). The distribution of building plots survives virtually intact from the 13th–14th centuries ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)).

## A Hanseatic trading city

The Old Town's story is the story of northern European trade. The Danes captured Tallinn in 1219 and built a fortress on Toompea hill, and trade flourished after the city joined the Hanseatic League in 1285; in 1346 it was sold to the Teutonic Knights ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). UNESCO describes the city as one of the most remote and powerful outposts of the Hanseatic League's colonising activities in north-eastern Europe in the 13th–16th centuries, a crucible in which an international secular-ecclesiastical culture formed from the interchange of Cistercians, Dominicans, the Teutonic Order, and Hanseatic traditions — and was itself exported throughout northern Europe ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). That is the basis for its inscription under criterion (ii), while criterion (iv) recognises the town plan and buildings as a remarkable reflection of a feudal seat and a Hanseatic trading centre coexisting within a common system of walls and fortifications ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)).

The buildings that carry that history are still standing. Surviving or restored relics include the 13th-century Toom Church, the Gothic Oleviste (St. Olaf's) and Niguliste (St. Nicholas's) churches, the Great Guildhall of 1410, the Town Hall completed in 1404, and much of the old castle ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). The lower town's town wall, Town Hall, pharmacy, churches, monasteries, merchants' and craftsmen's guilds, and the domestic architecture of the merchants' houses have survived to a remarkable degree ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). The town defences have been preserved over large sections at their original length and height, rising to over 15 metres in places ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). The combination of the upper town and the lower town, with its many church spires, forms an expressive skyline visible from a great distance both from land and sea ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)).

## Protection, expansion, and a living city

Protection came early, by Soviet standards. The Tallinn Old Town conservation area was established in 1966 by Regulation No. 360 of the Council of Ministers of the Estonian Soviet Socialist Republic, and confirmed in 1996 by the Ministry of Culture of the Republic of Estonia — the first conservation area established in the former [USSR](/places/soviet-union/) ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). Management is now governed by the Statutes of the Heritage Conservation Area under the Heritage Conservation Act of 2002 (amended 2011), with responsibility shared between the National Heritage Board at state level and the Tallinn City Government at municipal level ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). A Tallinn Old Town Management Committee was established in 2010 to strengthen coordination among responsible organisations, NGOs, the local community, and other stakeholders ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)).

The inscribed property itself grew in 2008, when its boundaries were modified to match the conservation area: the property increased from 60 to 113 hectares, now encompassing the upper town, the lower town inside the medieval walls, the 17th-century fortifications, and a range of primarily 19th-century structures, streetscapes, and views that form a green area around the medieval city ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). The buffer zone grew from 370 to 2,253 hectares, extended to the sea to include views from the Viimsi and Kopli peninsulas, and now includes 9 view sectors and 5 view corridors ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)).

Crucially, the Old Town is not a museum piece. UNESCO notes it has retained its traditional use as a living city, hosting domestic, commercial, and religious functions, with the upper town still the country's administrative centre ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). That living use creates its main tension: historic residential buildings are increasingly refurbished for touristic or public use, and the characteristic skyline could be vulnerable to planned high-rise development outside the buffer zone, partly addressed by a thematic plan on high-rise buildings adopted by Tallinn City Council in 2008 ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](http://whc.unesco.org/pg.cfm?cid=31&id_site=822)). Among its present-day tenants is the [Banned Books Museum](/organizations/banned-books-museum/), which opened on 5 December 2020 at Munga 5 in the Old Town as part museum, part bookstore for banned, censored, restricted, or burned books ([Book Riot](https://bookriot.com/banned-books-museum/)).
