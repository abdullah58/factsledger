## Overview

The Silk Road was an ancient network of trade routes linking China with the Mediterranean world, carrying silk, spices, gems and furs east and west while also spreading art, religion and technology ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)). It was not a single road but a shifting web of overland and sea routes that evolved over centuries ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)). The Chang'an–Tianshan corridor, a 5,000 km eastern section of the network, was inscribed on [UNESCO](/organizations/unesco/)'s World Heritage List in 2014 ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)).

UNESCO describes the network as linking the ancient societies of Asia, the Subcontinent, Central Asia, Western Asia and the Near East, stretching about 7,500 km east to west but covering over 35,000 km along all its many branch routes ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)). Britannica gives a simpler figure, a 4,000-mile road originating in China that facilitated the exchange of goods and ideas between the Roman and Chinese civilizations ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/question/What-is-the-significance-of-the-Silk-Road-in-Central-Asian-history)). The political, social and cultural impacts of the movements along the routes had far-reaching consequences for every society they touched ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)).

## Routes: a network, not a single road

The overland system ran through a chain of trading posts, or emporia, that enabled the exchange, distribution and storage of goods ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](http://www.metmuseum.org/TOAH/hd/silk/hd_silk.htm)). From the metropolis of Antioch, routes crossed the Syrian Desert via Palmyra to Ctesiphon and Seleucia on the Tigris, then east across the Zagros Mountains to Ecbatana and Merv, where one branch turned north via Bukhara and Ferghana into Mongolia and another led into Bactria ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](http://www.metmuseum.org/TOAH/hd/silk/hd_silk.htm)). Other overland routes ended at Mediterranean ports, from which merchandise was distributed to cities throughout the Roman empire ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](http://www.metmuseum.org/TOAH/hd/silk/hd_silk.htm)). Sea routes linked the incense ports of South Arabia and Somalia with ports in the Persian Gulf and [India](/places/india/) in the east, and with Red Sea ports from which goods went overland to the Nile and then to Alexandria ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](http://www.metmuseum.org/TOAH/hd/silk/hd_silk.htm)). Land transport used river craft and pack animals, notably the sturdy Bactrian camel, while sea travel depended on the Indian Ocean monsoons ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](http://www.metmuseum.org/TOAH/hd/silk/hd_silk.htm)).

UNESCO traces a further main branch through [Iran](/places/iran/): it enters from the eastern border with Turkmenistan, connects Merv to Neyshabur, then goes westward to Rey, now called Tehran, from where a southern branch goes to Iraq and Syria and a northern one heads northwest to [Turkey](/places/turkey/) and Constantinople ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)). The four focal points of the Iranian section are Neyshabur, Tehran (Rey), Hamadan and Tabriz, all of which were capitals of Iran in different periods ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)). Turkey, as a vital crossroads of many important routes, is one of the most critical areas of interaction of the Silk Roads, and UNESCO states that without Turkey it is not possible to fully understand cultural, social, economic and commercial relations between Asia and Europe on the Silk Roads ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)).

## What travelled: goods, faiths and technologies

Silk was the most famous cargo, but the network carried far more: UNESCO lists silk, spices, gems and furs as cargo, alongside shared art, religion and technology ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)). By the end of the first century B.C., a great expansion of international trade involved five contiguous powers — the Roman empire, the Parthian empire, the Kushan empire, the nomadic confederation of the Xiongnu, and the Han empire — and valuable goods moved over long distances through trade, exchange, gift giving and the payment of tribute ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](http://www.metmuseum.org/TOAH/hd/silk/hd_silk.htm)). Although travel was arduous and knowledge of geography imperfect, numerous contacts were forged as these empires expanded, spreading ideas, beliefs and customs among heterogeneous peoples ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](http://www.metmuseum.org/TOAH/hd/silk/hd_silk.htm)). The port of Spasinu Charax on the Persian Gulf was a great centre of seaborne trade, sending goods up the Tigris to Ctesiphon, up the Euphrates to Dura-Europos, and through the caravan cities of the Arabian and Syrian Desert ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](http://www.metmuseum.org/TOAH/hd/silk/hd_silk.htm)).

Beyond trade, the Silk Road fostered the spread of religions such as Nestorian Christianity and Buddhism from India, and Buddhism's interaction with shamanism, Zoroastrianism and Manichaeism in Central Asia led to changes and developments in those traditions ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/question/What-is-the-significance-of-the-Silk-Road-in-Central-Asian-history)).

## Rise, revival and decline

The corridor at the heart of the UNESCO inscription took shape between the 2nd century BC and 1st century AD and remained in use until the 16th century, linking multiple civilizations and facilitating far-reaching exchanges in trade, religious beliefs, scientific knowledge, technological innovation, cultural practices and the arts ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)). The inscribed property is a 5,000 km section stretching from Chang'an/Luoyang, the central capital of China in the Han and Tang dynasties, to the Zhetysu region of Central Asia, with thirty-three components including capital cities and palace complexes of empires and Khan kingdoms, trading settlements, Buddhist cave temples, ancient paths, posthouses, passes, beacon towers, sections of the Great Wall, fortifications, tombs and religious buildings ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/documents/179475)). The early imperial trade system was already in full expansion by the end of the first century B.C., when the five contiguous powers listed above were in contact across Eurasia ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](http://www.metmuseum.org/TOAH/hd/silk/hd_silk.htm)).

Britannica records that the Silk Road later declined due to safety concerns and the rise of Arabian power, then experienced a revival under the Mongols in the 13th and 14th centuries ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/question/What-is-the-significance-of-the-Silk-Road-in-Central-Asian-history)). The route also played a role in the spread of the bubonic plague, contributing to the Black Death pandemic in Europe ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/question/What-is-the-significance-of-the-Silk-Road-in-Central-Asian-history)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Was the Silk Road a single road?**
No. It was a shifting network of roads and pathways for trade that evolved over centuries, covering about 7,500 km east to west and over 35,000 km along all its branch routes. The network included overland caravan routes and sea routes, linked by trading posts, ports and caravan cities.

**What goods travelled along the Silk Road?**
Silk was the most famous cargo, but traders also carried spices, gems and furs, alongside art and technology. Goods moved through trade, exchange, gift giving and tribute between the Roman, Parthian, Kushan, Xiongnu and Han powers and the many peoples between them.

**Which religions spread along the Silk Road?**
The routes fostered the spread of Nestorian Christianity and Buddhism from India, among other faiths, since the network shared art, religion and technology as well as cargo. In Central Asia, Buddhism interacted with shamanism, Zoroastrianism and Manichaeism, and those encounters changed and developed the traditions involved.

**Why did the Silk Road decline?**
Britannica attributes the decline to safety concerns and the rise of Arabian power, followed by a revival under the Mongols in the 13th and 14th centuries. The same source notes the route's role in spreading bubonic plague, which contributed to the Black Death pandemic in Europe.

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not claim the Silk Road was one road with one route; the sources describe a shifting network of many branches, overland and maritime.
- It does not name a person who coined the term "Silk Road" or a date for the coining, because no source read in full verified that claim.
- It does not assert specific roles for Zhang Qian, the Sogdians, Marco Polo or other famous travellers, because those claims appeared only in search snippets, not in any source read in full.
- It does not treat the 2014 UNESCO inscription as covering the whole network; the inscription covers only the Chang'an–Tianshan Corridor and its 33 components.
- It does not reconcile the 4,000-mile figure with UNESCO's 7,500 km / 35,000 km figures; they measure different things and are reported with their sources.
- It does not claim that every branch was in use at the same time, or that the network ended on a single date.
