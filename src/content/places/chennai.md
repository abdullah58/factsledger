## Overview

Chennai, formerly Madras, is the capital and largest city of Tamil Nadu in southern [India](/places/india/), on the Coromandel Coast of the Bay of Bengal. Founded as a British East India Company trading fort in 1639–40 and renamed Chennai in 1996, it is a major administrative and cultural centre known as the "Gateway to South India" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). Its economy rests on automobiles, software and electronics, and specialty healthcare, while its 1688 municipal corporation is the oldest surviving one in India ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)).

The 2011 census counted 4,646,732 people in the city and 8,696,010 in its urban agglomeration ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). More recent accounts put the metropolitan population above 10 million, a figure the World Bank also uses when calling Chennai India's fourth-largest city ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/water/rethinking-wastewater-chennais-journey-towards-water-security)). The population is predominantly Tamil, about four-fifths Hindu, with Muslim, Christian and Jain minorities, and Tamil is the most common language, though many professionals speak English ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)).

## From Madraspatnam to Chennai

Chennai's recorded past begins long before its founding date. Pallavaram, now a southwestern neighbourhood, has been settled since the Paleolithic Period and became an important urban centre under the Pallava dynasty ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). Claims that St. Thomas was buried there drew Armenian and Portuguese merchants in the early 16th century, and the Portuguese port of San Thome was established in 1522 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). In 1639–40 the British East India Company built a fort and factory near the fishing village of Madraspatnam, from which the name Madras was shortened ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). By 1652 Fort St. George was recognised as a presidency, and between 1668 and 1749 the Company expanded its control until, by about 1801, the British were masters of southern India with Madras as its administrative and commercial capital ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). After independence Madras stayed the capital of Madras state, renamed Tamil Nadu, until the city became Chennai in 1996 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)).

## Government: the 1688 corporation

As Tamil Nadu's capital, Chennai hosts the state's executive and legislative headquarters inside Fort St. George itself, so the colonial fort remains the seat of government rather than a museum piece alone ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The municipal government, Chennai Corporation, was established in 1688 and is the oldest surviving municipal governing body in India, a distinction that predates the modern city by centuries in institutional terms ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). It is governed by a council elected to five-year terms, with a mayor chosen from among the councillors ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). That longevity matters because Chennai grew without a plan from its 17th-century core, formed by Fort St. George and the Indian quarters, with industrial areas to the north and northwest and the main residential areas to the west and south ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The corporation therefore governs a city whose shape was set by trade and settlement centuries ago, not by a master plan.

## A triple economy: cars, code and care

Since the late 1990s, software development and electronics manufacturing have made up the bulk of Chennai's economy, with numerous technology parks housing foreign companies throughout the city ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The World Bank describes the region as the nation's automotive hub, home to other manufacturing from petrochemicals to electronic hardware, textiles and apparel, and one of India's fastest-growing urban economies ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/water/rethinking-wastewater-chennais-journey-towards-water-security)). Other major industries include automobiles, rubber, fertilizer, leather, iron ore and cotton textiles, and the city has an oil refinery, while wheat, machinery, iron and steel and raw cotton are imported ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). Services, especially finance and tourism, are also significant: Indian Bank and Indian Overseas Bank, both administered by the Ministry of Finance, are headquartered in Chennai ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The third leg is healthcare, and the three together explain why a port city founded for cotton weaving now anchors factories, technology parks and hospitals at once. The back-office economy extends to financial services: at the end of 2021, 587 employees of the title insurer [Title365](/organizations/title365/) worked in Chennai ([Inman](https://www.inman.com/2022/04/21/blend-labs-laying-off-200-employees-as-mortgage-refinancings-plummet/)).

## Culture, temples and Tamil cinema

Chennai and its suburbs have more than 600 Hindu temples, and the oldest, the Parthasarathi Temple, was built in the 8th century by Pallava kings ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The 16th-century Kapaleeswarar Temple is dedicated to the Hindu god Shiva, while the city's other faiths are visible in Luz Church (1547–82), St. Mary's Church (1678–80), the first British church in India, the San Thome Basilica (1898) over the tomb of St. Thomas, and the Wallajah Mosque (1795) ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The international headquarters of the Theosophical Society sits in gardens between the Adyar River and the coast, where a banyan tree dates from about 1600 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The Madras Music Academy encourages Carnatic music, Kalakshetra is a centre of dance and music, and the city trains dancers in kuchipudi and bharata natyam, with annual dance festivals hosted by Kalakshetra and Sri Krishna Gana Sabha ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). Kodambakkam, with its many film studios, is described as the Hollywood of Tamil-language cinema ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)).

## Learning, medicine and science

Chennai has been a medical centre since the 1980s, when private specialty hospitals proliferated, especially for cardiac and eye care ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). Its leading facilities include Apollo Hospital, the Madras Medical Mission's Institute of Cardiovascular Diseases, Sri Ramachandra University Hospital, the Heart Institute of Chennai and Sankara Nethralaya, the ophthalmic hospital whose name means "Temple of the Eye" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). Education is equally old and layered: the city is the site of the University of Madras (1857), with several advanced centres of research, alongside the Indian Institute of Technology, the Central Leather Research Institute and regional laboratories of the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The M.S. Swaminathan Research Foundation focuses on agricultural development in Chennai and Tamil Nadu ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). Professional education spans state medical and veterinary colleges, engineering and technology colleges, the Tamil Nadu Isai Kalluri music college, the College of Arts and Crafts and teacher-training colleges ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)).

## Coast, rivers and climate

Chennai sits on the Coromandel Coast along the Bay of Bengal, with the Cooum River flowing through its centre and the Adyar River through its southern portion, while the Buckingham Canal runs parallel to the coast ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). Its climate is warm and humid, averaging 89°F (32°C) in May and 77°F (25°C) in January, with annual rainfall of about 50 inches (1,250 mm), mostly from October to mid-December ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). That concentrated monsoon shapes the city: hotels, resorts, restaurants, marinas and parks line Marina Beach, the coastline abutting the city ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). Chennai is well connected by road, rail, air and sea, with an international airport and seaport, and buses and auto-rickshaws are common within the city ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). About 37 miles (60 km) south lies Mamallapuram, whose Shore Temple is a popular tourist destination ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The city also has one of the highest penetrations of high-speed internet access in India and is one of a handful of cities connected to submarine fibre-optic cables ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)).

## The flood-and-drought paradox

Chennai's defining modern problem is that it floods and runs dry in turn. It experienced floods in 2005, 2010 and 2015, bookended by droughts in 2003–04 and 2016–18, and because it depends entirely on rain to recharge its water resources, monsoon failure leads to acute scarcity ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/water/rethinking-wastewater-chennais-journey-towards-water-security)). The 2015 flood killed hundreds of people and displaced many more, yet by June 2019 the city's four main reservoirs — Puzhal, Cholavaram, Chembarambakkam and Poondi — were virtually dry, with the first two completely dried up ([World Resources Institute](https://www.wri.org/insights/how-does-flood-prone-city-run-out-water-inside-chennais-day-zero-crisis)). The World Resources Institute argues this is a management problem, not only a rainfall problem: the population grew from 500,000 to more than 10 million over the last century, unplanned urbanisation and consumption worsened scarcity, and more than 80% of available water is used every year ([World Resources Institute](https://www.wri.org/insights/how-does-flood-prone-city-run-out-water-inside-chennais-day-zero-crisis)). Dumping untreated sewage into lakes is common, polluting groundwater further ([World Resources Institute](https://www.wri.org/insights/how-does-flood-prone-city-run-out-water-inside-chennais-day-zero-crisis)).

## Water security: reuse instead of new rivers

The response documented by the World Bank is to reuse water rather than import it. Chennai historically relied on rainfed lakes with combined storage of about 11,000 million cubic feet, plus groundwater supplying up to 120 million litres per day, but demand rose more than 50% over ten years ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/water/rethinking-wastewater-chennais-journey-towards-water-security)). The Chennai Metropolitan Water Supply and Sewerage Board is diversifying supply through desalination and sewage-treatment reuse, and the city mandated rainwater harvesting and became the first in India to reuse 10 percent of collected wastewater, with plans to reach 75 percent ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/water/rethinking-wastewater-chennais-journey-towards-water-security)). Between 2015 and 2019 it established two tertiary treatment plants supplying treated wastewater to industries, freeing freshwater for domestic use ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/water/rethinking-wastewater-chennais-journey-towards-water-security)). It also piloted two 10 MLD indirect potable reuse plants, planning to scale to 260 MLD by 2024, which would add about a quarter of the city's water demand ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/water/rethinking-wastewater-chennais-journey-towards-water-security)).

## Frequently asked questions

Founded as a British East India Company trading fort in 1639–40 and renamed in 1996, it is known as the "Gateway to South India" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)).

**How big is Chennai?** The 2011 census counted 4,646,732 people in the city and 8,696,010 in its urban agglomeration ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). More recent accounts, including the World Bank, describe more than 10 million people in the metropolitan area, making it India's fourth-largest city ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/water/rethinking-wastewater-chennais-journey-towards-water-security)).

**Why did Chennai run out of water in 2019?** Its four main reservoirs — Puzhal, Cholavaram, Chembarambakkam and Poondi — were virtually dry, but the World Resources Institute stresses management too: unplanned urbanisation, rising consumption and sewage pollution, with over 80% of available water used every year ([World Resources Institute](https://www.wri.org/insights/how-does-flood-prone-city-run-out-water-inside-chennais-day-zero-crisis)).

**What is Chennai's economy based on?** Since the late 1990s software development and electronics manufacturing have made up the bulk of the economy, alongside automobiles and other manufacturing, finance and tourism ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Chennai)). The World Bank calls the region the nation's automotive hub and one of India's fastest-growing urban economies ([World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/water/rethinking-wastewater-chennais-journey-towards-water-security)).
