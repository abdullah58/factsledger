## Overview

Estonia, officially the Republic of Estonia, is the northernmost of the three Baltic states, a parliamentary republic in northeastern Europe with [Tallinn](/places/tallinn/) as its capital and 1,360,745 people as of 1 January 2026 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia); [Statistics Estonia](https://stat.ee/en/news/1360745-people-estonias-population-declined-second-year-row)). It is a small country — 45,339 square kilometres, with about 1,500 islands — that has become outsized in reputation: it regained independence from the [Soviet Union](/places/soviet-union/) in 1991, joined [NATO](/organizations/nato/) and the [European Union](/organizations/european-union/) in 2004, and became the first country in the world to let all voters cast ballots online in national elections ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia); [Cambridge University Press](https://core-varnish-new.prod.aop.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4A59FC654A185D9B8C3D3BBB6B5AC793/S0960777324000031a.pdf/free_to_choose_evoting_infrastructure_and_the_origins_of_estonias_digital_republic.pdf)).

Estonia's economy reached $47 billion in 2025, with GDP per capita of $34,325, and its human development index stood at 0.905 in 2023, ranking 36th of 193 countries ([Destatis](https://www.destatis.de/EN/Themes/Countries-Regions/International-Statistics/Country-Profiles/estonia.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=15)). But the population is shrinking: it fell by 9,250 people in 2025, the second consecutive annual decline, with deaths far outnumbering births ([Statistics Estonia](https://stat.ee/en/news/1360745-people-estonias-population-declined-second-year-row)).

## Land, people, and government

Estonia juts out into the Baltic Sea, which surrounds it to the north and west; it is bounded by Russia to the east — predominantly by the Narva River and Lakes Peipus, Tyoploye, and Pskov — and by Latvia to the south ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)). Its landscape is largely the product of glacial activity, with a mean elevation of only 50 metres; the highest point is Suur Munamägi (Great Egg Hill) at 318 metres in the Haanja Upland in the southeast ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)). The longest river, the Pärnu, stretches about 145 kilometres, and the largest lake, Peipus, covers about 3,550 square kilometres and is shared with Russia ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)). Mixed forests with about 90 native tree and shrub species cover almost half the territory, Scots pine the most common, and about 60 mammal species live there, the largest the elk, with bears and lynx in the deep forests of the northeast ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)).

Only about two-thirds of the population are ethnic Estonians; Russians are the most significant minority at about one-fourth, with Ukrainians, Belarusians, and Finns also present ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)). Statistics Estonia's latest count puts ethnic Estonians at 68.5 percent (933,000 people), with 82.2 percent of residents holding Estonian citizenship, 5.5 percent Russian citizenship, and 4.6 percent Ukrainian ([Statistics Estonia](https://stat.ee/en/news/1360745-people-estonias-population-declined-second-year-row)). Estonian, a member of the Finno-Ugric branch of the Uralic family, is the official language, spoken as a first language by more than two-thirds of the population, while about one-fourth speak Russian as a first language, mostly in the northeast ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)). There is no state religion, and many people are nonreligious or atheist ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)).

The state is a unitary multiparty republic with a single legislative house, the Riigikogu, whose 101 members are directly elected through proportional representation to four-year terms ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)). The president, Alar Karis, is head of state and supreme commander of the armed forces, elected to up to two consecutive five-year terms by the Riigikogu; executive power belongs to the prime minister, Kristen Michal, nominated by the president, and the Council of Ministers ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)). The population is predominantly urban, with Tallinn and Tartu the two largest cities — though Helsinki, across the Gulf of Finland, is closer to Tallinn than Tartu is ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)).

## From first independence to the Singing Revolution

Estonia's modern history turns on two declarations of independence, seventy-three years apart. Independence was first declared on 23 February 1918, when a manifesto declaring Estonia a sovereign state was read from the balcony of the Endla Theater in Pärnu; the news reached Tallinn the next day, and 24 February is celebrated as Independence Day ([Visit Estonia](https://visitestonia.com/en/what-to-do/how-estonia-regained-its-independence)). That independence was short-lived: the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany signed the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact on 23 August 1939, placing Estonia in the Soviet sphere of influence and paving the way for annexation in 1940, followed by Nazi occupation from 1941 to 1944 and Soviet reoccupation until the late 1980s ([Visit Estonia](https://visitestonia.com/en/what-to-do/how-estonia-regained-its-independence); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)).

The return to independence was strikingly nonviolent. Events known as the Singing Revolution began in the summer of 1988, and on 11 September 1988 roughly 300,000 Estonians — almost one-third of the population — came together in Tallinn for the National Song Festival ([Visit Estonia](https://visitestonia.com/en/what-to-do/how-estonia-regained-its-independence)). A year later, on 23 August 1989, nearly two million people from Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania formed the Baltic Way, a human chain extending 670 kilometres, on the 50th anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact ([Visit Estonia](https://visitestonia.com/en/what-to-do/how-estonia-regained-its-independence)). Following the August Coup in Moscow, Estonia declared independence from the Soviet Union on 20 August 1991, when the Supreme Council established de facto independence; the Soviet Union recognised it on 6 September 1991, and UN membership followed shortly thereafter ([Visit Estonia](https://visitestonia.com/en/what-to-do/how-estonia-regained-its-independence); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)). The country then transformed its government into a parliamentary democracy, reoriented its economy toward market capitalism, and in 2004 joined NATO and the EU ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)). The cultural anchor of that national story is older than the state itself: song and dance festivals, an expression of Estonian nationalism dating from the mid-19th century, remain extremely popular, and the Song and Dance Celebration remains a linchpin of national identity ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Estonia)).

## A small, open economy — and the digital republic

Estonia's economy is small, open, and service-led. GDP reached $47 billion in 2025, up 98 percent since 2000, with GDP per capita of $34,325 and $49,492 at purchasing-power parity ([Destatis](https://www.destatis.de/EN/Themes/Countries-Regions/International-Statistics/Country-Profiles/estonia.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=15)). Services employ 70.5 percent of workers and produce 74.4 percent of gross value added, against 3.1 percent of workers in agriculture ([Destatis](https://www.destatis.de/EN/Themes/Countries-Regions/International-Statistics/Country-Profiles/estonia.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=15)). The trade picture is a deficit: exports of goods were $19.773 billion in 2024 against imports of $22.672 billion, a balance of −$2.899 billion, with Finland the largest export destination (13.9 percent) and Russia the largest import source (11.6 percent), ahead of Germany (9.3 percent) and [China](/places/china/) (8.7 percent) ([Destatis](https://www.destatis.de/EN/Themes/Countries-Regions/International-Statistics/Country-Profiles/estonia.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=15)). The stat that anchors the fiscal story is the low public debt: general government gross debt was 24.2 percent of GDP in 2025, against inflation of 4.8 percent ([Destatis](https://www.destatis.de/EN/Themes/Countries-Regions/International-Statistics/Country-Profiles/estonia.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=15)). Unemployment was 8.3 percent in 2025, but 19.7 percent among 15- to 24-year-olds ([Destatis](https://www.destatis.de/EN/Themes/Countries-Regions/International-Statistics/Country-Profiles/estonia.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=15)).

The digital republic is Estonia's signature achievement. In October 2005 it held its first local elections where voters could cast ballots entirely online, and two years later internet voting was extended to national elections, making Estonia the only country in the world where e-voting was open to all voters at the highest level ([Cambridge University Press](https://core-varnish-new.prod.aop.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4A59FC654A185D9B8C3D3BBB6B5AC793/S0960777324000031a.pdf/free_to_choose_evoting_infrastructure_and_the_origins_of_estonias_digital_republic.pdf)). In the 2023 national elections over half of all votes were cast online — 51.10 percent of participating voters ([Cambridge University Press](https://core-varnish-new.prod.aop.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4A59FC654A185D9B8C3D3BBB6B5AC793/S0960777324000031a.pdf/free_to_choose_evoting_infrastructure_and_the_origins_of_estonias_digital_republic.pdf)). The system rests on the digital ID: nearly every citizen has an ID card providing digital access to all of Estonia's e-services, and Wired magazine named Estonia the most advanced digital society in the world ([Foreign Policy Research Institute](https://www.fpri.org/article/2017/10/e-estonia-defending-digital-frontier/)). The same openness carried risk — in 2007 the country suffered debilitating cyber-attacks on public and private institutions ([Foreign Policy Research Institute](https://www.fpri.org/article/2017/10/e-estonia-defending-digital-frontier/)). The demographic counterweight is sobering: in 2025 there were 9,240 births against 15,688 deaths, net migration turned negative (−2,802) after more than ten years of positive flows, and the total fertility rate fell to 1.16 ([Statistics Estonia](https://stat.ee/en/news/1360745-people-estonias-population-declined-second-year-row)). Tallinn's [Old Town](/places/tallinn-old-town/), a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1997, anchors the country's tourism, and the [Banned Books Museum](/organizations/banned-books-museum/) has operated there since 5 December 2020 ([Book Riot](https://bookriot.com/banned-books-museum/)).
