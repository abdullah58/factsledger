## Overview

Lincoln Beach was a segregation-era beach and amusement park in New Orleans East, built as a recreational
space for the city's Black residents after a 1934 seawall built to protect white neighborhoods made the
Black-frequented Seabrook Beach dangerous, leading the city to ban swimming there in 1943. In 1938, fruit
importer Samuel Zemurray donated an unproductive orchard across the Industrial Canal to create an
alternative — though the site was initially remote, lacked public transit access, and was contaminated by
nearby sewage-dumping fishing camps, prompting health officials to recommend its closure
([Places Journal](https://placesjournal.org/article/lincoln-beach-new-orleans-black-and-indigenous-space/)).
A bathhouse and sand beach were built in 1940, with amusement rides added over time
([Old New Orleans](http://old-new-orleans.com/NO_LincolnBeach.html)). The National Register nomination
period of significance runs from 1941 to 1965, describing Lincoln Beach as the only public recreation
facility in New Orleans dedicated solely for use by African Americans in that span
([Preservation Resource Center](http://prcno.org/louisiana-adds-19-national-register-listings-and-one-historic-landmark/)).

## Segregation and the founding of the beach

The beach was born of Jim Crow. Lincoln Beach was created as the designated Black beach for residents
barred from the whites-only Pontchartrain Beach about five miles away, and its name honored Abraham
Lincoln, "the Emancipator." Its first years were bleak: the donated land was polluted and hard to reach,
and health officials recommended closing it. It took a major public investment to make the place viable
([Places Journal](https://placesjournal.org/article/lincoln-beach-new-orleans-black-and-indigenous-space/)).

## The amusement park years

Between 1951 and 1954, the Orleans Parish Levee Board transformed the site, building a new bathhouse, a
ten-acre parking lot, three pools and a restaurant, with private operators adding amusement rides. Its May
1954 dedication drew about 25,000 visitors and featured the marching band from Booker T. Washington High
School ([Places Journal](https://placesjournal.org/article/lincoln-beach-new-orleans-black-and-indigenous-space/)).
Over the following decade, Lincoln Beach became a major recreational destination for Black New Orleanians.
[Fats Domino](https://factsledger.com/people/fats-domino), Nat King Cole, Irma Thomas and Ray Charles all performed there, and a surviving 1960 poster
advertises a Fats Domino appearance ([Axios New Orleans](https://www.axios.com/local/new-orleans/2024/07/24/lincoln-beach-reopen-2025-national-register-historic-places);
[Old New Orleans](http://old-new-orleans.com/NO_LincolnBeach.html)). A local-history account also reports
that a 1954 Miss Lincoln Beach pageant held at the Municipal Auditorium had Nat King Cole acting as master
of ceremonies — a detail this profile could not independently corroborate with a second source
([Old New Orleans](http://old-new-orleans.com/NO_LincolnBeach.html)).

## Closure and decades of abandonment

The park closed abruptly in the mid-1960s. One account holds that after the Civil Rights Act's passage,
and shortly after the previously whites-only Pontchartrain Beach was integrated, the white-owned operator
invoked an escape clause in its agreement and shut the site down immediately, leaving rides including the
Ferris wheel to rust in place
([Places Journal](https://placesjournal.org/article/lincoln-beach-new-orleans-black-and-indigenous-space/)).
But the closing year itself is disputed: that account and a local-history site say 1964, while the
Historic New Orleans Collection describes the site as closed to the public since 1965, and the National
Register nomination's period of significance runs through 1965
([The Historic New Orleans Collection](https://my.hnoc.org/16804/16805);
[Preservation Resource Center](http://prcno.org/louisiana-adds-19-national-register-listings-and-one-historic-landmark/)).
Decades of neglect followed — hurricanes flooded the tunnels, rides rusted out, structures were tagged
with graffiti, and the site became an overgrown ruin, though people kept visiting it informally
([Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/onsi/adventure/adventure-feed-page/activists-revitalizing-once-segregated-since-abandoned-new-orleans-beach)).

## The long road to reopening

A revival began from the ground up. During the COVID-19 pandemic, activist Michael "Sage" Pellet started
visiting the derelict 15-acre site and organizing cleanups out of what he called a responsibility to his
elders who had walked the land; he was joined by Tricia "Blyss" Wallace and artist Reggie Ford in a
grassroots campaign to get the beach reopened
([Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/onsi/adventure/adventure-feed-page/activists-revitalizing-once-segregated-since-abandoned-new-orleans-beach)).
Their lobbying worked: in 2023 New Orleans secured $24.6 million for the project, and Congressman Troy
Carter secured $4.1 million in federal funding toward the revival
([Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/onsi/adventure/adventure-feed-page/activists-revitalizing-once-segregated-since-abandoned-new-orleans-beach)).

On July 10, 2024, the [[National Park Service](/organizations/national-park-service/)](https://factsledger.com/organizations/national-park-service) formally listed Lincoln Beach on the National Register of
Historic Places — 13835 and 14000 Hayne Blvd., reference number 100009216 — under Criterion A for Ethnic
Heritage (Black), Social History and Recreation ([U.S. National Park Service](https://WWW.NPS.GOV/subjects/nationalregister/weekly-list-2024-07-12.htm);
[Preservation Resource Center](http://prcno.org/louisiana-adds-19-national-register-listings-and-one-historic-landmark/)).
Architect Mia Kaplan, a Tulane School of Architecture alumna, spearheaded the nomination effort, which
backers said would open doors to additional funding and preservation support
([Tulane School of Architecture](https://architecture.tulane.edu/news/lincoln-beach-national-register)).
The listing makes the site eligible for preservation grants, tax credits and protection from demolition
([The Historic New Orleans Collection](https://my.hnoc.org/16804/16805)).

The reopening has been slower than first promised. A planned summer 2025 reopening reported in 2024 did
not happen. In May 2025 the city released its final draft of a four-phase master plan designed by Sasaki
Associates, dropping the quick 2025 opening in favor of reopening when phase one is complete — then
estimated for late 2026 or early 2027 — with phase one covering the bridge, half the parking lot,
restrooms and lockers, contracted under a construction-manager-at-risk model
([WWNO/KRVS](https://www.krvs.org/louisiana-news/2025-05-08/i-dont-know-how-theyre-going-to-complete-it-city-releases-final-draft-of-lincoln-beach-plan-advocates-want-more-details)).
Advocates including Reggie Ford said they wanted more assurance the city could afford the plan. As of
April 2026, the city's own project page put the anticipated public reopening at summer 2027, with
construction estimated to begin by the end of 2026; the site remains closed to the public in the meantime
([City of New Orleans](https://nola.gov/next/sustainability/lincoln-beach-redevelopment-en/lincoln-beach-faqs/)).
Phase 1 is now split into three construction packages — parking and access first, then the pedestrian
bridge and utilities, then beach nourishment and shoreline protection — and includes restoring historic
features such as the signage and pavilions
([City of New Orleans](https://nola.gov/next/sustainability/lincoln-beach-redevelopment/phase-1-redevelopment/)).
Alongside the construction, the city says its plan will preserve the site's cultural history as a
gathering place for Black families during segregation, with historical markers and interpretive signage
guided by community engagement; it recognizes the Lincoln Beach Center, founded by Janet Jupiter Tobias,
as the community hub collecting the site's artifacts and oral histories
([City of New Orleans](https://nola.gov/next/sustainability/lincoln-beach-redevelopment-en/lincoln-beach-faqs/)).
