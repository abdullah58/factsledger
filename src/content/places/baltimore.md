## Overview

Baltimore is Maryland's largest city — 585,708 people in the 2020 census — an independent city at the head of the Patapsco River estuary, about 40 miles northeast of Washington, D.C. ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Baltimore)). Established in 1729 as a tobacco port, it became the shipbuilding city of the clipper era, the city whose harbor fort gave America its national anthem, and the eastern terminus of the nation's first railroad. In the [United States](/places/united-states/)' engineering story it plays a quieter role: since 1996 it has been the headquarters city of [ABET](/organizations/abet/) — the accreditor founded in [New York City](/places/new-york-city/) in 1932 — which moved here from New York ([ABET](https://www.abet.org/about-abet/history/)).

## The port that built a city

Baltimore was "created as a port for shipping tobacco and grain," named for the Irish barony of Baltimore, seat of the Calvert family who held Maryland as proprietors ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Baltimore)). Local waterways were soon harnessed for flour milling, and by the Revolution it was "a bustling seaport and shipbuilding centre": Baltimore clippers plied the seas, trade extended to the Caribbean, and the U.S. Navy's first ship — the *Constellation* — was launched here in 1797. The Continental Congress itself met in Baltimore from December 1776 to March 1777, when a British attack on Philadelphia seemed imminent.

The city's signature moment came September 13–14, 1814, when British forces tried to capture Baltimore during the War of 1812 and the harbor's Fort McHenry held. Watching the bombardment, Francis Scott Key wrote the poem that became "The Star-Spangled Banner." Thirteen years later the city made transportation history again: the Baltimore and Ohio, the nation's first railroad (1827), with its eastern terminus at Mount Clare Station — preserved today as a railroad museum.

The Civil War split the city's sympathies though Maryland never seceded; Union troops occupied Baltimore throughout, and recovery was slow. A fire on February 7, 1904, razed most of the business district — recovery, this time, was rapid — and World War I brought steelworks, oil refineries, and war industries. In the 1920s and '30s the essayist H.L. Mencken and his *Sun* circle gave the city an intellectual aura; after postwar decay, the downtown and waterfront were substantially renovated.

## The contemporary city

Baltimore is now "a major seaport with ship-repair facilities and a highly diversified economy" — health care, education, finance, and insurance, with the Federal Social Security Administration headquartered here ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Baltimore)). It sits on the Northeast Corridor rail line; Baltimore/Washington International Airport serves both cities. The harbor tunnels and the Francis Scott Key Bridge (1977) cross the Patapsco — the bridge collapsed on March 26, 2024, after being struck by a ship.

It is also a university city: Johns Hopkins (1876), the Peabody Institute conservatory (1857), the University of Maryland's Baltimore campus (1807), Towson (1866), Morgan State (1867), Loyola Maryland (1852), and more. The Inner Harbor, revitalized in the 1980s, holds the National Aquarium; the city's teams are the Orioles and the Ravens; and near Oriole Park at Camden Yards stand both Babe Ruth's birthplace and the grave of Edgar Allan Poe.

## The engineering thread

Baltimore's place in this site's engineering graph is institutional. When ABET outgrew its New York home, it chose Baltimore: the accreditor has been headquartered at 415 North Charles St. since 1996 ([ABET](https://www.abet.org/about-abet/history/)). The city's electrical-engineering lineage is older still — the Baltimore Section of the [American Institute of Electrical Engineers](/organizations/aiee/) was founded December 16, 1904, becoming the [IEEE](/organizations/ieee/) Baltimore Section when the national bodies merged in January 1963 ([IEEE Baltimore Section](https://site.ieee.org/baltimore/section-history/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is Baltimore called an independent city?**
It separated from Baltimore County in 1851 and is the only Maryland city not located within a county — it functions as both city and county equivalent.

**What is Baltimore known for?**
Its 1729 port origins and clipper-ship era; Fort McHenry and "The Star-Spangled Banner" (1814); the B&O, America's first railroad (1827); Johns Hopkins University; the Inner Harbor; and the Orioles and Ravens.

**What is Baltimore's connection to ABET?**
ABET — the engineering-program accreditor founded in New York City in 1932 — relocated its headquarters to Baltimore, Maryland, in 1996, where it remains.

**When was Baltimore founded?**
Established as a town in 1729 (named for the Irish barony of the Calvert family, Maryland's proprietors) and incorporated as a city in 1796.

**What happened to the Francis Scott Key Bridge?**
It collapsed on March 26, 2024, after being struck by a ship, per Britannica's updated account.
