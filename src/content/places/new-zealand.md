## Overview

New Zealand is an island country in the South Pacific Ocean, the southwesternmost part of Polynesia, lying more than 1,600 km southeast of [Australia](/places/australia/), its nearest neighbour ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Zealand)). Its capital is Wellington and its largest urban area is Auckland, both on the North Island, and about 5.5 million people live in the country (2026 estimate). A constitutional monarchy founded on the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi between the British Crown and Māori chiefs, it is known for its Māori culture and landscapes — and, since 2019, for how fast it changed its gun laws after the [Christchurch mosque shootings](/events/christchurch-mosque-shootings/), in which 51 people were killed.

## Founding: the Treaty of Waitangi

New Zealand's modern history begins with Te Tiriti o Waitangi (the Treaty of Waitangi), the country's founding document — a written agreement made in 1840 between the British Crown and more than 500 Māori chiefs, after which New Zealand became a colony of Britain and Māori became British subjects ([Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/te-tiriti-o-waitangi-the-treaty-of-waitangi)). The treaty was drafted in English, translated into Māori by missionary Henry Williams and his son Edward, presented to around 500 Māori at Waitangi on 5 February 1840, and signed by more than 40 chiefs the next day; most Māori signed the te reo (Māori-language) version. The two versions did not mean the same thing: in Māori, Article One gave Queen Victoria governance (kāwanatanga) over the land, while in English it gave her sovereignty — a stronger term — and Article Two guaranteed chiefs te tino rangatiratanga, chieftainship over their lands, villages and treasured things ([Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/te-tiriti-o-waitangi-the-treaty-of-waitangi)). Those different understandings, and breaches of the treaty, caused conflict: the government often ignored the protections te Tiriti was supposed to give, land was confiscated from several North Island iwi, and by the end of the 19th century most land was no longer in Māori ownership ([Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/te-tiriti-o-waitangi-the-treaty-of-waitangi)). The Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975 established the Waitangi Tribunal to hear claims that the government had breached te Tiriti, and from the 1970s efforts to honour the treaty and its principles expanded.

## Government, land and economy

New Zealand was the largest country in Polynesia when it was annexed by Great Britain in 1840, and was successively a crown colony, a self-governing colony from 1856, and a dominion from 1907; by the 1920s it controlled almost all of its internal and external policies, and it became fully independent in 1947 when it adopted the Statute of Westminster ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Zealand)). It remains a member of the Commonwealth, a constitutional monarchy whose head of state is King Charles III, represented by Governor-General Dame Cindy Kiro, and whose head of government is Prime Minister Christopher Luxon, with a single legislative house, the 120-seat House of Representatives ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/facts/New-Zealand)). Its official languages are English, Māori and New Zealand Sign Language, and its official name in Māori is Aotearoa ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/facts/New-Zealand)). The country comprises the North and South Islands and smaller islands, is about 1,600 km long north to south, and covers 265,700 square kilometres — slightly less than the US state of Colorado and a little more than the United Kingdom ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Zealand)). Economically it was long dependent on agricultural exports, especially to Great Britain; after Britain entered the European Community in the early 1970s it expanded its trade relations and developed a more varied industrial sector, and tourism has played an increasingly important role ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Zealand)). New Zealand was also the first country to allow women to vote, in 1893 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/facts/New-Zealand)).

## Christchurch and the gun-law reform

On Friday 15 March 2019, a gunman attacked two Christchurch mosques — Al Noor Mosque, where about 500 people had gathered for prayer, and the Linwood Islamic Centre — livestreaming the attack on Facebook Live; 51 people were killed and 50 more injured ([Psychiatry, Psychology and Law](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8547820/)). The paper's authors describe it as New Zealand's first experience of terrorism, and it was the country's only mass shooting in the January 2004–March 2019 period. The attack landed in a country with an unusual gun profile: high civilian ownership — about 1.2 million firearms, 26 per 100 people, the 17th-highest rate in the world — but low gun violence, about 1.2 gun deaths per 100,000 people per year, and before the attack New Zealand was one of only three countries that had not mandated registration of most firearms other than handguns ([Psychiatry, Psychology and Law](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8547820/)).

Within weeks, new gun control laws were introduced with bipartisan support ([Psychiatry, Psychology and Law](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8547820/)). On 10 April 2019 — 26 days after the attack — Parliament voted 119–1 to ban most semi-automatic and military-style weapons, with owners given until the end of September to return banned firearms for compensation under a buyback program or face up to five years in prison ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/generationlisten/2019-04-10/new-zealand-passes-law-banning-most-semi-automatic-weapons)). The law kept narrow exceptions, including .22-calibre semi-automatics holding up to 10 rounds and shotgun magazines up to five cartridges, and exemptions for licensed dealers, pest controllers and bona fide collectors. Prime Minister Jacinda Ardern told lawmakers, "Fifty people died and they do not have a voice. We in this house are their voice" — the figure reflecting the toll known that day; the confirmed final toll is 51 ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/generationlisten/2019-04-10/new-zealand-passes-law-banning-most-semi-automatic-weapons)). The shooting also raised a harder problem the paper flags explicitly: the internet allowing terrorists to spread violent content and extremist ideology, where regulation proved harder to achieve than gun control ([Psychiatry, Psychology and Law](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8547820/)).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the capital of New Zealand?

Wellington. Auckland is the largest urban area, not the capital ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Zealand)).

### What is the Treaty of Waitangi?

Te Tiriti o Waitangi, signed in 1840 between the British Crown and more than 500 Māori chiefs, is New Zealand's founding document. The English and Māori versions differed in meaning — sovereignty versus governance — and the Waitangi Tribunal, established in 1975, hears claims that the government breached it ([Te Ara](https://teara.govt.nz/en/te-tiriti-o-waitangi-the-treaty-of-waitangi)).

### How did New Zealand respond to the Christchurch mosque shootings?

Within weeks Parliament passed a law banning most semi-automatic and military-style weapons, 119–1 on 10 April 2019, with a buyback scheme for owners to surrender prohibited firearms for compensation ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/generationlisten/2019-04-10/new-zealand-passes-law-banning-most-semi-automatic-weapons)).

### How many people were killed in Christchurch?

51 people were killed and 50 more injured in the attacks on Al Noor Mosque and the Linwood Islamic Centre on 15 March 2019 ([Psychiatry, Psychology and Law](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8547820/)). Early reports on the day of the gun-law vote said 50 had died; the confirmed final toll is 51.

### What is New Zealand's system of government?

A constitutional monarchy with one legislative house, the 120-seat House of Representatives. The head of state is King Charles III, represented by Governor-General Dame Cindy Kiro, and the head of government is Prime Minister Christopher Luxon ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/facts/New-Zealand)).
