## Overview

Seville — Sevilla in Spanish — is the capital and largest city of Andalusia, the southernmost region of [Spain](/places/spain/), a river city on the Guadalquivir whose cathedral, palace and archive together form a UNESCO World Heritage site ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain); [UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)). For two centuries it was the Spanish Empire's sole gateway to the Americas, and in the twentieth century it rebuilt itself twice around world fairs, in 1929 and 1992 ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/); [Andalucia.com](http://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/expo29.htm)). Today it has about 689,481 people, the fifth largest city in Spain, known for Holy Week, the April Fair and flamenco — and as the first city to name its heat waves ([World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/cities/spain/sevilla); [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90763832/seville-is-the-first-city-to-name-and-categorize-heatwaves-like-hurricanes)).

Seville's position explains much of its history. It stands on the Guadalquivir River, which flows southwest across almost the whole of Andalusia, passing Córdoba and Seville before emptying into the Atlantic Ocean west of Cádiz ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain)). The river's lower basin is the most densely settled part of Andalusia, and the city enjoys a Mediterranean climate of mild, rainy winters and hot, dry summers, with about 3,000 hours of sunshine a year across lowland Andalusia ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain)). Andalusia became an autonomous community by statute on 30 December 1981, with Sevilla as its capital ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain)). [UNESCO](/organizations/unesco/) inscribed the city's monumental heart — its Cathedral, Alcázar and Archivo de Indias — as a World Heritage site in 1987 ([Cathedral of Seville](https://www.catedraldesevilla.es/en/the-cathedral/world-heritage-site/)).

## From conquest to the Gateway of the Indies

Seville's documented history begins with conquest rather than a founding date. Muslims from North Africa conquered Andalusia in 711 CE, and the region became part of the Umayyad caliphate of Córdoba, with Córdoba, Sevilla and Granada becoming centres of culture ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain)). In 1147 Seville became the capital of a Muslim empire covering the whole of the Maghreb ([Cathedral of Seville](https://www.catedraldesevilla.es/en/the-cathedral/world-heritage-site/)). The city then passed to Christian rule in the reconquest of 1248, the date UNESCO uses as the starting point for the Christian Andalusia its World Heritage listing commemorates ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)).

What made Seville world-historical came next: the Atlantic trade. Seville became the capital of the *Carrera de Indias*, the Spanish trading monopoly with Latin America — the "Gateway to the Indies" and the only trading port with the Indies from 1503 until 1718 ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)). The machinery of that monopoly sat in the city's monuments: the Sala de los Almirantes in the Alcázar was the headquarters of the Casa de Contratación, the House of Trade, and plans were made there for the circumnavigation of the globe by Ferdinand Magellan and Sebastián Elcano in 1519–1522 ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)). Columbus's tomb is preserved in the Cathedral, and the Archivo de Indias holds the most valuable documents from the colonies' archives ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)).

The same centuries layered the city's culture. Andalusian culture still bears distinct traces of eight centuries of Moorish rule, and flamenco emerged from the intermingling of Roma migrants, Sephardic Jews and Moors ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain)). Roman Catholic observance in the region is heavily ceremonial, with elaborate processions during Holy Week ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain)).

## The monumental heart: Cathedral, Alcázar and Archivo

Together, the Cathedral, Alcázar and Archivo de Indias form a remarkable monumental complex epitomising the Spanish "Golden Age" ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)). The three buildings testify, in one compact quarter, to the major stages of the city's urban history: Islamic, Christian, and Seville's association with the New World ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)).

| Building | What it is | Key verified facts |
| -------- | ---------- | ------------------ |
| Seville Cathedral | Gothic cathedral on the former mosque's site | Founded 1403; five naves; the largest Gothic building in Europe; Columbus's tomb inside ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)) |
| La Giralda | The cathedral's bell tower, formerly the mosque's minaret | Built 1172–98 by Emir Yaqub al-Mansur; 97.52 m high; crowned with the bronze Giraldillo weathervane ([Cathedral of Seville](https://www.catedraldesevilla.es/en/the-cathedral/world-heritage-site/)) |
| Real Alcázar | Royal palace, still a residence of the Spanish royal family | Nucleus built in the 10th century as the palace of the Moslem governor; Mudejar Palacio de Pedro I ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)) |
| Archivo General de Indias | Archive of Spain's overseas empire | Built 1585 for the Casa Lonja, the merchants' consulate, designed by Juan de Herrera; archive from 1785 ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)) |

The Cathedral's own history reads as a summary of the city. Its chapter house is the first known example of the elliptical floor plan in the western world ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)). The Giralda's Renaissance top section, designed by Hernán Ruiz, makes the tower an example of cultural syncretism — an Almohad minaret finished as a Christian bell tower ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)). The mosque it replaced was destroyed beginning in 1401, and the 17th century filled the building with works by the Sevillian painters Murillo and Valdés Leal ([Cathedral of Seville](https://www.catedraldesevilla.es/en/the-cathedral/world-heritage-site/)).

## Two world fairs that rebuilt the city

Seville held two world expositions in the twentieth century — one in 1929 and one in 1992 — and both were intended to promote the city, encourage tourism and modernise it ([Andalucia.com](http://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/expo29.htm)).

| | Expo 29 (1929–1930) | Expo 92 (1992) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dates | 9 May 1929 – 21 June 1930 | 20 April – 12 October 1992 (176 days) |
| Site | In and around Parque María Luisa | Isla de la Cartuja, 215 hectares |
| Scale | 23 countries, focused on Latin America | 108 participating countries; 41,814,571 visits |

The 1929 exposition grew out of gardens donated to the city by Infanta María Luisa Fernanda, Duchess of Montpensier, after more than twenty years of planning ([Andalucia.com](http://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/expo29.htm)). Its masterpiece was the Plaza de España, designed as the Pabellón de Andalucía in red brick, towers and ceramics, mixing Mudéjar style with Art Deco and Regionalism ([Andalucia.com](http://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/expo29.htm)). The national pavilions were built to become consulates after the fair: the Argentine pavilion is now a dance conservatory, the Peruvian pavilion now houses the Casa de Ciencias, and the Portuguese pavilion is now the Portuguese consulate ([Andalucia.com](http://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/expo29.htm)).

Expo 92 was the larger gamble, and the numbers show it: 41,814,571 visits over 176 days, according to the Bureau International des Expositions — more than twice what the organisers had expected, and larger than the population of Spain at the time ([Expomuseum](https://www.expomuseum.com/expos/expo-1992-seville)). That explains the fair's physical legacy: a new airport terminal, a single train station replacing two older ones, a high-speed rail line to [Madrid](/places/madrid/), new highways and four bridges — Barqueta, Alamillo, Cartuja and El Cachorro ([Expomuseum](https://www.expomuseum.com/expos/expo-1992-seville)). The site's only existing structure was the La Cartuja monastery, where Columbus had stayed, restored for the Expo ([Expomuseum](https://www.expomuseum.com/expos/expo-1992-seville)). After the fair closed, Cartuja Island became Cartuja 93, now the Cartuja Science and Technology Park, and the restored monastery is now the Andalusian Center for Contemporary Art ([Expomuseum](https://www.expomuseum.com/expos/expo-1992-seville)).

## Festivals, flamenco and the spring calendar

Seville's two great festivals dominate its year, and both are spring events. Semana Santa, or Holy Week, is a week-long event of extraordinary processions of masked penitents and carnival-style floats, drawing people from across Spain and around the world ([Andalucia.com](https://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/festivals.htm)). Then comes the Feria de Abril, the April Fair — possibly the largest annual fair in Andalucia — a week-long party of drink, food, fino sherry and flamenco ([Andalucia.com](https://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/festivals.htm)).

The calendar does not stop with spring. The El Rocío pilgrimage is the most famous in the region, attracting nearly a million people, with Rocío Monday — Pentecost Monday, at the end of May or the start of June — a traditional holiday ([Andalucia.com](https://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/festivals.htm)). Corpus Christi is observed with a procession leaving the Cathedral, and the Virgen de los Reyes falls on 15 August ([Andalucia.com](https://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/festivals.htm)). The Bienal de Flamenco takes place in Seville every other year in even-numbered years — its 24th edition ran from 9 September to 3 October 2026 — and is the most important flamenco festival in the world ([Andalucia.com](https://www.andalucia.com/cities/seville/festivals.htm)).

## Seville today: heat, growth and the province around it

Seville's present is shaped by heat and slow demographic change. World Population Review's 2026 estimate, built on INE municipal register totals, puts the city's population at 689,481 — up just 58 people, or 0.01%, on the previous year, and down from 697,487 in 1996 ([World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/cities/spain/sevilla)). Its ranking table places Sevilla fifth in Spain, after Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia and Zaragoza — correcting the older Britannica wording that still calls it Spain's fourth largest city ([World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/cities/spain/sevilla); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain)).

The heat is the more distinctive story, and Seville has turned it into a world first. The city is the first in the world to name its heat waves and categorise them by health outcomes rather than meteorology ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90763832/seville-is-the-first-city-to-name-and-categorize-heatwaves-like-hurricanes)). The proMETEO pilot was announced by Seville's mayor Antonio Muñoz in June 2022, built on eighteen months of research by bioclimatologist Larry Kalkstein ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90763832/seville-is-the-first-city-to-name-and-categorize-heatwaves-like-hurricanes)). Names were to start at Z and run down the alphabet, alternating female and male names — Zoe, Yago, Xenia, Wenceslao and Vega — with only the most extreme category named ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90763832/seville-is-the-first-city-to-name-and-categorize-heatwaves-like-hurricanes)). The urgency was visible on the streets: a street thermometer read 47°C (116.5°F) during a heat wave in Seville on 13 June 2022 ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90763832/seville-is-the-first-city-to-name-and-categorize-heatwaves-like-hurricanes)). [Fast Company](/organizations/fast-company/) describes Seville as the hottest city in continental Europe ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90763832/seville-is-the-first-city-to-name-and-categorize-heatwaves-like-hurricanes)).

Seville's reach extends into its province. The [Asociación Española de Familias de Personas con Sordoceguera (APASCIDE)](/organizations/asociacion-espanola-de-familias-de-personas-con-sordoceguera-apascide/) operates its specialised [Centro Santa Ángela de la Cruz](/organizations/centro-santa-angela-de-la-cruz/) in [Salteras](/places/salteras/), in Seville province, supporting people with deafblindness and their families.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Seville the capital of Andalusia?**
Yes. Andalusia was established as an autonomous community by a statute of autonomy on 30 December 1981, and its capital is Sevilla ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Andalusia-region-Spain)). It is also the region's largest city, with an estimated 689,481 residents in 2026 ([World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/cities/spain/sevilla)).

**Why is Seville called the Gateway to the Indies?**
Because it was the capital of the Carrera de Indias, Spain's trading monopoly with Latin America, and the only trading port with the Indies from 1503 until 1718 ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)). The Casa de Contratación ran that monopoly from the Alcázar's Admirals' Hall ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)).

**What are the three UNESCO buildings in Seville?**
The Cathedral, founded in 1403 and the largest Gothic building in Europe; the Alcázar, a royal palace whose nucleus dates to the 10th century; and the Archivo General de Indias, built in 1585 for the merchants' consulate ([UNESCO World Heritage Centre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/383/)). UNESCO inscribed them together as a World Heritage site in 1987 ([Cathedral of Seville](https://www.catedraldesevilla.es/en/the-cathedral/world-heritage-site/)).

**What happened at Expo 92?**
The Universal Exhibition of Seville ran from 20 April to 12 October 1992 on Isla de la Cartuja, with 108 participating countries and 41,814,571 visits recorded by the BIE ([Expomuseum](https://www.expomuseum.com/expos/expo-1992-seville)). It left the city a high-speed rail line to Madrid, four new bridges and the Cartuja Science and Technology Park ([Expomuseum](https://www.expomuseum.com/expos/expo-1992-seville)).

**Why does Seville name its heat waves?**
Seville is the first city in the world to name and categorise heat waves, by their health impact rather than by temperature alone, so residents know how severe a heat event will be and what to do ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90763832/seville-is-the-first-city-to-name-and-categorize-heatwaves-like-hurricanes)). The proMETEO pilot began in June 2022, with names starting at Zoe ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90763832/seville-is-the-first-city-to-name-and-categorize-heatwaves-like-hurricanes)).
