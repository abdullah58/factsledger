## Overview

Tallinn is the capital and largest city of [Estonia](/places/estonia/), a port on Tallinn Bay of the Gulf of Finland with 456,518 residents at the start of 2025 ([City of Tallinn](https://files.tallinn.ee/live/s3fs-public/inline-files/Tallinn_2025_annual_publication_web_0.pdf)). It is a city of two faces: a medieval Hanseatic trading town whose historic centre is a UNESCO World Heritage site, and a modern information-technology hub that hosts NATO's cyber-defence centre and Skype's main research and development base ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn); [NATO Allied Command Transformation](https://www.act.nato.int/article/nato-centres-of-excellence-cooperative-cyber-defence-ccd-coe/)).

The city has been fought over, traded, occupied, and rebuilt for eight centuries. A fortified settlement existed on the site from the late 1st millennium BCE, and a town stood there in the 12th century before the Danes captured it in 1219 and built a fortress on Toompea hill ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). It became the capital of independent Estonia in 1918, was annexed to the Soviet Union in 1940, and became the capital of the refounded republic in 1991 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). Today it is Estonia's main governmental, financial, industrial, and cultural centre, and its airport is the biggest in the Baltics ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)).

## From Danish fortress to Hanseatic port

Tallinn's rise began with trade. After the Danish capture of 1219, trade flourished, especially after the city joined the Hanseatic League in 1285, and in 1346 it was sold to the Teutonic Knights ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). Through the medieval and early modern era the city — then more commonly known by its German name, Reval — was a multiethnic trading town economically dominated by its German merchant class ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). On the dissolution of the Teutonic Order in 1561 it passed to Sweden, and Peter I (the Great) captured it in 1710 during the Great Northern War, after which it became part of the Russian Empire ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)).

The city's surviving medieval fabric is concentrated on Toompea hill and in the old, walled Lower Town: the 13th-century Toom Church, the Gothic Oleviste (St. Olaf's) and Niguliste (St. Nicholas's) churches, the Great Guildhall of 1410, the Town Hall completed in 1404, and much of the old castle ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). That historic centre — covered in its own profile as [Tallinn Old Town](/places/tallinn-old-town/) — was designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1997 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)).

In the later 19th century Tallinn began to industrialise, and its historically German and Scandinavian character gradually changed as ethnic Estonian workers arrived from the countryside ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). It became the capital of independent Estonia in 1918 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)).

## Soviet decades and the refounded republic

The 20th century was the city's most violent. In 1940 Estonia was annexed to the [Union of Soviet Socialist Republics](/places/soviet-union/), and the city was occupied by German forces from 1941 to 1944 and severely damaged ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). After Soviet rule was re-established in 1944, the city expanded greatly on the back of new industries, reaching a population of about 500,000 by the late 1980s, against 137,792 recorded in the 1934 census ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). That growth was driven by workers brought in from Russia and other Soviet republics, and as a result today's Tallinn is only 53 percent ethnically Estonian ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). The human cost ran alongside: in both 1940 and again in 1944–49, many residents were deported and imprisoned by Soviet forces, others chose voluntary exile — a large proportion settling in Sweden or North America — and during the war the remaining Baltic German population was forcibly resettled while the Jewish community was destroyed ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)).

After the Supreme Soviet of Estonia declared independence in 1991, Tallinn became the capital of the refounded Estonian republic ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). The demographic picture has since stabilised but not reversed: the city was home to 456,518 people at the beginning of 2025, 1,054 fewer than a year before, with natural growth negative since 2020 ([City of Tallinn](https://files.tallinn.ee/live/s3fs-public/inline-files/Tallinn_2025_annual_publication_web_0.pdf)). The average resident lives to 80.8 years, with women living 7.5 years longer than men, and 3,262 children were born in the city in 2024, 9 percent fewer than in 2023 ([City of Tallinn](https://files.tallinn.ee/live/s3fs-public/inline-files/Tallinn_2025_annual_publication_web_0.pdf)). Within the city, the population grew in Haabersti and Kristiine and fell in the City Centre and Lasnamäe ([City of Tallinn](https://files.tallinn.ee/live/s3fs-public/inline-files/Tallinn_2025_annual_publication_web_0.pdf)).

## A digital capital — and a museum of banned books

Tallinn's post-independence economy rests on two pillars the sources describe in the same breath: the sea and the screen. It remains a major commercial and fishing port and industrial centre, with shipbuilding and machine building heading a range of engineering industries ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). At the same time, one of the most important recent developments has been its growth as an information technology centre, characterised by a high rate of local start-up companies and the siting of IT development facilities for major foreign corporations ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)). Skype's main centre for research and development is in Tallinn, because its original computer programmers were Estonian — eBay bought the company for $2.6 billion in 2005 ([American Enterprise Institute](https://www.aei.org/commentary/the-future-calling-in-estonia/)). Tourism, boosted by Estonia's membership in the EU and Schengen area, brings more than four million visitors a year ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)).

The digital rise also made the city a target and then a centre of cyber defence. A wave of cyber-attacks hit Estonian governmental and economic institutions in the spring of 2007 ([American Enterprise Institute](https://www.aei.org/commentary/the-future-calling-in-estonia/)). NATO's Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence is located in Tallinn and is the largest Centre of Excellence, sponsored by every member of the Alliance with contributions from eight additional Partner Nations ([NATO Allied Command Transformation](https://www.act.nato.int/article/nato-centres-of-excellence-cooperative-cyber-defence-ccd-coe/)). Its Locked Shields exercise in April 2023 involved 38 countries, 5,500 participating systems, and over 30 international organisations, industries, and academic partners, and the Centre produces the Tallinn Manual, its flagship work on cyber doctrine ([NATO Allied Command Transformation](https://www.act.nato.int/article/nato-centres-of-excellence-cooperative-cyber-defence-ccd-coe/)).

The city also hosts a smaller, stranger institution: the [Banned Books Museum](/organizations/banned-books-museum/), which opened on 5 December 2020 in the Old Town at Munga 5 and is part museum, part bookstore for books that have been banned, censored, restricted, or burned ([Book Riot](https://bookriot.com/banned-books-museum/)). [Joseph Maximillian Dunnigan](/people/joseph-maximillian-dunnigan/) is the spokesperson and leader of the initiative, which runs a book club, podcast, and discussion groups and held over 100 books at opening, organised by country ([Book Riot](https://bookriot.com/banned-books-museum/)). Culturally, Tallinn remains Estonia's centre, with an academy of sciences, polytechnic, fine-arts, and teacher-training institutes, a music conservatory, and several theatres and museums ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Tallinn)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim a single settled population figure: Britannica's 2021 census figure (437,817) and Statistics Estonia's start-of-2025 figure (456,518) come from different years and methods and are both stated with their years. It does not date Britannica's figure that the city is 53 percent ethnically Estonian, because the source does not date it in the text read. It does not claim Skype was founded in Tallinn — only that its main research and development centre was there. It does not describe the Banned Books Museum's founder beyond what Book Riot states (spokesperson and leader), because the press profiles of the founder were blocked in this session. It does not claim a current visitor number beyond Britannica's "more than four million annually".
