The Trump Taj Mahal died on the 27th anniversary of the accident that killed the men who were supposed to open it. When the casino shut its doors on October 10, 2016, the date marked exactly 27 years since a helicopter crash took the lives of Taj president Mark Grossinger Etess, Trump's top Atlantic City executive Stephen Hyde, and Trump Plaza executive Johnathan Benanav — the leadership team that had been preparing the resort's debut ([Atlantic City Weekly](https://atlanticcityweekly.com/trump-taj-mahal-closed-on-the-anniversary-of-executives-helicopter-crash/article_b3b59217-c61e-5337-b30e-11855d70e5c1.html)).

## A casino built on a loss

The crash happened on October 10, 1989, when the helicopter went down in a wooded Garden State Parkway median in Lacey Township. Two pilots died alongside the three executives, and federal investigators determined a faulty rotor caused the accident ([Atlantic City Weekly](https://atlanticcityweekly.com/trump-taj-mahal-closed-on-the-anniversary-of-executives-helicopter-crash/article_b3b59217-c61e-5337-b30e-11855d70e5c1.html)).

Etess was 37, Hyde 43, and Benanav 33. Etess was born into the Grossinger family that founded the famous Grossinger's Catskills resort in Liberty, New York, and he was considered Trump's point man for special sporting events — the 5,200-seat arena inside the Taj was later named the Mark G. Etess Arena in his honor ([Atlantic City Weekly](https://atlanticcityweekly.com/trump-taj-mahal-closed-on-the-anniversary-of-executives-helicopter-crash/article_b3b59217-c61e-5337-b30e-11855d70e5c1.html)). Trump appointed his brother Robert to replace Etess, but Robert quit after feeling humiliated by his older brother, and the Taj burned through three presidents by August 1990 ([Atlantic City Weekly](https://atlanticcityweekly.com/trump-taj-mahal-closed-on-the-anniversary-of-executives-helicopter-crash/article_b3b59217-c61e-5337-b30e-11855d70e5c1.html)).

The grief reached beyond business. Trump told New York Magazine in February 1990 that the crash convinced him life was short and fragile, and that it pushed him to pursue a divorce from his first wife, [Ivana Trump](/people/ivana-trump/) ([Atlantic City Weekly](https://atlanticcityweekly.com/trump-taj-mahal-closed-on-the-anniversary-of-executives-helicopter-crash/article_b3b59217-c61e-5337-b30e-11855d70e5c1.html)). He sued the helicopter's manufacturer and operator, but a federal judge ruled in April 1991 that Trump's casinos were not entitled to damages for business losses from the deaths; only the victims' families could seek wrongful-death damages, and they settled with both parties ([Atlantic City Weekly](https://atlanticcityweekly.com/trump-taj-mahal-closed-on-the-anniversary-of-executives-helicopter-crash/article_b3b59217-c61e-5337-b30e-11855d70e5c1.html)).

## The eighth wonder's shaky start

The Taj opened on April 2, 1990, at a reported construction cost of $1.2 billion, with Trump calling it "the eighth wonder of the world" ([Associated Press](https://www.reviewjournal.com/business/casinos-gaming/trump-taj-mahal-sold-for-4-cents-on-the-dollar/)). The opening was chaotic: Trump Plaza executive Jack O'Donnell got a panicked call from Trump the day before, drove to the Taj, and found staff who could not say how much money was in the casino's bank. His team spent 12 hours counting cash just to establish a starting point, and weeks passed before the full casino floor opened ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)).

The debt was crushing from the start. The New York Times noted the Taj would need to bring in a million dollars a day to cover it ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)). In 1991 the casino filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy for the first time; Trump struck a deal with lenders that forced him to surrender a large share of his ownership while keeping managerial control ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)).

That same year, the casino first came under suspicion as a possible haven for money laundering. Treasury records show the Taj failed to follow anti-money-laundering rules 106 times in its first two years of operation — more than once a week — and the resulting penalty of nearly half a million dollars was the highest ever paid by a casino for that type of violation at the time ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)).

## The record fine

The Taj never fixed its compliance problems. In 2003 Treasury inspectors again found "repeated significant violations of the Bank Secrecy Act," and the casinos filed for bankruptcy once more in 2004 ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)). A 2007 inspection warned the casino's suspicious-activity monitoring was "deficient," and return visits in 2010 and 2012 found no improvement ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)).

In 2015, Treasury and the Taj's owners agreed on a $10 million civil penalty — a new record. In the consent agreement, the casino admitted to willful and repeated violations of the Bank Secrecy Act ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)). Twelve years separated the first disturbed Treasury inspections from the penalty, and by then the money to pay it came not from Trump but from funds that would otherwise have gone to creditors in the Taj's fourth and final bankruptcy ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)). WNYC and ProPublica note that there is no evidence linking Trump himself to money laundering at the casino — only to lax controls ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)).

## Two nights that mattered to the UFC

In 2001, the Taj hosted the two shows that opened the modern UFC era. On February 23, 2001, UFC 30 "Battle at the Boardwalk" ran at the Trump Taj Mahal as the first UFC event under Zuffa, the new ownership group led by Frank and Lorenzo Fertitta and an executive then barely known to the public, [Dana White](/people/dana-white/) ([Sherdog](https://www.sherdog.com/news/articles/This-Day-in-MMA-History-Feb-23-16307)). They had bought the failing promotion from Semaphore Entertainment Group for $2 million the previous month ([Sherdog](https://www.sherdog.com/news/articles/This-Day-in-MMA-History-Feb-23-16307)). Tito Ortiz knocked out Evan Tanner to keep the light heavyweight title, and five current or eventual UFC champions fought on the card ([Sherdog](https://www.sherdog.com/news/articles/This-Day-in-MMA-History-Feb-23-16307)).

UFC 31 followed at the Taj, Zuffa's second show there: Randy Couture survived a five-round war with Pedro Rizzo to retain the heavyweight title, and Carlos Newton choked out Pat Miletich for the welterweight belt ([UFC](https://ufc.com/news/flashback-atlantic-city-gets-show-ages?language_content_entity=en)).

## Closure and the Hard Rock rebirth

Atlantic City's casino market kept shrinking as neighboring states opened their own casinos. Trump had cut most of his Atlantic City ties in 2009, keeping only a small stake in exchange for the right to use his name; that stake was wiped out when billionaire Carl Icahn acquired the parent company, Trump Entertainment Resorts, out of bankruptcy ([Associated Press](https://www.reviewjournal.com/business/casinos-gaming/trump-taj-mahal-sold-for-4-cents-on-the-dollar/)).

When Icahn and the main casino workers' union could not agree on restoring health insurance and pension benefits, workers went on strike in July 2016. In October, Icahn shut the casino down, saying he saw no way to make a profit and lamenting losses of $350 million on the property ([Associated Press](https://www.reviewjournal.com/business/casinos-gaming/trump-taj-mahal-sold-for-4-cents-on-the-dollar/)).

In March 2017, Hard Rock International — owned by Florida's Seminole Tribe — bought the shuttered Taj from Icahn for $50 million, a price revealed in SEC filings that worked out to about four cents on the dollar of the $1.2 billion build ([Associated Press](https://www.reviewjournal.com/business/casinos-gaming/trump-taj-mahal-sold-for-4-cents-on-the-dollar/)). Hard Rock planned a $375 million renovation drawing on the world's largest collection of music memorabilia — hotel guests would even be lent Fender electric guitars — and reopened the building in 2018 as the Hard Rock Hotel & Casino Atlantic City ([Associated Press](https://www.reviewjournal.com/business/casinos-gaming/trump-taj-mahal-sold-for-4-cents-on-the-dollar/)). The Trump name had come off the building the previous month ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)). That summer, bits of the old Taj were auctioned off: crystal chandeliers listed at $35,000, poker tables at $650, lamps for $8 ([WNYC Studios](https://www.wnycstudios.org/podcasts/trumpinc/episodes/money-laundering-trump-taj-mahal?tab=transcript)).
