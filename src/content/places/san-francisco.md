## Overview

San Francisco is the California city that began as a Spanish presidio and mission in 1776, on the ancestral homeland of the Ramaytush Ohlone people, and was remade twice in its first century: first by gold, then by fire ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)). By the Mexican-American War in 1846 it held only around one thousand people, mostly sailors, fishermen, whalers, and fur trappers — and within three years of the 1848 gold discovery it was a city of about twenty-five thousand, crowded with tent-houses, hotels, saloons, and gambling halls ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)). Control of the outpost had passed from Spain to Mexico and then, in 1846, to the United States, when it consisted of scattered ranchos and a settlement of several hundred people ([Advisory Council on Historic Preservation](https://www.achp.gov/preserve-america/community/san-francisco-california)). In 1945 it hosted the conference that created the United Nations, giving a city built by successive waves of newcomers a place in the founding of the postwar international order ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/1945-san-francisco-conference-and-creation-united-nations)).

## From outpost to instant city

The Gold Rush did not just grow San Francisco; it invented it in its modern form. Gold discovered in the Sierra Nevada foothills in 1848 brought, the following year, more than seven hundred ships into the bay, and the city that formed from their crews and passengers was improvised from the start ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)). The preservation agency's account uses the same image: an instant city springing up from new arrivals' tents and shacks, which then settled into more conventional patterns of growth as a hub of trade and manufacturing ([Advisory Council on Historic Preservation](https://www.achp.gov/preserve-america/community/san-francisco-california)). Its early industries expanded along the eastern shore — shipbuilding, iron works, agriculture, warehousing, and manufacturing — and its booms and busts stayed closely tied to regional and national events, from the Comstock Silver Lode in 1859 through the late-nineteenth-century depressions and the 1929 stock market crash ([Advisory Council on Historic Preservation](https://www.achp.gov/preserve-america/community/san-francisco-california)).

Immigration wrote the city's neighborhoods as surely as any planner. Thousands of Chinese immigrants came during the Gold Rush and established what became the oldest and largest Chinatown in California ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)). The pattern repeated for generations: the city flourished in the late nineteenth century, with plans for Golden Gate Park commencing in 1870, Andrew Smith Hallidie testing the first cable car on Clay Street in 1873, and Adolph Sutro's Baths building, accommodating up to ten thousand people, opening in 1894 ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)). By the turn of the twentieth century San Francisco was known as the Paris of the West — until April 1906, when an earthquake and its resulting fires leveled the city ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)).

## Fire, rebuilding, and the landmarks that followed

The 1906 disaster is the hinge of the city's history. The quake, estimated at 7.8, and the ensuing fire destroyed almost the entire urban area and killed thousands, but the city began to rebuild itself almost immediately ([Advisory Council on Historic Preservation](https://www.achp.gov/preserve-america/community/san-francisco-california)). Every year on April 6 the city holds a pre-dawn commemoration, with the mayor and residents observing a moment of silence at 5:13 a.m. ([Advisory Council on Historic Preservation](https://www.achp.gov/preserve-america/community/san-francisco-california)). The rebuilt city kept absorbing new communities: Japanese residents established Japantown in the Western Addition, where it remains today, and a burgeoning Filipino community formed Manilatown along Kearny and Jackson streets near Chinatown in the late 1910s and 1920s ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)).

Rebuilding was celebrated, not just endured. The city hosted the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in 1915, built Coit Tower in 1933, completed the Golden Gate Bridge in 1937, and followed it with the Golden Gate International Exposition in 1939 ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)). Steel production and shipbuilding reached their apex during World War II, when the city's eastern-shore industries were at their height ([Advisory Council on Historic Preservation](https://www.achp.gov/preserve-america/community/san-francisco-california)).

## A meeting ground, from the UN to the Summer of Love

San Francisco has long served as a meeting ground for diverse groups and countercultures, and its most consequential meeting was diplomatic. Between April 25 and June 26, 1945, delegates of 50 nations met at the United Nations Conference on International Organization, working from the Atlantic Charter, the Dumbarton Oaks proposals, and the Yalta Agreement to agree the Charter of the United Nations and the Statute of an International Court of Justice, effectively creating the United Nations ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/1945-san-francisco-conference-and-creation-united-nations)). There were 850 delegates from 50 countries; Poland, one of the original signatories of the 1942 Declaration by United Nations, had no representative at the conference because there was no internationally recognized Polish government at the time, but it was later admitted and counted as an original member, bringing the founding total to 51 ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/1945-san-francisco-conference-and-creation-united-nations)).

The same meeting-ground character shaped its cultural history. The Fillmore District was home to a large African American population and a lively jazz scene in the 1940s and 1950s, literary Beats flocked to North Beach's City Lights Books, and Latinx residents moving to the Mission District from the 1950s turned it into a vibrant Spanish-speaking enclave over the following decades ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)). The late 1960s welcomed the hippie generation and the Summer of Love in 1967; two years later Indigenous activists occupied Alcatraz Island, awakening the public to the plight of the country's Native Americans; and in 1972 San Francisco held its first large-scale Pride parade downtown, a groundbreaking event for the city's LGBTQ community ([SFO Museum](https://www.sfomuseum.org/exhibitions/san-francisco)).
