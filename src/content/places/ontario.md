## Overview

Ontario is [Canada](/places/canada/)'s most populous province, home to 14,223,942 people at the 2021 census, and one of the four founding provinces of Confederation in 1867. Its capital is [Toronto](/places/toronto/), Canada's largest city, and its economy — about CA$1.2 trillion in 2024, roughly 38% of national output — is the largest of any province. The province stretches from Hudson Bay in the north to four of the five Great Lakes in the south ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario); [Statistics Canada](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/251106/dq251106a-eng.pdf)).

Ontario is bounded by Manitoba to the west, Hudson Bay to the north, Québec to the east, and the [United States](/places/united-states/) and the Great Lakes to the south. At 1,076,395 square kilometres it is the second-largest province by area, after Quebec, and the fourth-largest jurisdiction in Canada once the territories are counted ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ontario-province-Canada)). The land sits on the traditional territories of the Ojibwa, Odawa, Potawatomi, Algonquin, Mississauga, Haudenosaunee, Neutral, Wendat, Cree, Oji-Cree and Métis peoples, and is now governed by 46 treaties, including the Upper Canada, Williams and Robinson treaties and Treaties 3, 5 and 9 ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

This profile covers Ontario — its history, its north–south geography, its government, its economy and its people — and adds one frame no general reference page offers: Ontario as the home province of Toronto's legal-tech cluster, the setting of this site's [Blue J](/organizations/blue-j/) profile.

## From Upper Canada to Confederation

The first residents arrived during the last ice age, approximately 11,000 years ago, living by fishing and hunting before farming took hold in the south; by 800 CE the Wendat and Haudenosaunee were established farmers growing corn, beans and squash ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). The first Europeans known to have approached the province were Henry Hudson on the James Bay coast and Étienne Brûlé and Samuel de Champlain, who travelled the Ottawa River in 1613 and reached the centre of the province in 1615; Brûlé was likely the first European to see Lakes Huron and Ontario ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

The French built a fur trade on Wendat, Innu and Algonquin networks; Haudenosaunee raids dispersed the Wendat in 1642–49. Britain did not control the region until 1758–59, and the Treaty of Paris in 1783 divided the Great Lakes down the middle, creating Ontario's southern boundary ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). Modern settlement began with some 6,000 to 10,000 Loyalists during and after the American Revolution, and the Constitutional Act of 1791 divided the old Province of Québec and created Upper Canada — called Upper Canada from 1791 to 1841, then Canada West until 1867 — with John Graves Simcoe as its first lieutenant-governor ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Canada-West)).

War came again in 1812: American forces raided and burned York (Toronto) in 1813, while British troops with Indigenous and militia help defended the Niagara frontier; the peace left the boundary unchanged. Treaties signed by the mid-1830s covered most arable land, including the Robinson treaties of 1850, and immigration tripled Upper Canada's population to 450,000 between 1825 and 1842 ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). After the Rebellions of 1837, led in Upper Canada by William Lyon Mackenzie, Britain united Upper and Lower Canada as the Province of Canada; responsible government followed in 1848–49, the Great Coalition of 1864 sought a union of the British North American colonies, and Confederation followed in 1867 ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). Its boundaries were settled at the Lake of the Woods in 1889 and drawn finally in 1912 ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

## A province of two halves

Ontario is divided by three of Canada's seven physiographic regions — the Hudson Bay Lowlands, the Canadian Shield and the St. Lawrence Lowlands — and two-thirds of the province lies under the Canadian Shield, whose rocks are among the oldest on Earth ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). Agriculture and most of the population are concentrated in the south, in the temperate, fertile Great Lakes–St. Lawrence Valley, while the thinly populated Shield dominates the north-west and centre ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

Ontario has over 250,000 lakes containing approximately one-fifth of the world's fresh water, and includes a portion of four of the five Great Lakes — Superior, Huron, Erie and Ontario ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). Other major lakes include Lake Nipigon (4,848 sq km), Lake of the Woods (3,150 sq km) and Lac Seul (1,657 sq km), and the Niagara Escarpment runs from Niagara north to Tobermory and through Manitoulin Island in Georgian Bay ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

More than 64 per cent of Ontarians live in the Golden Horseshoe, the network of communities around the western end of Lake Ontario from Peterborough to St. Catharines-Niagara, and 86 per cent of the population was urban in 2016 — the reverse of 1851 — making Ontario Canada's most urban province ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). The north's cities are strung along the railways: Sudbury sits at the heart of Canada's largest mining district, Sault Ste. Marie is a steel producer, and Thunder Bay a major transshipment port ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

## Government: a 124-seat legislature

Ontario's provincial government sits in Toronto, in the Legislative Building at Queen's Park, and handles matters affecting the province as a whole, including education, health care, the environment, agriculture and highways ([Legislative Assembly of Ontario](https://www.ola.org/en/visit-learn/teach-learn-play/levels-government/provincial)). Its parliament consists of 124 elected Members of Provincial Parliament (MPPs), each representing a geographical area called a riding; the party with the most MPPs forms the government and its leader becomes premier ([Legislative Assembly of Ontario](https://www.ola.org/en/visit-learn/teach-learn-play/levels-government/provincial)).

Ontario uses first-past-the-post voting, and under the Elections Act provincial elections are held on the first Thursday of June every four years, though one may be called earlier or follow a loss of confidence ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). Technically the lieutenant-governor, the monarch's representative, holds the highest provincial office, though the role is largely symbolic; the premier appoints Cabinet ministers from among the governing party's MPPs ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). The Canadian Encyclopedia records Doug Ford as the current premier, leading a majority Progressive Conservative government, and John Sandfield Macdonald as the first premier, from 1867 ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

## Canada's economic engine — and its legal-tech corridor

Ontario is the leading manufacturing province in Canada, employing roughly half of the country's manufacturing workers and generating about half of its total value of production; in 2016 it held 44 per cent of Canada's manufacturing jobs ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ontario-province-Canada/Manufacturing); [The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). The pre-eminence dates to Confederation and rests on steel centred at Hamilton and Sault Ste. Marie, an automobile industry aided by the 1965 Canada–United States Automotive Agreement, and proximity to American markets ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ontario-province-Canada/Manufacturing)). But manufacturing has shrunk: jobs fell 25 per cent (245,500) between 2006 and 2016, including the Caterpillar and Kellogg's plants in London and the Heinz plant in Leamington ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

Services and technology have filled part of the gap. Toronto's Bay Street area is the centre of the Canadian financial system, the principal chartered banks are headquartered there, and the Toronto Stock Exchange is the country's largest; Toronto has been Canada's main financial-services centre since the 1930s ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ontario-province-Canada/Manufacturing)). Toronto's rise as a financial centre was tied to Northern Ontario mining: mineral development around 1900 made the city first a competitor and then a winner in its long-standing competition with [Montreal](/places/montreal/) ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). Meanwhile the Toronto–Waterloo corridor has become North America's "Silicon Valley North," with technology-services employment up 13 per cent in 2011–2016 ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). One product of that corridor is Blue J, the Toronto legal-tech company founded in 2015 as a [University of Toronto](/universities/university-of-toronto/) spinout, which rebuilt its business around generative-AI tax research — new professional software sold from the province's financial capital.

Ontario produces more metals and other minerals by value than any other province or territory, leading the country in cobalt, gold, silver, nickel, selenium and platinum group metals, with most mines on the Canadian Shield around Timmins and Sudbury, where one of the world's largest nickel and copper deposits was found in 1883 ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). About 58 per cent of the province's electricity comes from nuclear power, 23 per cent from hydroelectricity and 10 per cent from natural gas; Bruce Power on Lake Huron is one of the largest nuclear plants in the world, and ending coal-fired generation between 2005 and 2015 cut electricity-sector emissions by 80 per cent ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). Forestry covers roughly 71 million hectares, about two-thirds of the province, and farming holds just over half of Canada's best agricultural land ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

The latest national accounts add a caution: Ontario's real GDP grew 1.6 per cent in 2024, slower than Canada's 2.0 per cent, housing investment fell 4.8 per cent, and Ontario households carried the highest debt-service ratio in the country at 10.9 per cent, even as the province's saving rate recorded the strongest increase in net saving ([Statistics Canada](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/251106/dq251106a-eng.pdf)). The implication is direct: the country's largest provincial economy, at roughly CA$1.2 trillion and about 38 per cent of national output, is also the most exposed when housing cools and borrowing costs bite ([Statistics Canada](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/251106/dq251106a-eng.pdf)).

## People: the most urban province

Ontario's 14,223,942 residents at the 2021 census made up more than one-third of Canada's population, up 5.8 per cent from 2016 against 5.2 per cent nationally; nine in ten Ontarians lived in a metropolitan area or agglomeration ([Statistics Canada](https://www12.statcan.gc.ca/census-recensement/2021/as-sa/fogs-spg/Page.cfm?Lang=e&Dguid=2021A000235&topic=1); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ontario-province-Canada)). The population is strikingly diverse: 65.1 per cent report English as their mother tongue and 3.3 per cent French, while 42.1 per cent of Torontonians report a non-official language as mother tongue; French-speaker shares are highest in Ottawa (37.8 per cent) and Sudbury (33.1 per cent) ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

By origin, 62.2 per cent is of European origin, Indigenous peoples (First Nations, Métis and Inuit) make up 2.9 per cent, and visible minorities comprise 34.3 per cent, with South Asian, Chinese and Black people the largest communities ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). By religion, 52.2 per cent identify as Christian, followed by Islam at 6.7 per cent, Hinduism at 4.1 per cent and Judaism at 1.4 per cent; 31.6 per cent claim no religious affiliation ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)). Toronto (2,794,356) is Canada's largest city, followed in the province by Ottawa (1,017,449), Mississauga (717,961), Brampton (656,480) and Hamilton (569,353) ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/ontario)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the capital of Ontario?**
Toronto — Ontario's provincial capital and Canada's largest city, with 2,794,356 residents at the 2021 census. The provincial government meets at Queen's Park; Canada's national capital, Ottawa, is also in Ontario.

**When did Ontario join Confederation?**
On July 1, 1867, under the British North America Act, Ontario became one of the four founding provinces of the Dominion of Canada, alongside Quebec, New Brunswick and Nova Scotia. Before that the region was Upper Canada (1791–1841) and then Canada West (1841–1867).

**Is Ontario Canada's largest province?**
By population, yes — the most populous province, with 14,223,942 residents in 2021, more than one-third of Canada's population. By area it is second, at 1,076,395 square kilometres, after Quebec; fourth-largest once the territories are counted.

**What is Ontario known for economically?**
Canada's largest provincial economy (about CA$1.2 trillion in 2024), the leading manufacturing province, the top producer of metals and minerals by value, and home to the Toronto Stock Exchange and the Toronto–Waterloo technology corridor. Its real GDP grew 1.6 per cent in 2024, slower than the national 2.0 per cent.
