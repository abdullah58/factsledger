The District of Columbia is the federal district of the United States — the 68-square-mile jurisdiction containing the capital city of Washington — and a jurisdiction that is neither a state nor an ordinary territory ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Washington-DC)). Congress holds plenary legislative authority over it, while roughly 690,000 people live and work inside its borders ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12122/IN12122.3.pdf); [U.S. Census Bureau](https://www2.census.gov/geo/maps/DC2020/Profiles20/2020_State_Profile_District_of_Columbia.pdf)). Its defining tension is fiscal as much as constitutional: a 2026 House resolution asserts that District residents pay more federal tax per capita than any state — and more total federal tax than 26 states — while being denied any voting representation in Congress ([H. Res. 1244](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hres1244ih/pdf/BILLS-119hres1244ih.pdf)).

## Home rule with an asterisk

Since the 1973 Home Rule Act (in operation from 1974), the District elects a mayor and city council that can set tax rates, pass budgets, and organize or abolish agencies — but Congress retains the power to veto District actions, and council-passed budgets must be reviewed and enacted by Congress ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Washington-DC)). The veto is procedural, not theoretical. Nearly every council law sits through a congressional "layover" — 30 legislative days for most bills, 60 for criminal legislation — during which any member of Congress can introduce a joint resolution of disapproval; if both chambers adopt it and the president signs (or a veto is overridden), the D.C. law dies ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12122/IN12122.3.pdf)). Since 1973, three District laws have actually been nullified this way, and in February 2023 the House voted to disapprove two more D.C. acts ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12122/IN12122.3.pdf)). Congress can also simply rewrite local law directly: in September 2025 the House debated H.R. 5143, which overrode the District's own 2022 police-pursuit rules, with opponents calling the chamber a "super city council" for Washington ([Congressional Record](https://www.congress.gov/congressional-record/volume-171/issue-152/house-section/article/H4379-1)).

The presidency holds its own lever. Section 740 of the Home Rule Act lets the president place the District's Metropolitan Police under federal control for up to 30 days, on 48 hours' notice to Congress, when the president determines an emergency exists ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Washington-DC)). In August 2025 [Donald Trump](/people/donald-trump/) became the first president ever to invoke it, citing "total lawlessness" in the District even as crime rates had been declining since their pandemic-era spike ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Washington-DC)). Even the judiciary is federal: judges of the D.C. Superior Court and the D.C. Court of Appeals are nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate, after screening by the D.C. Judicial Nomination Commission — and in September 2025 the House passed H.R. 5125 to terminate that commission outright and hand chief-judge appointments to the president ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5125)).

## The statehood question

The standing legislative answer is the Washington, D.C. Admission Act (H.R. 51 / S. 51), which would admit most of the District as the State of Washington, Douglass Commonwealth — keeping the familiar "D.C." initials while honoring abolitionist Frederick Douglass — and retain a small "Capital" enclave containing the Capitol, the [White House](/organizations/the-white-house/), the [Supreme Court](/organizations/us-supreme-court/) building, federal monuments, and adjacent federal office buildings as the constitutionally required seat of government ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/51)). The House passed the bill on April 22, 2021; the Senate received it the same day and never acted ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/51)). Reintroduced in the 119th Congress on January 3, 2025 by Delegate Eleanor Holmes Norton — the District's non-voting House delegate, whose office the bill would eliminate — it remains at the "Introduced" stage ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/51)).

The political case for it was restated in April 2026, when Norton introduced H. Res. 1244, which supports designating May 1, 2026 as "D.C. Statehood Day" and calls on Congress to pass the admission bills ([H. Res. 1244](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hres1244ih/pdf/BILLS-119hres1244ih.pdf)). The resolution asserts that the District has a larger population than two states, a larger GDP than 15 states, and the highest per-capita GDP and personal income of any state; that 86 percent of District voters backed statehood in a 2016 referendum; and that residents have been fighting for voting representation and full self-government for more than 200 years ([H. Res. 1244](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hres1244ih/pdf/BILLS-119hres1244ih.pdf)). Under the 23rd Amendment, the District already participates in presidential elections through the Electoral College ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/51)).

## Land, people, and the federal footprint

In 1790 Congress established a 100-square-mile territory along the Potomac as the permanent seat of the federal government, on land ceded by Maryland and Virginia ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Washington-DC)). The Virginia portion south of the river was returned to the state in the mid-19th century, leaving today's 68 square miles (176 square kilometres) on the Potomac's northeast bank, bordered by Maryland on the other three sides ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Washington-DC)). The 2020 census counted 689,545 residents: 41.4% Black or African American alone, 39.6% White alone, 4.9% Asian alone, with 11.3% Hispanic or Latino of any race, living in 350,364 housing units — nearly two-thirds of them renter-occupied ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www2.census.gov/geo/maps/DC2020/Profiles20/2020_State_Profile_District_of_Columbia.pdf)).

The federal presence is the local economy. As of 2025, more than 168,000 federal jobs were based in the District — more than one in every five jobs in the city — and about half of Washington's land is owned by the U.S. government, which pays no taxes on it ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Washington-DC)).
