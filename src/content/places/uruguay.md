## Overview

Uruguay is a small, stable democracy on South America's southeastern coast, between Brazil and Argentina, with about 3.5 million people, Montevideo as its capital and largest city, and an economy built on agriculture, cellulose, and software. It hosted and won the first football World Cup in 1930, endured a military dictatorship from 1973 to 1985, and has since built one of Latin America's strongest democracies, with progressive social policies and a large middle class. ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay); [NBC Sports](https://www.nbcsportsphiladelphia.com/soccer/when-was-the-first-world-cup/259615/))

The country is the second smallest on the continent, yet Britannica describes it as one of Latin America's most politically stable democracies, known for a high standard of living, progressive social policies, and strong civic participation ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). Its official name is the Oriental Republic of Uruguay, its official language is Spanish, and its currency is the peso uruguayo ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). Almost half the population is concentrated in metropolitan Montevideo, a port city at the mouth of the Río de la Plata, while Salto and Paysandú are small by comparison ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). Uruguay became a high-income country by World Bank standards in 2012 and has the region's largest per-capita middle class, at around 60 percent of the population; Montevideo, a major port city, handles most of the country's foreign trade ([U.S. International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/knowledge-product/uruguay-market-overview); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)).

## Geography

Uruguay is a wedge-shaped country bounded by Brazil to the north and east, the Atlantic Ocean to the southeast, and the Río de la Plata to the south, while the Uruguay River serves as its western boundary with Argentina ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). Its landscape is largely gently rolling land, averaging about 117 metres, with valleys, plains, and ridges, notably Haedo Ridge in the north and Grande Ridge in the southeast, and Mount Catedral (514 metres) is the highest point ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). No large river flows entirely within Uruguay, but the Uruguay River and the Río de la Plata estuary are navigable for oceangoing ships as far as Paysandú, and the Negro River crosses the country from northeast to southwest ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). The climate is temperate: July averages 54 °F (12 °C) at Salto and 50 °F (10 °C) at Montevideo, January averages 79 °F (26 °C) at Salto and 72 °F (22 °C) at Montevideo, frost is almost unknown on the coast, and rainfall generally exceeds 40 inches (1,000 mm) a year ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)).

## History

Montevideo was founded in 1726 by Bruno Mauricio de Zabala, and Uruguay has been independent since 1828, with strong ties to the [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/), France, and Italy ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). The 19th century brought factional struggles, but in the early 20th century Uruguay became one of Latin America's more progressive societies, with advanced social legislation ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). José Batlle y Ordóñez, president in 1903–1907 and 1911–1915, built a robust welfare state with labour rights, public education, progressive taxation, and public health ([NACLA](https://nacla.org/light-and-struggle-social-justice-human-rights-and-accountability-uruguay-review/)). On 27 June 1973, President Juan María Bordaberry shut down Parliament and ceded power to the armed forces in a so-called autogolpe, beginning 12 years of authoritarian rule ([NACLA](https://nacla.org/light-and-struggle-social-justice-human-rights-and-accountability-uruguay-review/)). The military arrested one in every 50 people; 300,000–400,000 citizens fled into exile, against the CRS's narrower tens of thousands forced into political exile, 3,000–4,000 imprisoned, and several hundred killed or disappeared ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF10881/IF10881.3.pdf)). The regime lost a 1980 plebiscite by 57 to 43 percent, civilian rule returned in 1985 under Julio María Sanguinetti, and the 1986 Ley de Caducidad amnesty was repealed in 2011 ([NACLA](https://nacla.org/light-and-struggle-social-justice-human-rights-and-accountability-uruguay-review/)).

## Government and society

Uruguay is a presidential republic whose president is both head of state and head of government, with a two-chamber General Assembly ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF10881/IF10881.3.pdf)). The Broad Front first took the presidency in 2005 under Tabaré Vázquez, ending 170 years of domination by the National and Colorado parties, and returned in 2025 under Yamandú Orsi, succeeding Luis Lacalle Pou on 1 March ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF10881/IF10881.3.pdf); [U.S. International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/knowledge-product/uruguay-market-overview)). Under Broad Front governments Uruguay led Latin America on LGBT rights, legalised first-trimester abortion, and became the first country to legalise and regulate every aspect of the marijuana market ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF10881/IF10881.3.pdf)). Most Uruguayans descend from 19th- and 20th-century Spanish and Italian immigrants ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). Spanish is spoken throughout, although a mix of Portuguese and Spanish called portuñol is heard in Rivera and other border towns ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). Nearly half the people are nominally Roman Catholic, one-third other or nondenominational Christian, and one-sixth agnostic or atheist ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). The 2023 census counted 3,499,451 people, including 1,287,452 in Montevideo city; the next largest cities were Salto, Ciudad de la Costa, and Maldonado ([CityPopulation](https://www.citypopulation.de/en/uruguay/cities/?admid=1467)).

## Economy

Uruguay's economy rests on agriculture, but its export mix has shifted. More than 80 percent of goods exports are agricultural-based, going to more than 160 countries ([U.S. International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/knowledge-product/uruguay-market-overview)). In 2024, cellulose became the top export product for the first time, at 20 percent of goods exports with a value of $2.54 billion, surpassing beef at $2 billion, while soybeans ranked third at 9 percent with $1.2 billion ([U.S. International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/knowledge-product/uruguay-market-overview)). Growth averaged 5.4 percent a year in 2004–2014, contracted 6 percent in 2020, and recovered to 3.1 percent in 2024 after a drought-hit 0.4 percent in 2023 ([U.S. International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/knowledge-product/uruguay-market-overview)). Software is the standout service export: Uruguay is Latin America's second-largest software exporter per capita, and its ICT sector generated $3.681 billion in 2024, or 4.5 percent of GDP ([Uruguay XXI](https://www.uruguayxxi.gub.uy/en/information-center/article/ict-in-uruguay-2026/?download=en)). International sales reached $2.323 billion, or 63 percent of revenue, and the [United States](/places/united-states/) remained the main destination at 77.6 percent; internet access reached 94 percent of households ([Uruguay XXI](https://www.uruguayxxi.gub.uy/en/information-center/article/ict-in-uruguay-2026/?download=en)).

## Culture

Uruguayan culture blends European heritage, especially Spanish and Italian, with the gaucho tradition, African-descended music, and a strong sporting identity. The gaucho tradition is vital to Uruguayan art and folklore, and Montevideo is the cultural hub ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). Beef is central to the cuisine, with barbecues (parrilladas) very common, and Uruguayans share a national drink, maté, often enjoyed communally ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Uruguay)). [UNESCO](/organizations/unesco/) has inscribed two Uruguayan elements on its Representative List, both in 2009: candombe and tango, the latter jointly with Argentina ([UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/state/uruguay-UY)). Candombe drum calls enliven the Sur, Palermo, and Cordón districts of Montevideo, where the practice begins around communal fires before the march ([UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL?RL=00182)). Transmitted within families of African descent, candombe is recognised as an expression of resistance as well as a musical celebration and collective social practice ([UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL?RL=00182)). In football, Uruguay hosted and won the first World Cup in 1930, beating Argentina 4-2 in the final, and won again in 1950, beating Brazil 2-1 in Rio de Janeiro ([NBC Sports](https://www.nbcsportsphiladelphia.com/soccer/when-was-the-first-world-cup/259615/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Where is Uruguay?**
Uruguay is on the southeastern coast of South America, bordered by Brazil to the north and east, the Atlantic Ocean to the southeast, and the Río de la Plata to the south, with the Uruguay River as its western boundary with Argentina. It is the continent's second-smallest country, and Montevideo is its capital and largest city.

**What is Uruguay known for?**
Uruguay is known for its stable democracy, progressive social policies, and large middle class, for beef and cellulose exports, and for a fast-growing software sector. Culturally it is known for football, including the first World Cup in 1930, for tango and candombe, for beef barbecues, and for the communal drink maté.

**How big is Uruguay's population?**
The 2023 census counted 3,499,451 people in Uruguay, including 1,287,452 in Montevideo city and 1,302,954 in Montevideo department. The next largest cities were Salto, Ciudad de la Costa, and Maldonado. Britannica's 2026 estimate of 3,545,000 is a later projection, not a census count.

**What are Uruguay's main exports?**
In 2024, cellulose became Uruguay's top goods export at $2.54 billion, ahead of beef at $2 billion and soybeans at $1.2 billion. More than 80 percent of goods exports are agricultural-based. Software is the standout service export, with ICT international sales of $2.323 billion in 2024.
