## Overview

Poland is a Central European country of about 37.3 million people, officially the Republic of Poland, with Warsaw as its capital. A unitary multiparty republic with a two-house parliament, it is a market democracy, a member of NATO and the European Union, and the largest economy in Central Europe. Its story runs from disappearance in the partitions, through World War II's devastation and half a century of communist rule, to Solidarity and the 1989 transition ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland); [World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/poland)). Poland sits at a geographic crossroads linking the forested lands of northwestern Europe and the Atlantic sea lanes to the fertile plains of the Eurasian frontier, bounded by seven nations ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Polish culture stayed strong even when the state vanished: Poles settled in numbers in the [United States](/places/united-states/), Canada, Argentina and Australia, and Romantics such as Frédéric Chopin and Adam Mickiewicz became leading lights across Europe ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Warsaw, heavily damaged in World War II, was faithfully restored in a thoroughgoing reconstruction effort ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)).

## Geography, cities and people

Poland lies at the physical centre of the European continent, roughly between latitudes 49° and 55° N and longitudes 14° and 24° E ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). It is bordered to the north by the Baltic Sea, to the northeast by Russia and Lithuania, to the east by Belarus and Ukraine, to the south by Slovakia and the Czech Republic along the Beskid, Carpathian and Sudeten mountain watersheds, and to the west by Germany along the Neisse and Oder rivers ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). The country covers 311,895 square kilometres and is a land of great forests and rivers, broad plains and tall mountains ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Its population is about 37,253,000 (2026 estimate), about 59.7 percent urban, with life expectancy of 74.9 years for men and 82.3 years for women ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Warsaw is the capital and largest city; other historic cities include Poznań, seat of Poland's first bishopric, Gdańsk, an active Baltic port, and Kraków, a historic centre of arts and education and the home of Pope John Paul II ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)).

## History: partitions, war and communist rule

In the mid-1500s, united Poland was the largest state in Europe and perhaps the continent's most powerful nation ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Two and a half centuries later, during the Partitions of Poland (1772–1918), it disappeared, parceled out among the contending empires of Russia, Prussia and Austria ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Even in crisis, Polish ideals travelled: patriots such as Kazimierz Pułaski and Tadeusz Kościuszko informed the American Revolution, and the Polish constitution of 1791, the oldest in Europe, incorporated ideals of the American and French revolutions ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Restored as a nation in 1918, Poland was ravaged by two world wars ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). World War II was particularly damaging: Poland's historically strong Jewish population was almost wholly annihilated in the Holocaust, and millions of non-Jewish Poles also died, victims of more partition and conquest ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). With the fall of the Third Reich, Poland effectively lost its independence again, becoming a communist satellite state of the [Soviet Union](/places/soviet-union/) ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)).

## Solidarity and the 1989 transition

Nearly half a century of totalitarian rule followed, though not without strong challenges from Poland's workers, who were supported by a dissident Catholic Church that called the Soviet system's economic failures into question ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). In the late 1970s, beginning in the shipyards of Gdańsk, those workers formed a nationwide movement called Solidarity (Solidarność) ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Despite the arrest of Solidarity's leadership, its newspapers kept publishing, spreading its values and agenda throughout the country ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Britannica describes Solidarity as the first independent labour union in a Soviet bloc country ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). In May 1989 the Polish government fell, along with communist regimes throughout eastern Europe, beginning Poland's rapid transformation into a democracy ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). By the turn of the twenty-first century, Poland was a market-based democracy, abundant in products of all kinds and a member of both NATO and the European Union ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)).

## Government

Poland is a unitary multiparty republic with two legislative houses: the Sejm (460 seats) and the Senate (100 seats) ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). The president serves as head of state, and the country's official name is Rzeczpospolita Polska, the Republic of Poland ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). The official language is Polish and there is no official religion ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Warsaw is the centre of the country's social, political, economic, scientific and cultural life, and its layout is divided into seven districts reflecting centuries-old local names: Śródmieście, Żoliborz, Wola, Ochota, Mokotów, Praga-Południe (Praga South) and Praga-Północ (Praga North) ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). The city features historical monuments such as the Gothic St. John's Cathedral and the red-brick fortifications known as the Barbican ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Poland's accession to NATO in 1999 and to the European Union in 2004 firmly placed the country in the European and Trans-Atlantic community ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/poland)).

## Economy

Poland is the largest economy in Central Europe, with GDP surpassing $1 trillion in 2025 ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/poland)). Since the return to the market economy in the 1990s, Poland has experienced remarkable economic success, with an over-30-year growth streak only briefly interrupted by a shallow COVID-19-induced recession ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/poland)). Its economy is characterised by resilience, relatively strong diversification and openness ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/poland)). Growth is solid, unemployment is among the lowest in the European Union, the current account is close to balance, and inflation has moderated ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/poland)). A sound financial sector, adaptable firms and a diversified export base continue to support this performance, while fiscal pressures remain a key policy challenge and defence and security spending has increased in response to a more demanding regional security environment ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/poland)). Faster absorption of EU Recovery and Resilience Facility funds has kept investment strong, supporting construction and manufacturing ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/poland)).

## Culture and heritage

Polish culture remained strong through national crisis, flourishing even far from home ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Romantic pianist Frédéric Chopin and poet Adam Mickiewicz were leading European lights in the nineteenth century, and Polish intellectuals, musicians, filmmakers and writers still enrich world arts and letters, including Nobel poet Wisława Szymborska ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Polish traditions blend folk culture, religion, nationhood: the Catholic Church and ensembles such as Mazowsze have preserved religious and folk elements, while dress, dialects and festivals have felt mass culture's impact ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Polish cuisine features duck soup, red beet soup, dumplings, kielbasa, sauerkraut; pączki are pastries served before Lent ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). Major holidays include Easter, Christmas, Constitution Day on 3 May, Independence Day on 11 November and Topienie Marzanny, when children throw winter dolls into rivers ([Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/place/Poland)). UNESCO lists 18 World Heritage properties in Poland — 16 cultural and 2 natural — including Kraków and Warsaw's Historic Centres, Auschwitz Birkenau, Malbork Castle, the Wieliczka and Bochnia salt mines, Białowieża Forest and Gdynia Modernist City Centre ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/statesparties/PL/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Where is Poland located?**
Poland lies at the physical centre of the European continent, roughly between latitudes 49° and 55° N and longitudes 14° and 24° E. It borders the Baltic Sea, Russia, Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, Slovakia, the Czech Republic and Germany. Its capital is Warsaw, and other major historic cities include Kraków, Gdańsk and Poznań.

**What happened to Poland in World War II?**
World War II was particularly damaging for Poland. Its historically strong Jewish population was almost wholly annihilated in the Holocaust, and millions of non-Jewish Poles also died, victims of partition and conquest. Warsaw was heavily damaged in the war and later faithfully restored.

**What was Solidarity?**
Solidarity (Solidarność) was a nationwide workers' movement that began in the shipyards of Gdańsk in the late 1970s. Britannica describes it as the first independent labour union in a Soviet bloc country. Despite the arrest of its leadership, its newspapers kept publishing, and its pressure helped bring down Poland's communist government in May 1989.

**What type of government does Poland have?**
Poland is a unitary multiparty republic with two legislative houses: the Sejm, with 460 seats, and the Senate, with 100 seats. The president serves as head of state. The official language is Polish, there is no official religion, and the currency is the złoty.

**How big is Poland's economy?**
Poland is the largest economy in Central Europe, with GDP surpassing $1 trillion in 2025. Growth accelerated from 3.2% in 2024 to 3.8% in 2025 and is projected at 3.6% in 2026. Unemployment is among the lowest in the European Union, though fiscal deficits near 7% of GDP are a key challenge.
