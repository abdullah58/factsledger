# Yale Law School

Yale Law School is the rare elite institution whose students once rebelled against its grading system — and won. The law school of Yale University in New Haven, Connecticut, formally founded in 1824, runs on an Honors/Pass/Low Pass/Fail scale with no GPA and no class rank, a direct product of the student protests that consumed the school from 1967 to 1970 ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/about-yale-law-school/offices-services/registrar/grades-yale-law-school); [Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)). Directories will tell you it spent 35 years atop the national rankings; what they underplay is how the place got its character — a law-office apprenticeship turned university department, a suspected arson fire fought by students including a future presidential nominee, and the class of 2013 that produced both the vice president and his wife ([The Washington Free Beacon](https://freebeacon.substack.com/p/yale-law-school-loses-top-spot-in); [The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/us/2025/01/20/who-is-usha-chilukuri-vance/)).

## From a law office to a law school

The school's origins are humbler than its reputation. It began in the New Haven law office of Seth Perkins Staples — one of the defense lawyers in the 1839 Amistad case — where aspiring lawyers learned through apprentice clerkships before formal law schools existed ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)). Staples' former student Samuel J. Hitchcock eventually became his partner and proprietor of what was called the New Haven Law School, and 1824 — the year former U.S. Senator David Daggett joined Hitchcock as co-proprietor — is the founding year the school formally recognizes, though it acknowledges debate over a singular date ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)).

The affiliation with Yale evolved gradually: the Yale College catalogue began listing "law students" separately in 1824, Daggett received a joint appointment as Professor of Law in Yale College in 1826, and students began receiving Yale degrees in 1843, with the Yale Corporation formally establishing a Law Department in 1846 after purchasing Hitchcock's library following his death ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)). The school's first full-time professor, Francis Wayland, was hired in 1871 and named the first full-time dean two years later; his 30-year deanship organized the law library and established the Yale Law Journal ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)).

The early milestones read like a catalog of firsts. Edwin Archer Randolph graduated in 1880, the school's first Black graduate, and later that year became the first Black person admitted to the Connecticut Bar Association ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)). Alice Rufie Jordan Blake gained admission in 1885 and became the first female graduate, arguing there was no written policy excluding women — though women were not formally permitted to enroll until 1918, still ahead of Yale's Ivy League counterparts and two years before the 19th Amendment ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)). The Sterling Law Building, modeled on London's Inns of Court, opened on Wall Street in 1931 and still occupies an entire city block; the 1930s also saw legal realism flourish at Yale, the movement that treated law as empirical evidence rather than theoretical doctrine and helped make the school a national institution ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)).

## The rebellion that abolished grades

The most consequential episode in the school's modern history was a student revolt. From 1967 to 1970 — a period former dean Abraham Samuel Goldstein '49 called the "dark ages" — students frustrated by the curriculum, the Socratic method, and the hierarchy of the grading system demanded institutional change, with discontent running deepest among Black and female students ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)). The unrest culminated in the law library fire of 1970, suspected but never proven to be arson, which destroyed roughly 500 books; students formed a bucket brigade to put it out, among them Hillary Rodham, class of 1973 ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)).

The school's response defined its modern identity. It made two fundamental changes: a real commitment to supporting its clinics, and a new grading system — first-term courses on a credit-fail basis, then Honors/Pass/Low Pass/Fail, with no grade point averages computed and no individual class rank ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school); [Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/about-yale-law-school/offices-services/registrar/grades-yale-law-school)). The small-group approach to instruction, pioneered in 1955, and the public-interest emphasis remain embedded features of the school today ([Yale Law School](https://law.yale.edu/yls-today/news/striving-golden-age-historical-look-yale-law-school)).

## The class of 2013 and the rankings fall

The school's most politically consequential recent alumni arrived as students in 2010. [J.D. Vance](/people/jd-vance/) and Usha Chilukuri met at Yale Law School in the 2010s while organizing a student discussion group on "social decline in white America," and married in 2014, a year after they graduated — placing both in the class of 2013 ([The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/us/2025/01/20/who-is-usha-chilukuri-vance/)). Vance's memoir *Hillbilly Elegy* grew out of a paper he wrote at the school, and the couple's Yale years later became one of the most dissected biographical passages in American politics ([The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/news/us/2025/01/20/who-is-usha-chilukuri-vance/)).

The school's relationship with its most famous recent graduate is strained. After Vance's election as vice president in 2024, the school declined to congratulate him publicly; chief of staff Debra Kroszner cited a policy of institutional neutrality and said the news would appear in the biannual alumni magazine, while declining to produce the policy's text ([The College Fix](https://www.thecollegefix.com/double-standard-yale-law-school-silent-on-jd-vance-election-citing-institutional-neutrality/)). The contrast was noted: the school had congratulated Hillary Clinton, class of 1973, on her "historic nomination" the day after the 2016 Democratic convention, while Vance's undergraduate alma mater, Ohio State, congratulated him within hours ([The College Fix](https://www.thecollegefix.com/double-standard-yale-law-school-silent-on-jd-vance-election-citing-institutional-neutrality/)).

The rankings story closed a longer arc. In 2022 the school withdrew from the *U.S. News & World Report* law school rankings, arguing the methodology disincentivized programs supporting public-interest careers ([The Washington Free Beacon](https://freebeacon.substack.com/p/yale-law-school-loses-top-spot-in)). In 2026 it lost the top spot it had held for 35 years anyway — Stanford took first place, Yale tied for second with the University of Chicago, and Harvard fell to sixth ([The Washington Free Beacon](https://freebeacon.substack.com/p/yale-law-school-loses-top-spot-in)).
