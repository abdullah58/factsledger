## Overview

The Battle of Stalingrad — fought from 17 July 1942 to February 1943 in and around the Soviet city on the Volga that bore [Joseph Stalin](/people/joseph-stalin/)'s name — was the bloodiest battle of the Second World War and its Eastern Front turning point ([IWM](https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/second-world-war/eastern-front/what-you-need-to-know-about-the-battle-of-stalingrad)). For [Adolf Hitler](/people/adolf-hitler/), it was a campaign for oil: the Caucasus oilfields were, in his judgment, "the key to victory — without them the war could not be won" ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). It ended with an entire German army encircled and destroyed: the Sixth Army's starved, frozen remnants surrendered at the end of January 1943, with 91,000 taken prisoner ([IWM](https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/second-world-war/eastern-front/what-you-need-to-know-about-the-battle-of-stalingrad)).

The Imperial War Museums' verdict is unsparing: Stalingrad "marked the high tide of [Hitler's] ambitions in the East, after which retreat and defeat were irreversible" — and, in the IWM curator's phrase, the battle may have been "doomed from the start," its outcome decided before the Germans even reached the city ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)).

## The oil gamble

By 1942 Germany's position was deteriorating. It had previously relied on the Soviet Union itself for raw materials; now at war with its former ally after [Operation Barbarossa](/events/operation-barbarossa/), those supplies were running short — and since December 1941 the United States had entered the war, so Hitler feared a two-front war against American industrial power and felt his window closing ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). The 1941 invasion force had been the largest ever assembled; a year later it was stretched over a 2,500km front, with the Leningrad siege consuming the north and Rzhev the center — so for the supposedly war-winning 1942 offensive, Army Group South could pull together only 72 German divisions, about a million men ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)).

The plan, Fall Blau (Case Blue), launched 28 June 1942: break through, take Voronezh, encircle Soviet forces in eastern Ukraine, then split — Army Group B racing to a Don–Volga blocking line at Astrakhan while Army Group A turned south for the oilfields at Maikop and Grozny ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). Hitler had to supplement his forces with Romanian and Italian allies — "relatively poorly equipped" — and had, once again, underestimated the Soviets, who could field over a million more fighting troops and were creating new air and tank armies ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). Then, on 23 July 1942, with the Red Army disintegrating before him, Hitler changed the plan: both army groups would pursue their objectives simultaneously, and Friedrich Paulus's Sixth Army was ordered to take Stalingrad first, to intercept Soviet oil shipments moving up the Volga ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)).

## The meatgrinder

Army Group A reached the Maikop oilfields on 10 August — and found the wells destroyed by Soviet engineers ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). The Sixth Army had it worse: slowed by logistics and stiffening Soviet resistance, its men were reduced to eating their horses before they even reached the city ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). Crossing the Don on 23 August, they entered Stalingrad's northern suburbs the next day — "the fight of their lives" ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)).

The city's defense fell to General Vasily Chuikov's 62nd Army, with much of the civilian population still in the ruins ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). The Luftwaffe's bombing reduced Stalingrad to rubble — which "failed to destroy the city's defences and caused more problems than it solved," since rubble hindered the attackers and favored the defenders ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). It was the Germans' first real taste of urban warfare, and the Red Army proved the superior practitioner; snipers competed to inflict casualties, and "perhaps even more than its actual toll, sniperism's most effective impact was the fear it created" ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). By mid-November the Germans held 90% of the city, but the Soviets clung to thin strips along the Volga, ferried reinforcements across the river all winter — while the Sixth Army starved on the end of just three single railway lines, not fully converted to German gauge, short of ammunition, food, fuel, and spares ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)).

## Uranus and the Kessel

On 19–20 November 1942, Georgy Zhukov struck. Operation Uranus sent over a million men, 1,500 aircraft, and 800 tanks against the thinly-spread Romanian and Italian forces guarding the Sixth Army's flanks; on 23 November the pincers met at Kalach, and the Sixth Army was encircled ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). Many German commanders wanted a breakout; Hitler forbade it, promised a Luftwaffe airlift, and organized a relief force under Erich von Manstein — "but neither had the strength or resources to succeed" ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). When the Soviets broke through the Italian lines in Operation Little Saturn, the relief was abandoned and Army Group A withdrew from the Caucasus, leaving the Sixth Army alone ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)).

The end came at the close of January 1943. In a final irony, "at the very moment he was preparing to surrender his forces to the Soviets, Hitler promoted Paulus to Field Marshal" — expecting him to commit suicide, since no German Field Marshal had ever been captured alive — "but Paulus refused" ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). Britannica records the surrender on 31 January 1943, against Hitler's orders ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/eb/article-9069378/Battle-of-Stalingrad)).

| Side | Losses (attributed) |
|---|---|
| Axis | ~800,000 casualties incl. missing/captured (Britannica); ~500,000 killed, wounded, or taken prisoner in/around the city Aug 1942–Jan 1943, 91,000 taken prisoner at the surrender (IWM) |

The IWM adds one figure rarely quoted: some 14,000 Red Army soldiers — two to three divisions — "were deliberately killed by their own side, shot by special units of the Security Police for 'cowardice' or other un-Soviet behaviour" ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Who won the Battle of Stalingrad?**

The Soviet Union. A strategic counteroffensive flanked and surrounded the German Sixth Army, which surrendered against Hitler's orders on 31 January 1943 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/eb/article-9069378/Battle-of-Stalingrad)).

**Why was Stalingrad the turning point?**

It destroyed two entire German armies, used up precious reserves, and humiliated the German war machine — "the high tide of [Hitler's] ambitions in the East, after which retreat and defeat were irreversible" ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). It also marked "the coming of age of the USSR as a military power" and the emergence of Zhukov.

**Why did Hitler want the city?**

Oil. Germany's raw-material supplies were running short, the US had entered the war, and the Caucasus oilfields were "the key to victory — without them the war could not be won" ([IWM](https://www.IWM.org.uk/history/the-battle-of-stalingrad-doomed-from-the-start)). Stalingrad itself mattered as the Volga chokepoint for Soviet oil shipments — and as the city bearing Stalin's name.

**How many died at Stalingrad?**

Britannica estimates ~800,000 Axis casualties (including missing and captured), ~1,100,000 Soviet casualties, and ~40,000 civilian dead — "one of the deadliest battles in [World War II](/events/world-war-ii/)" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](http://www.britannica.com/eb/article-9069378/Battle-of-Stalingrad)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not merge the casualty figures into a single total: Britannica and the IWM count different scopes, and each figure is attributed to its source. It does not claim Stalingrad alone decided the war — the IWM notes that El Alamein and the North Africa landings were dealing Germany fatal setbacks simultaneously. It attributes the 14,000 shot-by-their-own-side figure to the IWM transcript alone. And it treats the 1943–45 Eastern Front as outside its scope — the Barbarossa profile covers the invasion that opened the front.
