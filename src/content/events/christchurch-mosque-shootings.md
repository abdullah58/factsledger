## Overview

On 15 March 2019, during Friday prayer, Brenton Harrison Tarrant attacked two mosques in Christchurch, [New Zealand](/places/new-zealand/) — the Al Noor Mosque and the Linwood Islamic Centre — killing 51 people and injuring 89 others, 40 of them by gunfire ([New Zealand Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/sites/default/files/publications/review-of-case-brenton-tarrant-terrorist-entity-summary-1-aug-2023.pdf)). The 28-year-old Australian livestreamed the attack on Facebook and had posted a manifesto online beforehand ([NPR](https://www.whro.org/national-news/2020-08-27/new-zealand-s-christchurch-mosque-shooter-sentenced-to-life-without-parole)). He pleaded guilty in 2020 and was sentenced to life imprisonment without parole — the first such sentence ever imposed in New Zealand ([New Zealand Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/sites/default/files/publications/review-of-case-brenton-tarrant-terrorist-entity-summary-1-aug-2023.pdf)). The attacks led within weeks to a ban on military-style semi-automatic weapons, a Royal Commission of Inquiry, and an international pledge against terrorist content online.

Before the attack, he uploaded a hate-filled post to the anonymous message board [8chan](/organizations/8chan/), and he livestreamed the attack on Facebook for 17 minutes before it was removed ([NPR](https://www.whro.org/national-news/2020-08-27/new-zealand-s-christchurch-mosque-shooter-sentenced-to-life-without-parole)). The manifesto he posted immediately beforehand explicitly stated a series of ideological objectives, and New Zealand Police describe the attack as driven by extreme right-wing, Islamophobic ideology ([New Zealand Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/sites/default/files/publications/review-of-case-brenton-tarrant-terrorist-entity-summary-1-aug-2023.pdf); [Voice of America](https://www.globalsecurity.org/security/library/news/2020/12/sec-201208-voa01.htm)).

## Trial, sentence, and terrorist designation

On 26 March 2020 Tarrant pleaded guilty to engaging in a terrorist act, 51 murders and 40 attempted murders ([New Zealand Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/sites/default/files/publications/review-of-case-brenton-tarrant-terrorist-entity-summary-1-aug-2023.pdf)). At sentencing on 27 August 2020, Judge Cameron Mander received more than 200 victim impact statements, and told Tarrant: "As far as I am able to gauge, you are empty of any empathy for your victims", calling him "brutal", "callous" and "inhuman" ([NPR](https://www.whro.org/national-news/2020-08-27/new-zealand-s-christchurch-mosque-shooter-sentenced-to-life-without-parole)). The sentence — life imprisonment without parole — was the first time New Zealand had imprisoned someone without the possibility of release ([NPR](https://www.whro.org/national-news/2020-08-27/new-zealand-s-christchurch-mosque-shooter-sentenced-to-life-without-parole)). Prime Minister Jacinda Ardern said afterwards that "nothing will take the pain away", but hoped the country's Muslim population "felt the arms of New Zealand around you" ([NPR](https://www.whro.org/national-news/2020-08-27/new-zealand-s-christchurch-mosque-shooter-sentenced-to-life-without-parole)).

On the same day, the Prime Minister designated Tarrant a terrorist entity under the Terrorism Suppression Act 2002, freezing his New Zealand assets and making it a criminal offence to participate in or support specific activities of his ([New Zealand Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/sites/default/files/publications/review-of-case-brenton-tarrant-terrorist-entity-summary-1-aug-2023.pdf)). A periodic review completed on 1 August 2023 concluded the designation should continue: Tarrant "remains clearly and consistently committed to his ideology", and his actions "continued to influence the radicalisation of groups and individuals both domestically and internationally, and to inspire others" ([New Zealand Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/sites/default/files/publications/review-of-case-brenton-tarrant-terrorist-entity-summary-1-aug-2023.pdf)).

## Gun-law reform and the Royal Commission

The political response was fast. Ardern announced a ban on military-style semi-automatics and assault rifles, and Police Minister Stuart Nash introduced the Arms (Prohibited Firearms, Magazines and Parts) Amendment Bill, saying: "Every semi-automatic weapon used in the terrorist attack will be banned" ([New Zealand Government](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/tighter-gun-laws-enhance-public-safety)). The bill banned semi-automatic weapons and military-style semi-automatics, parts and magazines that could assemble or convert a firearm, and pump-action shotguns with more than five-shot capacity; it created an amnesty for handing in unlawful weapons by 30 September 2019 and a buyback estimated to cost between NZ$100 million and NZ$200 million ([New Zealand Government](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/tighter-gun-laws-enhance-public-safety)). Nash said the attack "exposed considerable weaknesses in our laws": the firearms, magazines and parts used by the attacker were purchased lawfully and modified into military-style semi-automatics through legal loopholes ([New Zealand Government](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/tighter-gun-laws-enhance-public-safety)).

A Royal Commission of Inquiry, the nation's highest form of inquiry, examined how the attacks happened and what led to them. Its report, released on 8 December 2020, found that New Zealand's intelligence services were "almost exclusively" focused on the threat from Islamist terrorism and that police had failed to enforce proper checks on firearm licences — Tarrant had moved to New Zealand in 2017 and was granted a gun licence about three months later — but concluded that, despite these failures, the attacks were unpreventable ([Voice of America](https://www.globalsecurity.org/security/library/news/2020/12/sec-201208-voa01.htm)). Ardern apologised for the failings, and the government accepted in principle the report's 44 recommendations, including a new security and intelligence agency, a new counter-terrorism minister, and funding for research into extremism specific to New Zealand ([Voice of America](https://www.globalsecurity.org/security/library/news/2020/12/sec-201208-voa01.htm)). Families of the survivors and victims felt the recommendations did not go far enough ([Voice of America](https://www.globalsecurity.org/security/library/news/2020/12/sec-201208-voa01.htm)).

The attack also produced an international response to the way it was broadcast. On 15 May 2019 in Paris, Ardern and French President Emmanuel Macron led world leaders, tech companies and organisations to adopt the Christchurch Call, a pledge to eliminate terrorist and violent extremist content online ([New Zealand Law Society](https://www.lawsociety.org.nz/news/newsroom/christchurch-call-adopted-by-many-nations/)). Leaders from 10 countries and major tech companies committed to collective action, and the Call was adopted by France, New Zealand, Canada, Indonesia, Ireland, Jordan, Norway, Senegal, the UK and the European Commission, as well as Amazon, Facebook, Dailymotion, Google, Microsoft, Qwant, Twitter and YouTube ([New Zealand Law Society](https://www.lawsociety.org.nz/news/newsroom/christchurch-call-adopted-by-many-nations/)). The [El Paso shooting](/events/el-paso-shooting-2019/) five months later showed the limits of that effort: its perpetrator cited the Christchurch attack as inspiration and posted his own manifesto to 8chan ([Cloudflare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/terminating-service-for-8chan/)).

## Frequently asked questions

### How many people were killed in Christchurch?

Fifty-one people were killed and 89 injured, 40 of them by gunfire ([New Zealand Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/sites/default/files/publications/review-of-case-brenton-tarrant-terrorist-entity-summary-1-aug-2023.pdf)). Early reporting gave 50 killed; the final confirmed toll is 51, which is also the number of murder counts Tarrant pleaded guilty to.

### What sentence did Brenton Tarrant receive?

Life imprisonment without parole, imposed on 27 August 2020 — the first such sentence in New Zealand ([NPR](https://www.whro.org/national-news/2020-08-27/new-zealand-s-christchurch-mosque-shooter-sentenced-to-life-without-parole)). He had pleaded guilty on 26 March 2020 to 51 murders, 40 attempted murders and engaging in a terrorist act ([New Zealand Police](https://www.police.govt.nz/sites/default/files/publications/review-of-case-brenton-tarrant-terrorist-entity-summary-1-aug-2023.pdf)).

### What changed in New Zealand's gun laws?

Within days, the government announced a ban on military-style semi-automatic weapons and assault rifles, with an amnesty and a buyback estimated at NZ$100–200 million ([New Zealand Government](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/release/tighter-gun-laws-enhance-public-safety)). The ban covered the semi-automatic weapons used in the attack and the parts and high-capacity magazines used to modify them.

### What did the Royal Commission find?

It found intelligence agencies were "almost exclusively" focused on Islamist terrorism and police had failed to enforce proper firearms-licence checks, but concluded the attacks were unpreventable despite those failings ([Voice of America](https://www.globalsecurity.org/security/library/news/2020/12/sec-201208-voa01.htm)). It made 44 recommendations, which the government accepted in principle.

### What is the Christchurch Call?

A pledge launched by New Zealand and France in Paris on 15 May 2019, in which governments and tech companies committed to eliminate terrorist and violent extremist content online ([New Zealand Law Society](https://www.lawsociety.org.nz/news/newsroom/christchurch-call-adopted-by-many-nations/)). It was a direct response to the attack being livestreamed and then copied and reposted at scale.

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not reproduce the manifesto or the livestream, or describe the violence in graphic detail; it reports what the perpetrator posted and what the courts and police found.
- It does not use the first-day toll of 50 killed; the final confirmed toll is 51 killed and 89 injured, 40 by gunfire.
- It does not claim the Royal Commission found the attacks preventable — it found the opposite — nor does it claim the gun-law ban or the Christchurch Call ended extremist content online; the El Paso attack five months later cited Christchurch as inspiration.
