## Overview

Operation Barbarossa — code-named for the medieval emperor Frederick Barbarossa — was Nazi Germany's invasion of the [Soviet Union](/places/soviet-union/), launched on 22 June 1941 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). It was, as Britannica states, "in effect the largest and most powerful invasion force in human history": some 150 German divisions — about three million men, with 19 panzer divisions, 3,000 tanks, 7,000 artillery pieces, and 2,500 aircraft — plus more than 30 Finnish and Romanian divisions, striking along a 1,800-mile front ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). It failed. By December 1941 the Soviet counteroffensive before Moscow had doomed it, and the Eastern Front it opened became the war's decisive theater — "the German army lost World War II on the Eastern Front" ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/operation-barbarossa)).

[Adolf Hitler](/people/adolf-hitler/) had congratulated himself on the 1939 [German–Soviet Nonaggression Pact](/events/german-soviet-nonaggression-pact/) as expediency, but anti-Bolshevism "had remained his most profound emotional conviction," joined by the hunger for *Lebensraum* ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). The invasion was originally scheduled for mid-May 1941; the unforeseen need to invade Yugoslavia and Greece that April forced a five-week postponement — a delay that "was to prove the more serious because in 1941 the Russian winter would arrive earlier than usual" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)).

## The plan and the surprise

German planning rested on two miscalculations. Intelligence estimated about 150 Soviet divisions in the west and perhaps 50 more producible; in fact the Soviets brought up more than 200 fresh divisions by mid-August — 360 in total — so that "though the Germans succeeded in shattering the original Soviet armies by superior technique, they then found their path blocked by fresh ones" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). And Berlin believed the Soviet regime would collapse from lack of domestic support within three to six months — "purely political, though no less mistaken" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)).

The attack came in three army groups: Wilhelm von Leeb's in the north toward Leningrad, Gerd von Rundstedt's in the south toward Kiev and Ukraine, and Fedor von Bock's in the center — with Heinz Guderian's and Hermann Hoth's panzer groups — thrusting at Smolensk and Moscow ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). It "took the Soviet leadership completely by surprise and caught the Red Army in an unprepared and partially demobilized state" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). Guderian's tanks raced 50 miles past the frontier on day one and reached Minsk, 200 miles in, by 27 June; 300,000 prisoners were taken there, 200,000 more at Smolensk in mid-July ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). Isolated Soviet troops fought with a stubbornness the French had not shown, blocking road centers long after the German tide swept past ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)).

Then the campaign stalled on arguments. By mid-July the Germans had advanced 400 miles and stood 200 miles from Moscow with ample time before winter — but "lost the opportunity, primarily because of arguments throughout August between Hitler and the OKH about the destination of the next thrusts": the high command wanted Moscow; Hitler wanted Ukraine, the Donets Basin, and the Caucasus ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). The diversion south produced the Kiev encirclement — over half a million Soviet troops trapped by end of September — but cost the drive on Moscow its momentum ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)).

## Scorched earth, mud, and winter

The retreating Soviets denied the invaders everything: crops burned, bridges destroyed, whole steel and munitions plants dismantled and shipped east by rail to resume production there ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). One detail decided the logistics war: the Soviets destroyed or evacuated most of their rolling stock, "thus depriving the Germans of the use of the Soviet rail system, since Soviet railroad track was of a gauge different from that of German track and German rolling stock was consequently useless on it" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). Mid-July rainstorms turned the sandy Russian roads into clogging mud that wheeled transport could barely cross ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)).

[Joseph Stalin](/people/joseph-stalin/), as commander in chief, made the encirclements worse: he "stubbornly overrode the advice of his generals and ordered his armies to stand and fight instead of allowing them to retreat eastward and regroup in preparation for a counteroffensive" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)).

By December 1941 the Red Army had suffered four million casualties — three million of them starving in German POW camps — and German hopes ran high that the war was won ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/operation-barbarossa)). "That all changed at Moscow": General G. K. Zhukov, having "coolly" amassed 17 fresh armies before the capital, launched a vast counterstrike in early December that "inflicted punishing losses on the invader" and sent the exhausted, freezing, understrength Germans reeling back ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/operation-barbarossa)). Hitler forbade retreat under any circumstances; the Wehrmacht had already suffered a million casualties on the Eastern Front ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/operation-barbarossa)). "The human cost of the war in the east, one of its distinguishing features, was determined in these opening months" — and the Soviets could replace their losses more efficiently than the Germans could ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/operation-barbarossa)).

## Why it mattered

The verdict the museum draws is blunt: for most of the war, 75–80 percent of the Wehrmacht had to be deployed in the East, and 80 percent of German war dead — about four million of the five million German soldiers killed in [World War II](/events/world-war-ii/) — perished there ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/operation-barbarossa)). Roosevelt and Churchill knew who was killing the most Germans; Lend-Lease was designed to help the Soviets do exactly that — and the Allies' one undispellable nightmare was that Stalin might make a separate peace with Hitler ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/operation-barbarossa)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Why was it called Operation Barbarossa?**

The invasion was originally code-named Operation Fritz; as preparations began, Hitler renamed it for the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick Barbarossa (reigned 1152–90), "who sought to establish German predominance in Europe" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)).

**Why did Barbarossa fail?**

Britannica lists the combination: conflicting strategic objectives within the German command, strong Soviet resistance, the harsh Russian winter for which German forces were ill-prepared, the delayed start, and the underestimation of Soviet reserves ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)). The December 1941 Soviet counteroffensive before Moscow "effectively doomed the operation."

**How big was the invasion?**

About 3 million German troops (3.5 million by the museum's count) plus ~700,000 allied troops, against a Red Army of some 5.5 million — nearly 10 million people in combat from the outset, with more than 14 million Soviets eventually called up ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/operation-barbarossa); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Operation-Barbarossa)).

**What was the Eastern Front's share of the war?**

For most of the war 75–80% of the Wehrmacht was deployed in the East, and 80% of German war dead — about four million of five million — died there ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/operation-barbarossa)).
