## The prize that almost never existed

The Nobel Prize is now treated as the inevitable summit of human achievement,
but the awarding body's own history says it was a near thing. Alfred Nobel
signed his third and last will on November 27, 1895, at the Swedish-Norwegian
Club in Paris, directing his fortune to prizes for those who "have conferred
the greatest benefit to humankind"
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/alfred-nobel/alfred-nobels-will/?ref=bambooweekly.com)).
When the will was opened after his death on December 10, 1896, it caused
controversy both in Sweden and internationally: his family opposed the
prize's establishment, and the prize-awarding institutions he had named
refused to do what he asked. It was five years before the first Nobel Prize
could be awarded in 1901
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/alfred-nobel/alfred-nobels-will/?ref=bambooweekly.com)).

The resistance was serious: some members of the Nobel family sued the
executors, arguing the prize as written in the will was too vague to be
realized and that the awarding institutions would use the funds to enrich
themselves
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).
The settlement included the rule that a prize not be shared by more than
three people
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).
Getting the institutions to accept took the years 1898–99, during which the
only one to agree was the Norwegian Storting (parliament) — decisive were a
reliable nomination system and the Nobel Institutes, funded by the Foundation
but belonging to the awarders
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).
In 1897 the New York Times itself was skeptical, writing that the donation
gave eminent Swedes and Norwegians "a task before them which will inevitably
bring to them more trouble than glory"
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).

## The will's exact machinery

Nobel's instructions were precise. His entire remaining estate was to fund
the prizes, divided into five equal parts: one for the most important
discovery or invention in physics, one for the most important chemical
discovery or improvement, one for the most important discovery in physiology
or medicine, one for the most outstanding work of literature "in an ideal
direction," and one for the person who had done the most for fellowship
among nations, the abolition or reduction of standing armies, and the
establishment and promotion of peace congresses
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/alfred-nobel/alfred-nobels-will/?ref=bambooweekly.com)).
The will named the awarders — the Swedish Academy of Sciences for physics
and chemistry, Karolinska Institutet for medicine, the Academy in Stockholm
for literature, and a five-person committee elected by the Norwegian Storting
for peace — and added an explicit wish: "no consideration be given to
nationality, but that the prize be awarded to the worthiest person, whether
or not they are Scandinavian"
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/alfred-nobel/alfred-nobels-will/?ref=bambooweekly.com)).

## The money problem nobody tells

The prize's survival was an investment story as much as a ceremonial one.
Nobel's fortune was some 33 million SEK (roughly 2 billion in today's
money), and moving it from the death estate to the new foundation required
complicated negotiations with the tax authorities of the countries where
Nobel held investments; the capital shrank slightly, from 33 to 31 million
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).
Then Nobel's single word — "safe" — bound the executors to a conservative
strategy of company bonds, Stockholm real estate, and loans to Swedish
municipalities, and taxes took a further toll (a 1904 parliamentary bill for
tax exemption failed)
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).
The Foundation is explicit: despite best efforts, the fortune decreased over
the coming decades
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).

## The sixth "Nobel" that isn't one

In 1968 Sweden's central bank, Sveriges Riksbank, celebrated its tercentenary
by proposing a prize in economic sciences; after meetings with the Nobel
Foundation through 1967, it was created — first awarded in 1969 — but it did
not become a Nobel Prize, which by definition can only be those mentioned in
Nobel's will
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).
It was instead "a prize in memory of Alfred Nobel," funded by an annual
donation from the bank covering the prize amount, the selection costs, and a
share of the festivities
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).
The Foundation accepted it as an ad hoc decision — explicitly not opening the
door to new prizes — and refused every later proposal: architecture (already
in December 1968), environmental sciences (from a member of the Nobel
family), agriculture (suggested by laureate Norman Borlaug), popular music,
and ballet
([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/were-in-this-forever-the-history-of-the-nobel-foundation/)).

## How the prize runs today

Each October the announcements run across a week — in 2025, October 6–13:
medicine, physics, chemistry, literature, peace, and finally economics
([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/10/6/nobel-prize-2025-what-they-are-when-will-the-awards-be-announced)).
Each prize is worth 11 million Swedish kronor (about $1.2 million), with a
gold medal and a diploma funded through the Nobel Foundation's management of
Nobel's endowment
([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/10/6/nobel-prize-2025-what-they-are-when-will-the-awards-be-announced)).
The prizes are formally presented on December 10, the anniversary of Nobel's
death, in twin ceremonies: Stockholm — where laureates in physics, chemistry,
medicine, literature, and economics receive their medals and diplomas from
the king, followed by a banquet at City Hall for more than a thousand guests
— and Oslo, where the Peace Prize is presented by the Norwegian Nobel
Committee chair at Oslo City Hall
([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/10/6/nobel-prize-2025-what-they-are-when-will-the-awards-be-announced)).
The four awarding institutions are the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, the
Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet, the Swedish Academy, and the
Norwegian Nobel Committee; nominations are secret for fifty years, and no one
can nominate themselves
([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/10/6/nobel-prize-2025-what-they-are-when-will-the-awards-be-announced)).
The peace prize often rewards the past year's work; the science prizes usually
wait decades for discoveries to stand the test of time
([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/10/6/nobel-prize-2025-what-they-are-when-will-the-awards-be-announced)).

The laureate pipeline starts earlier than most histories suggest. Nine
alumni of the [Bronx High School of Science](https://factsledger.com/schools/bronx-high-school-of-science/)
have won Nobel Prizes, and the school counts more Science Talent Search
finalists and semifinalists than any other high school in history
([Bronx High School of Science](https://bxscience.edu/pdf/BXS-College_Profile_FINAL_FALL_2024_2025.pdf)).
At the university level, [Caltech](https://factsledger.com/universities/caltech/)
counts 48 Nobel laureates — 49 prizes — among its faculty, alumni, postdocs,
and trustees
([Caltech](https://caltech.edu/about/legacy/awards-and-honors)).
