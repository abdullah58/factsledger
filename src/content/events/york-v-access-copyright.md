## Overview

*York v. Access Copyright* is the July 30, 2021 unanimous decision of the [Supreme Court of Canada](/organizations/supreme-court-of-canada/), 2021 SCC 32, holding that copyright tariffs approved by the Copyright Board of Canada are voluntary, not mandatory: a collective society cannot force a licence — or royalty payments — on a user who chooses not to be bound by its terms ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). The case ended a decade-long fight between York University and [Access Copyright](/organizations/access-copyright/), the collective that licences reproduction rights in published literary works, over whether York had to keep paying royalties after it opted out of its licence in July 2011 and relied on fair dealing instead. Writing for all nine judges, Justice Rosalie Abella dismissed both appeals: Access Copyright could not enforce its tariff, and York could not get a declaration that its fair dealing guidelines were fair ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)).

The dispute matters beyond one university because it reset the balance of power between copyright collectives and the institutions that use their works. It was also Justice Abella's last case heard before her retirement, capping a career in which she shaped Canadian fair dealing law ([Lenczner Slaght](https://www.litigate.com/an-ode-to-users-rights)). [Colleges and Institutes Canada](/organizations/colleges-and-institutes-canada/) and Universities Canada were among the many interveners in the appeals ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). This profile is a reference account of the case, not legal advice.

## From a $3.38 licence to a $45 tariff

From 1994 to 2010, York and Access Copyright operated under a licence agreement — a model licence negotiated by the Association of Universities and Colleges of Canada — that let York professors copy published works in the collective's repertoire for $3.38 per full-time-equivalent student per year plus $0.10 per page copied into a course pack ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). York, Canada's third-largest university, with 11 faculties and more than 45,000 full-time-equivalent students on two [Toronto](/places/toronto/) campuses, distributed the copies as course packs and through its online learning system ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)).

Renewal talks broke down in 2010. That March, Access Copyright filed a proposed tariff for 2011–2013: a flat $45 per full-time-equivalent student, with no per-page rate — over $2 million a year for York alone — drawing objections from just over 100 individuals and institutions ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). The Board granted an interim tariff matching the old licence, effective January 1, 2011; York paid briefly, then told Access Copyright in July 2011 that it would not continue as a licensee, relying on its fair dealing guidelines instead. Access Copyright sued in Federal Court to enforce the interim tariff for copying between September 2011 and December 2013, pointing to five professors and 87 works; York counterclaimed for a declaration that copying within its guidelines was fair dealing ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)).

Access Copyright's proposed $45-per-student flat rate would have cost York University over $2 million a year — more than thirteen times the $3.38 per-student rate in its old licence ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)) — which is why the question of whether a Board-approved tariff could be forced on an unwilling user mattered far beyond one university's budget.

## Why the tariff was not enforceable

The Federal Court sided with Access Copyright in 2017, holding the interim tariff mandatory and York's guidelines unfair; the Federal Court of Appeal reversed on the tariff in 2020, calling Board-approved tariffs voluntary, but upheld the refusal of York's declaration ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). The Supreme Court agreed. Section 68.2(1) of the *Copyright Act* lets a collective society collect royalties in its approved tariff and recover them in court if unpaid — but the Court held the text is silent on who the society may collect from and on what conditions, and nowhere in the Act does Parliament create a duty to pay approved royalties to a collective society operating a licensing scheme ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)).

Sections 68.2(1) and 70.17 together create a dichotomy: a person who has paid or offered to pay the royalties becomes a licensee and can be sued for defaulted payments, while a person who has not paid or offered to pay is not licensed and can only be liable for infringement ([Lenczner Slaght](https://www.litigate.com/an-ode-to-users-rights)). The regime exists to protect users from the market power of collectives, and reading tariffs as mandatory would turn them into an anti-competitive tool ([Lenczner Slaght](https://www.litigate.com/an-ode-to-users-rights)). Access Copyright cannot sue for infringement itself, not being an assignee or exclusive licensee; the remedy for unauthorized use is an infringement action by the copyright owner — a difficulty the Court called "the consequence of its freely chosen contractual arrangements with its members" ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)).

## Fair dealing, and what happened next

York won the tariff issue but lost the second half of its case. With the tariff unenforceable, the Court held there was no live dispute left, and it was inappropriate to rule on York's fair dealing guidelines in the abstract: this was not an infringement action, Access Copyright had no standing to bring one, and the copyright owners who did were not parties ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). But it expressly refused to endorse the lower courts' fair dealing reasoning. Under the two-step test from *CCH*, a dealing must be for an allowable purpose — education, added to the Act in 2012 — and then be fair, judged on six factors. The lower courts had assessed fairness only from the institution's perspective, treating York's cost-saving as a commercial purpose; the Supreme Court said the purpose of copying by teachers for students is the students' education, and money saved by fair dealing goes to that core objective ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). The amount of a dealing is judged per individual use, not in the aggregate, and large-scale organized dealings are not inherently unfair.

York said it was pleased the Court affirmed "the voluntary nature of the tariff and the ability of educational institutions to obtain license agreements from other sources" ([University Affairs](https://universityaffairs.ca/news/supreme-court-sides-with-york-university-in-copyright-tariff-dispute/)). Access Copyright called on the federal government to "remedy the untenable situation in which creators find themselves", with president and CEO Roanie Levy saying "there are no winners"; the Association of Canadian Publishers and the Writers' Union of Canada echoed the call for *Copyright Act* reform ([University Affairs](https://universityaffairs.ca/news/supreme-court-sides-with-york-university-in-copyright-tariff-dispute/)). Law professor Sam Trosow of Western University called the voluntary-tariff finding "a real weight off our shoulders", saying the opposite result "would have been an absolute disaster" ([University Affairs](https://universityaffairs.ca/news/supreme-court-sides-with-york-university-in-copyright-tariff-dispute/)). [Fasken](https://www.fasken.com/en/knowledge/2021/10/ott-newsletter-york-university-v-access-copyright) noted many educational institutions had opted out of Access Copyright tariffs and that the decision validated that choice.

## FAQ

### What did the Supreme Court decide in York v. Access Copyright?

It held unanimously on July 30, 2021 that Copyright Board tariffs are voluntary, not mandatory: section 68.2(1) of the *Copyright Act* does not let Access Copyright enforce royalties against a user who chooses not to be bound by a licence on the approved terms ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). Both appeals were dismissed.

### Why can't Access Copyright enforce its tariff against York?

Because the *Copyright Act* creates no duty to pay approved royalties to a collective society, and a licence cannot be forced on an unwilling user ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). Someone who has paid or offered to pay is a licensee and can be sued for default; someone who has not is liable only for infringement, if at all.

### Did the Court rule that York's copying was fair dealing?

No. It refused York's request for a declaration that copying within its fair dealing guidelines was fair, because there was no live dispute once the tariff was unenforceable and the copyright owners were not parties ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://rbscopyrightforum.ca/wp-content/uploads/1629323013_York-University-v.-Canadian-Copyright-Licensing-Agency-Access-Copyright-2021-SCC-32.pdf)). It also rejected the lower courts' institution-only fair dealing analysis.

### What does the decision mean for other universities?

Other institutions are free to meet their copying needs through means other than an Access Copyright licence, including fair dealing, direct licences and open-access content ([Fasken](https://www.fasken.com/en/knowledge/2021/10/ott-newsletter-york-university-v-access-copyright)). The decision validated the choice of the many institutions that had already opted out of Access Copyright tariffs.

### Was this Justice Abella's last case?

The May 2021 hearing was the last case heard by Justice Rosalie Abella before her retirement from the Supreme Court of Canada ([Lenczner Slaght](https://www.litigate.com/an-ode-to-users-rights)). She wrote the unanimous reasons, released on July 30, 2021, weeks after she left the bench.

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not claim York University's copying was fair dealing: the Supreme Court expressly declined to decide that question, and its comments on the lower courts' reasoning were obiter.
- It does not state the total royalties Access Copyright sought from York; the trial was bifurcated and the quantum phase never took place.
- It does not present the tariff as unenforceable against everyone: a user who pays or offers to pay the royalties becomes a licensee and can be sued for defaulted payments under s. 68.2(1).
- It does not claim Access Copyright can never collect royalties — it can enforce payment against voluntary licensees; what it cannot do is force a licence on an unwilling user or sue for infringement, which only copyright owners can do.
- It does not assert that Parliament has reformed the *Copyright Act* in response to Access Copyright's post-decision call; that has not been verified from a fully-read source.
- It does not treat Fasken's dating of York's opt-out to 2013 as correct: the decision says July 2011, and this profile follows the decision.
- This is a reference profile of a court case, not legal advice; the fair dealing analysis of any particular copying must be assessed on its own facts.
