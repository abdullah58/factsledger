## Overview

The German–Soviet Nonaggression Pact — better known as the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, the Nazi–Soviet Pact, or the Hitler–Stalin Pact — was the agreement signed on 23 August 1939 in Moscow by German foreign minister Joachim von Ribbentrop and Soviet foreign minister Vyacheslav Molotov, in [Joseph Stalin](/people/joseph-stalin/)'s presence ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)). On paper it was a ten-year promise not to attack each other; underneath it was a secret protocol that divided Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)). Eight days later Germany invaded Poland, and [World War II](/events/world-war-ii/) began ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)).

The pact was, as the USHMM puts it, "an agreement of convenience between two bitter enemies" ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)). Each side got what it wanted from the cynicism: Hitler could invade Poland without Soviet interference and without a two-front war; Stalin bought time to rebuild a military gutted by the 1937 purges and collected a buffer of territory ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)). It lasted less than two years — until Hitler tore it up on 22 June 1941 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)).

## Why the two enemies signed

The road to Moscow ran through Munich. The Soviet Union had failed to reach a collective-security agreement with Britain and France against Nazi Germany — most notably at the Munich Conference of September 1938 — and by early 1939 faced the prospect of resisting German expansion in Eastern Europe virtually alone ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)). The pivot was signaled on 3 May 1939, when Stalin fired Foreign Minister Maksim Litvinov — Jewish, and an advocate of collective security — and replaced him with Molotov, who soon opened negotiations with Ribbentrop ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)).

Stalin's calculation, as Britannica reconstructs it, mixed strategy with prejudice: keep the Soviet Union at peace with Germany, gain time to rebuild the Red Army officer corps destroyed in the 1937 purges, and — alongside a distrust of the Western democracies — what Britannica calls his "inexplicable personal preference for the Nazis" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)). Hitler's was simpler: a nonaggression pact would let his armies "invade Poland virtually unopposed by a major power," after which Germany could deal with France and Britain in the west without fighting the Soviet Union in the east at the same time ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)).

## What the pact said — and what it hid

| Part | Contents |
|---|---|
| Public pact | No attack on each other, alone or with other powers; no support for any third power attacking the other; consultation on common interests; no joining any group threatening either party; disputes settled by negotiation or arbitration. 10 years, auto-extendable by 5. |
| Secret protocol (23 Aug 1939) | Poland east of the Narew–Vistula–San line → Soviet sphere; Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia, Finland → Soviet sphere; Bessarabia's separation from Romania broached |
| Supplementary protocol (28 Sep 1939) | Clarified Lithuanian borders; set the Polish-German border; assigned Bessarabia to the Soviet sphere |
| Third protocol (10 Jan 1941) | Germany renounced claims to portions of Lithuania in return for a Soviet payment |

The Soviet Union denied the secret protocols existed until 1989 — "because they were considered evidence of its involuntary annexation of the Baltic states" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)).

The pact worked exactly as drawn. Germany attacked Poland on 1 September 1939 "without fear of Soviet intervention"; Britain and France declared war on 3 September, fulfilling a five-month-old promise to protect Poland's borders; on 17 September the Soviet Union invaded Poland from the east, and the two invaders' forces met near Brest-Litovsk two days later ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)). "These events mark the beginning of World War II" ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)). The partition was formalized on 29 September, with the dividing line moved eastward to the Bug River in Germany's favor — roughly the frontier that would reappear after the war ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)).

The USSR then collected its sphere: it attacked Finland on 30 November 1939 and forced it to yield the Isthmus of Karelia in March 1940; the Baltic republics were annexed and organized as Soviet republics in August 1940; and Northern Bukovina and Bessarabia were seized from Romania ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact); [USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)).

## A pact Hitler never meant to keep

Hitler "regarded the German-Soviet non-aggression pact as a tactical and temporary maneuver" and "never intended to uphold the terms of the agreement for ten years": his long-range plan was always to attack the Soviet Union and establish *Lebensraum* there ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)). In July 1940, a month after France fell, he ordered preparations for war against the USSR; on 18 December 1940 he signed Directive 21 — code-named [Operation Barbarossa](/events/operation-barbarossa/) — the first operational order for the invasion, with German authorities already intending "a war of annihilation" ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)). On 22 June 1941 Germany attacked without warning, and the pact became "a dead letter" after less than two years ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What was the secret part of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact?**

A secret protocol, reached the same day as the public pact (23 August 1939), that "divided the whole of eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence": eastern Poland, the Baltic states, Finland, and Bessarabia were assigned to the Soviet sphere ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)). Two further secret protocols (September 1939, January 1941) refined the borders.

**Why did Stalin sign a pact with Hitler?**

To keep the USSR out of war with Germany while he rebuilt the Red Army after the 1937 purges, to gain a territorial buffer, and — per Britannica — out of distrust of Britain and France plus an "inexplicable personal preference for the Nazis" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)).

**How long did the pact last?**

Less than two years: from 23 August 1939 to 22 June 1941, when Germany invaded the Soviet Union ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)). Hitler had already signed the invasion order (Directive 21) on 18 December 1940 ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/german-soviet-pact)).

**Did the USSR admit the secret protocol existed?**

Not until 1989 — half a century of denial, because the protocols documented the involuntary annexation of the Baltic states ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/German-Soviet-Nonaggression-Pact)).
