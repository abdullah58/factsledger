## Overview

The International Polar Year is not a closed chapter of science history — it is an open one. Twenty-five years after the fourth edition ran in 2007–2008, the [International Arctic Science Committee](https://iasc.info/news/iasc-news/1079-joint-statement-from-iasc-and-scar-planning-for-the-5th-international-polar-year-ipy-2032-33) and the Scientific Committee on Antarctic Research confirmed that preparatory work has started for a fifth International Polar Year in 2032–33, describing it as a "crucial new phase in a 150-year-old process." Researcher Paul Arthur Berkman, writing in *Cambridge Prisms: Coastal Futures*, has called IPY-5 potentially the most consequential iteration yet — a piece of science-diplomacy machinery aimed at an Arctic that is both rapidly warming and geopolitically contested ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2025-02-landmark-polar-sciences-decade.html)).

The series behind that announcement began in 1882–1883, when an international meteorological congress in Vienna in 1873 — inspired by the Austrian explorer and naval officer Lt. Karl Weyprecht — launched the first coordinated international study of the polar regions. The premise has never changed: geophysical phenomena cannot be surveyed by one nation alone, but require a coordinated international effort. The second IPY followed in 1932–1933, the third in 1957–1958 (renamed the International Geophysical Year), and the fourth in March 2007–March 2009 ([Hydro International](https://Www.hydro-international.com/content/article/editorial-12?output=pdf)).

## Four editions, one idea

The first IPY's most important product was not a measurement but a precedent: the setting of international scientific cooperation as a working method ([Hydro International](https://Www.hydro-international.com/content/article/editorial-12?output=pdf)). That precedent kept paying off. The third IPY — the 1957–58 International Geophysical Year — initiated the satellite era and "awakened" Earth system science, and its lessons in international cooperation provided the roadmap for the 1959 Antarctic Treaty, which Berkman describes as the first nuclear arms agreement ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2025-02-landmark-polar-sciences-decade.html)).

The fourth IPY was the largest coordinated research program ever run in Earth's polar regions. Organized by the International Council for Science and the World Meteorological Organization, with SCAR and IASC coordinating the Antarctic and Arctic efforts, it ran two full annual cycles from March 2007 to March 2009 so that both poles would be covered. An estimated 50,000 researchers, local observers, educators, students, and support personnel from more than 60 nations took part in 228 international projects — 170 in science, 1 in data management, and 57 in education and outreach ([International Science Council](https://council.science/our-work/international-polar-year-2007-2009/)).

The fourth year broke with its predecessors in two ways. It spanned an unusually wide disciplinary range — geophysics, ecology, human health, the social sciences, and the humanities — and every project included partners from several nations and/or from indigenous communities and polar residents' organizations. Education, outreach, public communication of results, and training the next generation of polar researchers were made primary missions, broadening participation "to an extent never realized or even envisioned in the earlier IPYs" ([International Science Council](https://council.science/our-work/international-polar-year-2007-2009/)). The program produced a 'pulse' of new funding for polar research and monitoring, new observational and analysis technologies, integrated system-level approaches, and organizational paradigms that were expected to leave a lasting legacy of their own ([International Science Council](https://council.science/our-work/international-polar-year-2007-2009/)).

## The first edition, and a Svalbard connection

The 1882–83 edition reached as far as Spitsbergen. Sweden's contribution to the first IPY was a research station at Kapp Thordsen — the Swedish House, or [Svenskhuset](https://factsledger.com/events/svenskhuset-tragedy), rebuilt and expanded under the mechanical engineer Salomon August Andrée for a six-scientist expedition led by meteorologist Nils Gustav Ekholm, with hourly observations made through the polar winter. Eight countries established fourteen research stations for that first polar year, twelve of them in the Arctic. The Kapp Thordsen data later fed into the Norwegian Kristian Birkeland's research into the causes of the northern lights around 1900. (For the full account of the station and its earlier, darker history, see this site's profile of the [[Svenskhuset tragedy](/events/svenskhuset-tragedy/)](https://factsledger.com/events/svenskhuset-tragedy).)

## What IPY-5 is planned to be

The 2032–33 edition is being designed as more than a repeat. The IASC–SCAR initiative aims to build a year that reflects the needs not only of the international research community but also of Indigenous Peoples and other residents of the polar regions and wider stakeholders, with backing from the World Meteorological Organization, the International Science Council, the University of the Arctic, the International Arctic Social Sciences Association, the Association of Polar Early Career Scientists, and other partners ([IASC](https://iasc.info/news/iasc-news/1079-joint-statement-from-iasc-and-scar-planning-for-the-5th-international-polar-year-ipy-2032-33)). A joint SCAR-IASC Polar Conference is planned for 2030 as a lead-up milestone ([IASC](https://iasc.info/news/iasc-news/1079-joint-statement-from-iasc-and-scar-planning-for-the-5th-international-polar-year-ipy-2032-33)). Berkman frames the stakes explicitly: "Science diplomats can broker dialogues among allies and adversaries alike simply by introducing questions," and he asks whether IPY-5 will "awaken the first International Century among its legacies" — science diplomacy turned into research-for-action across generations ([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2025-02-landmark-polar-sciences-decade.html)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim to describe the scientific content of the second IPY (1932–33) — only its dates are stated here, because the sources read in full did not cover it in detail. It does not claim the fifth IPY is formally launched or funded; the IASC/SCAR statement confirms only that preparatory work has started. It does not date the IPY-5 joint statement precisely, since the statement carries no on-page date (see openItems). It does not present the "first nuclear arms agreement" characterization of the 1959 Antarctic Treaty or the "crucial new phase" framing as established fact — both are Paul Arthur Berkman's reported framing, not an independently verified consensus. It does not claim the fourth IPY ended scientific work in the polar regions; many of its activities continued beyond March 2009 ([International Science Council](https://council.science/our-work/international-polar-year-2007-2009/)).
