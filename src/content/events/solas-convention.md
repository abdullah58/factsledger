## Overview

The International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) is the [International Maritime Organization](/organizations/international-maritime-organization/)'s principal treaty on merchant-ship safety, setting minimum standards for how ships are built, equipped and operated. Adopted in its current form on 1 November 1974 and in force since 25 May 1980, it is amended rather than replaced, and its parties flag almost 99% of the world's merchant tonnage ([IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/about/conventions/pages/international-convention-for-the-safety-of-life-at-sea-(solas),-1974.aspx); [Lloyd's Register Foundation](https://heritage.lrfoundation.org.uk/stories/from-icebergs-to-international-treaty-a-3-minute-history-of-solas)).

The Convention's main objective is to specify minimum standards for the construction, equipment and operation of ships, compatible with their safety. Flag States — the countries whose flags ships fly — are responsible for ensuring that ships under their flag comply, and a number of certificates are prescribed as proof that they have done so. Control provisions also allow Contracting Governments to inspect ships of other Contracting States where there are clear grounds for believing that a ship and its equipment do not substantially comply — a procedure known as port State control ([IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/about/conventions/pages/international-convention-for-the-safety-of-life-at-sea-(solas),-1974.aspx)). The IMO describes SOLAS in its successive forms as generally regarded as the most important of all international treaties concerning the safety of merchant ships ([IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/about/conventions/pages/international-convention-for-the-safety-of-life-at-sea-(solas),-1974.aspx)).

## Five versions, one living treaty

SOLAS has existed in five versions, each born from the limits of its predecessor. The first was adopted in London on 20 January 1914, at the first international conference on safety of life at sea, after the Titanic sank on 14 April 1912; the conference had been convened at the invitation of the British Government, and the resulting Convention involved 13 countries and tried to mandate minimum onboard safety equipment such as lifeboats, whose shortage had doomed many aboard the Titanic. The First World War halted its implementation, though many countries wrote its recommendations into national law. The second version, adopted in 1929 and in force from 1933, carried forward fire protocols such as fireproof bulkheads, whose necessity the sinking of the Vestris in 1928 had demonstrated, and it implemented many of the first Convention's protocols ([Lloyd's Register Foundation](https://heritage.lrfoundation.org.uk/stories/from-icebergs-to-international-treaty-a-3-minute-history-of-solas); [DGRM](https://dgrm.pt/en/solas)).

| Version | Adopted | Entered into force | Defining note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SOLAS 1914 | 20 January 1914 | Implementation halted by the First World War | First response to the Titanic; lifeboats and emergency equipment |
| SOLAS 1929 | 1929 | 1933 | Fireproof bulkheads after the Vestris sinking |
| SOLAS 1948 | 1948 | 1952 | Fire safety after the Morro Castle fire; creation of the IMO agreed |
| SOLAS 1960 | 17 June 1960 | 26 May 1965 | First major IMO task; covered nuclear ships |
| SOLAS 1974 | 1 November 1974 | 25 May 1980 | Current version; tacit acceptance procedure |

The third version, adopted in 1948 and in force from 1952, added fire-safety rules shaped by the Morro Castle fire of 1934 and by wartime fireproof technology; its most notable change was agreement to create a maritime branch of the newly formed United Nations, the International Maritime Organization, which would henceforth develop and update SOLAS. The fourth, adopted in 1960 and in force from 1965, was the IMO's first major task and covered the then-new technology of nuclear ships, among other modernisations ([Lloyd's Register Foundation](https://heritage.lrfoundation.org.uk/stories/from-icebergs-to-international-treaty-a-3-minute-history-of-solas); [DGRM](https://dgrm.pt/en/solas)).

## Tacit acceptance: the amendment engine

The 1974 Convention's decisive innovation was procedural, not technical. Under earlier versions, amendments could take years to enter into force because countries had to give notice of acceptance and minimum thresholds of countries and tonnage had to be met; in practice it had become obvious that entry into force of amendments within a reasonable period could not be ensured. SOLAS 1974 replaced that with the tacit acceptance procedure: an amendment enters into force on a specified date unless, before that date, objections are received from an agreed number of Parties. The new Convention also folded in all amendments agreed up to that date, so it started life as a consolidated text. That single procedural change is what has let one Convention absorb half a century of disasters and new technology without being replaced ([IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/about/conventions/pages/international-convention-for-the-safety-of-life-at-sea-(solas),-1974.aspx); [Lloyd's Register Foundation](https://heritage.lrfoundation.org.uk/stories/from-icebergs-to-international-treaty-a-3-minute-history-of-solas); [DGRM](https://dgrm.pt/en/solas)).

Since 1980, every change has arrived as an amendment, and many trace directly to a named disaster. New rules for roll-on/roll-off ferries followed the Herald of Free Enterprise in 1987 and the loss of the Estonia in 1994; the Scandinavian Star fire of 1990 prompted mandatory sprinkler or fire-detection systems aboard cruise ships; and in 2019 SOLAS added regulations on safety signage in response to the Costa Concordia incident. Technology drives amendments too: the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS) replaced Morse code for distress signalling, and the Automatic Identification System (AIS) became mandatory for navigation ([Lloyd's Register Foundation](https://heritage.lrfoundation.org.uk/stories/from-icebergs-to-international-treaty-a-3-minute-history-of-solas)).

Security entered the same way. After the terrorist attacks of 11 September 2001, a Conference on Maritime Security in December 2002 adopted the International Ship and Port Facility Security (ISPS) Code and a new Chapter XI-2 on special measures to enhance maritime security; the regime entered into force on 1 July 2004, a mere 18 months after adoption. The ISPS Code's Part A is mandatory, while Part B gives guidance on meeting its requirements ([IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/security/pages/guidemaritimesecuritydefault.aspx)).

## Scope, chapters and enforcement

As a general rule, SOLAS applies to cargo ships of 500 gross tonnage or over and to passenger ships on international voyages, with two notable extensions: Chapter IV on radiocommunications also reaches cargo ships of 300 gross tonnage or over, and Chapter V on safety of navigation applies as a general rule to all ships, except warships, naval auxiliaries and other ships owned or operated by a Contracting Government and used only on Government non-commercial service. Chapter V also identifies navigation safety services that Contracting Governments should provide — meteorological services for ships, the ice patrol service, routeing of ships and search and rescue services — and carries a general obligation for masters to proceed to the assistance of those in distress. The chapter makes mandatory the carriage of voyage data recorders and automatic ship identification systems ([DGRM](https://dgrm.pt/en/solas); [IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/about/conventions/pages/international-convention-for-the-safety-of-life-at-sea-(solas),-1974.aspx)).

| Chapters | Subject | Notable content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | General provisions | Surveys, certification, control of ships in foreign ports |
| II-1, II-2 | Construction and fire | Subdivision, stability, machinery; fire protection, detection, extinction |
| III, IV | Life-saving and radio | Lifeboats, rescue boats, life jackets; GMDSS, EPIRBs, SARTs |
| V, VI | Navigation and cargoes | Navigation safety services; stowage, securing, grain |
| VII, VIII | Dangerous goods, nuclear ships | [IMDG](/events/imdg-code/), IBC, IGC and [INF Codes](/events/inf-code/); nuclear merchant ship code |
| IX, X | Management, high-speed craft | ISM Code; HSC Code |
| XI-1, XI-2 | Safety and security | Recognized organizations, surveys; ISPS Code |
| XII–XV | Bulk carriers onward | Bulk-carrier structure; audit scheme; Polar Code; industrial personnel |

Enforcement rests on three legs. Flag States certify their own ships against the Convention's standards; port State control lets other Contracting Governments inspect visiting ships where there are clear grounds for non-compliance; and since 1 January 2016, Chapter XIII has made the IMO Member State Audit Scheme mandatory, auditing the States themselves. SOLAS also stands alongside two sibling pillars: the [International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships (MARPOL)](/events/marpol-convention/), which targets pollution rather than safety, and the International Convention on Standards of Training, Certification and Watchkeeping for Seafarers (STCW) ([IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/about/conventions/pages/international-convention-for-the-safety-of-life-at-sea-(solas),-1974.aspx); [DGRM](https://dgrm.pt/en/solas)).

## Frequently asked questions

### What does SOLAS stand for?

SOLAS stands for the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea. It is the International Maritime Organization's main treaty on merchant-ship safety, setting minimum standards for ship construction, equipment and operation that flag States must enforce for ships flying their flags.

### Which ships must comply with SOLAS?

As a general rule, SOLAS applies to cargo ships of 500 gross tonnage or over and to passenger ships on international voyages. Chapter IV on radiocommunications extends to cargo ships of 300 gross tonnage or over, while Chapter V on navigation applies to nearly all ships, with exceptions for warships and Government non-commercial vessels.

### How is SOLAS different from MARPOL?

SOLAS governs safety — keeping ships, passengers and crews alive through construction, equipment and operating standards. MARPOL, the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships, governs pollution — preventing and minimising oil, chemical, sewage, garbage and air pollution from ships. The two are companion IMO pillars with different purposes.

### What is the tacit acceptance procedure?

Under tacit acceptance, an amendment to SOLAS 1974 enters into force on a specified date unless, before that date, objections are received from an agreed number of Parties. It replaced the slow explicit-acceptance system, under which amendments could take years to take effect, and is why the 1974 Convention is still in force today.

### Is the 1914 SOLAS Convention still in force?

No. The 1914 Convention was superseded by later versions, and the version in force today is SOLAS 1974, as amended. The 1914 text matters historically: its implementation was halted by the First World War, though many countries adopted its recommendations into their own national laws.

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not assert that the 1914 Convention never entered into force: the sources read describe its fate differently (implementation halted by war, per the Lloyd's Register Foundation; "came into force later because of the war," per the DGRM), and both framings are reported instead. It does not resolve the IMO page's statement that the Annex has 14 chapters against its own listing of a Chapter XV, nor does it present the 168-country ratification figure as current beyond its source's date. It does not catalogue the 1978 and 1988 Protocols in detail, assess how well States enforce SOLAS in practice, or list every amendment adopted since 1980.
