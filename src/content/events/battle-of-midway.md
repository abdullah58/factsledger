## Overview

The Battle of Midway — fought 4–7 June 1942 near the Midway Atoll in the central Pacific — was the decisive Allied naval victory that turned the Pacific War ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333)). Six months after [Pearl Harbor](/events/attack-on-pearl-harbor/), Japan aimed to invade the atoll and lure the smaller US Pacific Fleet into a annihilating battle that would force a negotiated peace; instead, warned by broken Japanese codes, Admiral Chester Nimitz set an ambush, and in a single day the US Navy sank four Japanese fleet carriers — every one of them a veteran of the Pearl Harbor attack ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333); [National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)).

The toll, in one day: Japan lost four modern carriers, 248 carrier aircraft, and over 3,000 sailors and aircrews; the Americans lost the carrier USS *Yorktown* (sunk 7 June), the destroyer USS *Hammann*, about 144 aircraft, and some 362 men ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333); [National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)). Naval power in the Pacific was now at parity — but Japan would never recover from Midway, while American industry buried the loss under new construction ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333)).

## The trap, and the trick that confirmed it

Japan's plan depended on surprise: destroy the US Pacific Fleet and use Midway as a base to attack Pearl Harbor, "securing dominance in the region and then forcing a negotiated peace" ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)). But US Navy cryptanalysts had begun breaking Japanese communication codes in early 1942, and Station Hypo in Hawaii could give Nimitz the target's code name ("AF"), the date (4 or 5 June), and the Imperial Japanese Navy's order of battle ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)).

The confirmation was a piece of deception now taught in intelligence courses: the American base at Midway sent out a false message that it was short of fresh water — and Japan promptly reported that "AF" was short of fresh water, confirming the target was Midway ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)). Putting his faith in the decrypts, Nimitz placed two carrier groups in waiting to ambush the Japanese ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333)).

## Five minutes over the Pacific

Early on 4 June, aircraft from the four Japanese carriers — *Akagi*, *Kaga*, *Soryu*, *Hiryu* — bombed the Midway base ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)). The Japanese had no idea the US carriers lay just to the east ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333)). As the Japanese aircraft returned to rearm and refuel, TBD Devastator torpedo-bombers and SBD Dauntless dive-bombers from the *Enterprise*, *Hornet*, and *Yorktown* struck: the *Akagi*, *Kaga*, and *Soryu* were hit, set ablaze, and abandoned ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)). The Australian War Memorial's account compresses the catastrophe: "In just five minutes on the morning of the 4th of June, American aircraft disabled three Japanese carriers" ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333)).

The surviving *Hiryu* struck back twice, bombing the *Yorktown* into a crippled hulk — but that afternoon a *Yorktown* scout plane located the *Hiryu*, and *Enterprise* dive-bombers left her burning and unable to launch aircraft before she sank ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)). Over the next two days the Americans pursued the retreating Japanese; a Japanese submarine's torpedoes interrupted the salvage of the *Yorktown*, and she rolled and sank at dawn on 7 June ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)).

## Why Japan never recovered

The four carriers sunk at Midway had all participated in the Pearl Harbor attack — sinking them was, the museum notes, "a resounding defeat over the enemy fleet which had wrought such destruction only six months before" ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)). Japan could not overcome the loss of four carriers and over 100 trained pilots; its Pacific offensive was overturned, and the United States began offensive action ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)).

The reason was industrial, and the Australian War Memorial states it as a stark comparison: from 1942 to 1944 the Japanese navy would maintain six carriers — while "the might of American industrial power saw the US Navy launch fourteen carriers, nine light carriers and sixty-six escort carriers" ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333)). The Japanese were now on the defensive in the Pacific, and the initiative lay with the Allies ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333)). Two months later, in August 1942, the American counteroffensive began at Guadalcanal "and did not cease until Japan's surrender three years later" ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)).

## Frequently asked questions

**How did the US know Japan was coming?**

Navy cryptanalysts had broken Japanese codes in early 1942; Station Hypo gave Nimitz the target ("AF"), the date, and the enemy order of battle — and a faked "short of fresh water" message from Midway tricked Japan into confirming "AF" was Midway ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)).

**What did Japan lose at Midway?**

Four fleet carriers (*Akagi*, *Kaga*, *Soryu*, *Hiryu*), one heavy cruiser, about 248 carrier aircraft, and over 3,000 men — in a single day ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333)). All four carriers had attacked Pearl Harbor six months earlier ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)).

**What did the US lose?**

The carrier *Yorktown* (sunk by submarine torpedo on 7 June), the destroyer *Hammann*, about 144 aircraft, and approximately 362 men ([National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/battle-midway)).

**Was Midway really the turning point?**

Yes, for the Pacific: it ended six months of unbroken Japanese victories, destroyed Japan's first-line carrier force, and shifted the initiative to the Allies permanently — the industrial mismatch (14+9+66 new US carriers vs six maintained Japanese, 1942–44) made recovery impossible ([AWM](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/E84333)).
