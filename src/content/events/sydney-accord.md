## Overview

The Sydney Accord is the [Washington Accord](/events/washington-accord/)'s younger sibling — the 2001 agreement that extends mutual recognition from professional engineers to engineering technologists. Pioneered by the U.K.'s Engineering Council to complement the 1989 pact, it recognizes the "substantial equivalency" of engineering technology programs: a qualification approved in one signatory country is accepted by the others as equivalent to their own ([accreditation.org](https://accreditation.org/accreditation-processes/accords/sydney-accord)). Twelve bodies have signed, most recently Canada's Technology Professionals Canada in 2023. The [United States](/places/united-states/) — via [ABET](/organizations/abet/) — did not join until 2009, eight years after the founding. And the Accord's most charming feature is its title table: the same worker is an "engineering technologist" in Australia, an "incorporated engineer" in Britain, and an "associate engineer" in Ireland.

## Seven names for the same worker

The Accord's signatories agreed that qualifications are substantially equivalent — but they could not agree what to call the people holding them. The founding titles:

| Country | Title under the Accord |
|---|---|
| Australia | Engineering Technologist |
| Canada | Certified Engineering or Applied Science Technologist |
| Hong Kong | Associate Member of the Hong Kong Institution of Engineers |
| Ireland | Associate Engineer |
| New Zealand | Engineering Technologist |
| South Africa | Professional Engineering Technologist |
| United Kingdom | Incorporated Engineer |

The table is the Accord in miniature: mutual recognition is substantially an agreement about translation. An engineering technology program "approved in one country, (for example, National Diploma in Engineering, BEng Tech degree, and BTech degree) would be accepted by the other Accord signatories as equivalent to their own accredited engineering technology degree and diploma programs" ([accreditation.org](https://accreditation.org/accreditation-processes/accords/sydney-accord)).

## The late American entry

The seven founders signed in 2001: Australia, Canada, Hong Kong, Ireland, New Zealand, South Africa, and the U.K. The United States arrived in 2009. Korea followed in 2013, Taiwan in 2014, Malaysia in 2018. The eight-year American delay is an observed fact; the likely reason is structural — in the U.S., the engineering technologist sits lower in the professional hierarchy than in Commonwealth systems, where "incorporated engineer" is a protected professional title. ABET accredits the relevant programs through its Engineering Technology Accreditation Commission (ETAC), and its history page notes it "has been a signatory of the parallel Sydney Accord for engineering technology since 2009" ([ABET](https://www.abet.org/about-abet/history/)).

The follow-up went beyond education into practice: in June 2001 the signatories established the Engineering Technologist Mobility Forum, after meetings in Sydney (November 1999) and Thornybush, South Africa, to pursue mutual recognition for *experienced* technologists — backed by a decentralized International Register of Engineering Technologists meant to ease licensing or registration across borders. In 2007 the accords funded a shared secretariat, creating the International Engineering Alliance, which today spans 41 jurisdictions in 29 countries ([IEA](https://www.internationalengineeringalliance.org/about/our-history)).

## Timeline

- **1989** — The Washington Accord sets the model (engineers only).
- **2001** — The Sydney Accord is signed (technologists); Mobility Forum established.
- **2002** — Dublin Accord (technicians).
- **2007** — International Engineering Alliance formed.
- **2009** — ABET signs for the United States.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the Sydney Accord?**
A 2001 international agreement recognizing the substantial equivalency of engineering technology programs across its signatory countries — the technologist-level companion to the Washington Accord.

**Which countries are in the Sydney Accord?**
Twelve full signatories: Australia, Canada, Hong Kong, Ireland, Korea, Malaysia, New Zealand, South Africa, Taiwan, the United Kingdom, and the United States (ABET, since 2009), plus provisional signatories Peru and Sri Lanka.

**Why did the U.S. join in 2009, eight years late?**
The profile observes the delay without a sourced explanation; the likely structural reason is that the engineering technologist holds a lower professional profile in the U.S. than the "incorporated engineer" does in Commonwealth systems. This reading is flagged as analysis, not a verified claim.

**What is an "incorporated engineer"?**
The U.K. title for the engineering technologist under the Accord — the same role Australia calls "engineering technologist" and Ireland calls "associate engineer."

**How does the Sydney Accord relate to the Washington Accord?**
Washington (1989) covers professional engineering degrees; Sydney (2001) extends the same substantial-equivalency model to engineering technology programs. Dublin (2002) covers technicians.
