## Overview

The American Civil War (1861–1865) is usually summarized in three numbers — four years, 620,000 dead, several million freed. But the second number is a century-old estimate that modern research suggests badly undercounts the true toll: in 2011, demographic historian Dr. J. David Hacker published a census-based analysis concluding that about 750,000 Americans died, with a possible range of 650,000 to 850,000 — making the conflict an even larger catastrophe than the received figure implies ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/articles/american-civil-war-deaths)).

Nor did the war keep a fixed purpose. It began as a war over secession and the expansion of slavery, and only midway — with President Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation — did it become a war of emancipation. The National Archives describes the Proclamation as a "fit and necessary war measure for suppressing rebellion" that "fundamentally transformed the character of the war," recognizing the moral force behind the Union cause and strengthening the Union both militarily and politically ([National Archives](https://archives.gov/press/press-releases/2024/nr24-28)).

The war grew out of more than 80 years of sectional tensions — over economic policies, cultural values, the reach of the federal government and, "most importantly," the role of slavery within American society, according to the National Park Service ([U.S. National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/civilwar/causes.htm)). Its political trigger was the 1860 election of Abraham Lincoln, who had declared that "this government cannot endure permanently half slave and half free" — an outcome the American Battlefield Trust identifies as the catalyst for secession ([American Battlefield Trust](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/civil-war/battles/fort-sumter)).

## Secession and the opening of the war

By 1861 the country had endured decades of short-lived, ultimately failed compromises over the expansion of slavery in the United States and its territories. Lincoln's 1860 victory threatened the culture and economy of the Southern slave states; South Carolina seceded on December 20, 1860, six more states followed by February 2, 1861, and on February 4 delegates met in Montgomery, Alabama, to establish the Confederate States of America, electing Mississippi senator Jefferson Davis its provisional president. Confederate militia forces began seizing U.S. forts and property throughout the South ([American Battlefield Trust](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/civil-war/battles/fort-sumter)).

The crisis exploded at Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor. After Lincoln informed Southern delegates on April 4 that he intended to resupply the critically short garrison, the Confederate cabinet decided to "strike a blow." At 4:30 a.m. on April 12, 1861, Confederate batteries opened fire. For nearly 36 hours the two sides kept up an unequal contest before Union major Robert Anderson surrendered. Remarkably, there were no battle casualties: the only deaths came afterward, during the 100-gun salute, when a round exploded prematurely and killed Pvt. Daniel Hough. Both North and South immediately called for volunteers — the war had begun ([American Battlefield Trust](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/civil-war/battles/fort-sumter)).

## Emancipation and the transformation of the war

The war's political pivot point was President [Abraham Lincoln](https://factsledger.com/people/abraham-lincoln): his 1860 election had set secession in motion, and on January 1, 1863, as the nation approached the war's third year, he issued the Emancipation Proclamation, declaring "that all persons held as slaves" within the rebellious states "are, and henceforward shall be free." The Proclamation also opened the Union armed forces to Black men; by the war's end, almost 200,000 Black soldiers and sailors had fought for the Union and freedom ([National Archives](https://archives.gov/press/press-releases/2024/nr24-28)).

The promise of emancipation reached the last enslaved people in stages. On June 19, 1865 — two and a half years after the Proclamation and two months after Union victory — Maj. Gen. Gordon Granger issued General Order No. 3 in Texas, informing 250,000 people still enslaved there that they were free; the day is commemorated as Juneteenth. Slavery's legal end came on December 6, 1865, when the requisite number of states ratified the 13th Amendment ([National Archives](https://archives.gov/press/press-releases/2024/nr24-28)).

## The casualty count, contested

For more than a century, the accepted death toll was about 620,000 — often cited precisely as 618,222, split into 360,222 Union and 258,000 Confederate deaths. That number was not a guess: it was established in the late 19th century by Union veterans William F. Fox, Thomas Leonard Livermore and others after years of research through army documents, muster rolls, cemetery records, census records and pension records ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/articles/american-civil-war-deaths)).

Hacker's 2011 study, published in the scholarly quarterly *Civil War History*, used newly digitized census data to show that roughly 750,000 more young men were "missing" from the 1870 census than normal survival patterns would predict — a gap he attributed to the war. Many historians had long suspected the old figure was too low, particularly on the Confederate side, where written records were sparse. The American Battlefield Trust, however, continues to cite 620,000, arguing Hacker's 650,000–850,000 range is too broad, includes civilian casualties, and is not tied directly to the war years 1861–1865. Whatever the true number, the war remains the deadliest conflict in American history ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/articles/american-civil-war-deaths)).

## What this profile does not claim

- This profile does not declare Hacker's ~750,000 figure the settled consensus — it reports the debate as open, with the Battlefield Trust still standing by 620,000.
- It does not claim that soldiers fought primarily for the causes their governments stated; the National Park Service notes that individual soldiers' motives were a complex mix of personal, social, economic and political values that often did not match their governments' aims.
- It does not pin a single end-of-war date or narrate individual battles and campaigns beyond Fort Sumter — those were not verified in this research pass.
- It does not cover Reconstruction, the political settlement that followed the war, beyond the 13th Amendment's ratification.
