## Overview

The Washington Accord is the reason an engineering degree from Sydney can count in Seattle — at least academically. Signed in 1989 by six countries' accreditation bodies, it is an international agreement that recognizes the "substantial equivalency" of engineering degree programs: a graduate of any signatory's accredited program is treated as having met the academic requirements for engineering practice in every other signatory country ([Engineers Canada](https://engineerscanada.ca/accreditation/the-washington-accord)). The [United States](/places/united-states/) signed through [ABET](/organizations/abet/)'s Engineering Accreditation Commission — ABET calls itself "a founding signatory of the 1989 Washington Accord" ([ABET](https://www.abet.org/about-abet/history/)). Twenty-five bodies have now joined, most recently Bangladesh and the Philippines in 2023. The honest footnote: a peer-reviewed assessment found that with U.S. licensing boards, the Accord made "only modest inroads" in its first 25 years.

## From a bilateral handshake to a 25-country pact

The Accord's seed was a 1979 bilateral mutual-recognition agreement between ABET's predecessor (the Engineers' Council for Professional Development) and Canada's engineering accreditation board. A decade later, meetings initiated by the U.K.'s Engineering Council and ABET developed the multilateral version, signed in 1989 by the six founders:

| Founding signatory (1989) | Body |
|---|---|
| Australia | Institution of Engineers Australia |
| Canada | Engineers Canada (CEAB) |
| Ireland | Engineers Ireland |
| New Zealand | Institution of Professional Engineers NZ |
| United Kingdom | Engineering Council |
| United States | ABET (Engineering Accreditation Commission) |

The mechanism is trust-but-verify: signatories pledge to accept each other's accreditation decisions, exchange information, observe each other's accreditation visits — and submit to peer monitoring every three to six years to prove their systems remain comparable ([Engineers Canada](https://engineerscanada.ca/accreditation/the-washington-accord)). The payoff is meant to be mobility: the Accord "facilitates mobility of graduates between signatory jurisdictions" and is intended to expedite the academic portion of licensing reviews.

Growth came in waves — Hong Kong (1995), South Africa (1999), Japan (2005), Korea (2007), India (2014, Tier-1 programs only), China (2016), Indonesia (2021), Mexico (2021), Bangladesh and the Philippines (2023). The model also replicated downward: the [Sydney Accord (2001)](/events/sydney-accord/) for engineering technologists and the Dublin Accord (2002) for technicians, with all three plus practitioner agreements forming the International Engineering Alliance in 2007.

## The "modest inroads" problem

Here is the gap between the treaty's promise and the licensing counter. Anwar and Richards, writing in a peer-reviewed engineering-education journal in 2015, examined the Accord's position with U.S. licensing boards — the [NCEES](/organizations/ncees/) member boards that actually grant PE licenses — and concluded it "has made only modest inroads in its first 25 years and needs to set a much more ambitious path for the next 25 to achieve truly reciprocal mobility." In other words: substantial equivalency is recognized between accreditors, but the state boards that decide who practices have been slower to honor it. The profile states this as the paper's finding (from its abstract), not as an adjudicated fact — and it is the open question hanging over the Accord's next quarter-century.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the Washington Accord?**
A 1989 international agreement among engineering-degree accreditation bodies, recognizing their programs as "substantially equivalent" so graduates meet the academic requirements for engineering practice across all 25 signatory countries.

**Which countries founded the Washington Accord?**
Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, the United Kingdom, and the United States — all 1989 signatories. The U.S. signed through ABET's Engineering Accreditation Commission.

**Does the Washington Accord guarantee a U.S. engineering license?**
No — and this is the live caveat. The Accord covers the *academic* requirements; actual licensure is granted by state boards. A 2015 peer-reviewed paper found the Accord had made "only modest inroads" with U.S. licensing boards.

**How is quality maintained across 25 countries?**
Each signatory's accreditation system is peer-monitored every three to six years to ensure continued comparability, with mutual observation of accreditation visits.

**What are the Sydney and Dublin Accords?**
Companion agreements extending the Washington model to engineering technologists (Sydney, 2001) and engineering technicians (Dublin, 2002). Together with practitioner agreements they form the International Engineering Alliance (2007).
