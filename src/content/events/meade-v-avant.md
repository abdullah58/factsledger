## Overview

*Meade v. Avant* is the March 1, 2018 decision in which the [U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado](/organizations/us-district-court-district-of-colorado/) held that federal banking law did not completely preempt Colorado's consumer-credit claims against online lender [Avant](/organizations/avant/), and sent the case back to state court for lack of federal jurisdiction ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). [Colorado](/places/colorado/)'s regulator had sued Avant over finance and delinquency charges above state limits on [WebBank](/organizations/webbank/)-originated loans sold to Avant within two days; the court ruled FDIA Section 27 governs state banks, not non-bank buyers, and left the "true lender" question undecided ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)).

Formally *Meade v. Avant of Colorado, LLC*, 307 F. Supp. 3d 1134 (D. Colo. 2018), No. 17-cv-0620-WJM-STV ([Hudson Cook](https://www.hudsoncook.com/insights/whats-old-is-new-again.pdf)), it pitted Julie Ann Meade, Administrator of the Colorado Uniform Consumer Credit Code, against Avant of Colorado, LLC and Avant, Inc. ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). Judge William J. Martínez adopted Magistrate Judge Scott T. Varholak's recommendation and remanded the case to Denver District Court ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). Its narrowness is the point: it did not hold Avant was the true lender, that Colorado's caps applied, or that the bank-partnership model was unlawful — only that the federal court had no jurisdiction, and Avant's preemption arguments were defences for state court ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)).

## The lending arrangement at the centre of the case

Avant of Colorado, a Delaware LLC and Avant, Inc. subsidiary, became a Colorado supervised lender after a March 2013 licence application to originate instalment loans of $1,000 or less ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). In 2014 Avant, Inc. entered into a lending program agreement with WebBank, a Utah-chartered industrial bank: WebBank made loans to consumers who applied through Avant's website and, within two business days, sold them to purchasers including Avant or an Avant affiliate ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf); [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b4560b61-7957-4ba9-baab-abd52db8ad48)).

The Administrator alleged that this structure made Avant, not WebBank, the real lender, because WebBank "does not bear the predominant economic interest in the loans" ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). As the court summarised the allegations, Avant paid the start-up and legal fees, bore the marketing costs, decided who received loans, ensured compliance, and serviced the loans "even during the period before WebBank sells the loans" ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). Avant bore all default risk, indemnified WebBank, and collected 99% of the profits with the other non-banks; "WebBank's share in the profit is only approximately one percent" ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). The alleged purpose was to let non-banks "circumvent state laws" on finance charges ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). These are allegations, not findings; the court decided no facts about the true lender ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)).

A January 2016 examination found Avant charging finance and delinquency charges in violation of Colorado law, with Utah choice-of-law provisions that also violated it; the Administrator directed refunds and application of Colorado law ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). Avant answered that WebBank preempted Colorado's limits, refused to act, and was sued in Denver District Court on January 27, 2017, for violating Colo. Rev. Stat. §§ 5-2-201 and 5-2-203 (excess charges) and § 5-1-201(8) (unlawful choice of law) ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf); [Pepper Hamilton](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=e65cc6e5-de30-44b4-a10a-59498880b31d)).

## Why the case went to federal court — and came straight back

Avant removed the action on March 9, 2017, arguing federal-question jurisdiction because FDIA Section 27, 12 U.S.C. § 1831d, completely preempted the state claims ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). The argument turned on rate exportation: Section 27 lets a state-chartered bank charge its home state's rate in another state, as Section 85 of the National Bank Act does for national banks ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf); [Law360](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=bca5bf72-f640-45cb-9ddf-75723020fa42)). Since WebBank was chartered in Utah, where permissive usury laws would allow the charges, Avant said Section 27 was the exclusive cause of action ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)).

The court rejected the removal in four steps ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b4560b61-7957-4ba9-baab-abd52db8ad48)). Ordinary preemption is only a defence and creates no federal jurisdiction; complete preemption applies only where a federal statute displaces the state claim and substitutes a federal cause of action ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). The Supreme Court calls it "quite rare," recognised in only three areas — labour law, ERISA, and usury claims against national banks ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)).

| Avant's argument | The court's answer |
|---|---|
| Section 27 applies to "any loan … made" by a state bank, so preemption "travels with the loan" | Rejected: § 1831d governs what a "State bank" may charge and "does not on its face regulate" charges by a non-bank |
| *Beneficial* and *Sawyer* support complete preemption | Rejected: *Beneficial* concerned claims "against national banks" only; *Sawyer* decided express preemption as a defence, not complete preemption |
| The Administrator sued the wrong party to evade preemption | Rejected: the complaint governs under the well-pleaded complaint rule; as pleaded, Avant ran the program and kept 99% of the profits |
| Valid-when-made means a loan valid when made stays valid after sale | Rejected as irrelevant to complete preemption: it "does not lead to an inference in favor of an exclusive federal forum," and Avant could raise it in state court |

Sources: [Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf); [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b4560b61-7957-4ba9-baab-abd52db8ad48).

The court also distinguished Avant's cases. In *Krispin*, the bank was the assignee in control of the accounts, so it was the real party in interest; here "something close to the opposite holds true," with Avant as assignee and WebBank's role "ephemeral" ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). In *Vaden*, the bank set the terms; here Avant was "for all practical purposes in control" ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). Following *Ace Cash* (D. Colo. 2002), claims only against non-banks are not completely preempted even where the non-bank is closely tied to a bank ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). The American Bankers Association filed an amicus brief attacking the claims; the FDIC stayed silent — "conspicuous," its own journal said ([ABA Banking Journal](https://bankingjournal.aba.com/2018/07/regulators-silence-on-preemption-could-spell-troubles-for-banks/)).

## What the ruling did not decide

The most misunderstood part is what the case left open. The court held the true-lender issue is not controlling on jurisdiction: even if Avant proved WebBank was the true lender, that would at most support a preemption defence ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). It did not decide who the true lender was; Law360's claim that the court "embraced" those arguments goes further than the opinion ([Law360](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=bca5bf72-f640-45cb-9ddf-75723020fa42); [Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). Meade conceded WebBank was named as lender in the contracts, but argued Avant was the true lender because it did the essential work and held the "predominant economic interest"; she did not dispute Section 27 preempts claims against a bank itself — only that it does not reach non-bank assignees ([Law360](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=bca5bf72-f640-45cb-9ddf-75723020fa42)).

The same limit applied to valid-when-made, under which a loan valid when made does not become usurious when sold ([Law360](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=bca5bf72-f640-45cb-9ddf-75723020fa42)). The court ruled it had "little, if any, bearing" on complete preemption: the defence could be asserted in state court ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). The court also declined to follow or reject *Madden v. Midland Funding* (2d Cir. 2015), which rejected NBA preemption for a collector of charged-off debt, because it addressed express, not complete, preemption ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). Congress was already being asked to intervene: H.R. 3299, codifying valid-when-made, had passed the House on February 14, 2018 but not been acted on by the Senate; H.R. 4439, overruling multi-factor true-lender tests, was pending in committee ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf); [Law360](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=bca5bf72-f640-45cb-9ddf-75723020fa42)).

## The parallel cases and the 2020 settlement

*Meade v. Avant* was one of a pair: in January 2017 the Administrator also sued Marlette Funding, LLC — the lender behind Best Egg — over its Cross River Bank partnership, on the same theory ([Hudson Cook](https://www.hudsoncook.com/insights/whats-old-is-new-again.pdf); [Pepper Hamilton](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=e65cc6e5-de30-44b4-a10a-59498880b31d)). Both were removed and remanded in March 2018, the Marlette court following *Avant* ([Hudson Cook](https://www.hudsoncook.com/insights/whats-old-is-new-again.pdf)). The banks then filed their own federal suits seeking a declaration that Section 27 expressly preempts Colorado usury law; both were dismissed in March 2018 under Younger abstention, leaving preemption to be raised in state court ([Cadwalader](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/litigation-mediation-arbitration/marketplace-lending-update-%232-another-rocky-mountain-remand); [Hudson Cook](https://www.hudsoncook.com/insights/whats-old-is-new-again.pdf)).

They never got a merits ruling there. On August 18, 2020, Attorney General Phil Weiser announced a settlement of the two "precedent-setting" lawsuits over the state's right to enforce its interest-rate limits ([Colorado Attorney General](https://coag.gov/press-releases/8-18-20/)). He had alleged Avant and Marlette "illegally partnered" with WebBank and Cross River Bank "in a scheme to 'rent' those banks' ability to lend above Colorado's rate limits" ([Colorado Attorney General](https://coag.gov/press-releases/8-18-20/)). Under it, the banks and non-bank partners agreed to protections "to ensure that they are making true banks loans," a 36% cap, and Colorado licences for the non-banks; the companies paid $1,050,000 to the State and $500,000 to MoneyWi$er ([Colorado Attorney General](https://coag.gov/press-releases/8-18-20/)). By then the cases were captioned *Fulford v. Avant*, No. 17CV30377, and *Fulford v. Marlette*, No. 17CV30376 ([Colorado Attorney General](https://coag.gov/press-releases/8-18-20/)). The settlement created a safe harbor and ended the litigation without any court deciding those questions.

## FAQ

### What did the court actually decide in Meade v. Avant?

Only a jurisdictional question, decided March 1, 2018: FDIA Section 27 did not completely preempt Colorado's claims against Avant, a non-bank, so the case returned to state court ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). It did not decide the true-lender question or the caps' preemption.

### What is complete preemption, and why did it fail here?

Complete preemption is a rare doctrine under which a federal statute displaces a state claim and substitutes a federal cause of action, allowing removal; ordinary preemption is only a defence ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). It failed because Section 27 governs what a state bank may charge — not a non-bank buyer.

### What is the "true lender" question in this case?

Whether WebBank or Avant was the real lender, judged by who did the essential work and bore the predominant economic interest, not by the contract's named lender ([Law360](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=bca5bf72-f640-45cb-9ddf-75723020fa42)). The Administrator alleged Avant did the work and kept 99% of the profits; the court left it undecided ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)).

### How did the Colorado litigation end?

With a settlement announced August 18, 2020, not a ruling on the merits ([Colorado Attorney General](https://coag.gov/press-releases/8-18-20/)). The parties agreed to a safe harbor, a 36% APR cap, Colorado licences, a $1,050,000 payment and a $500,000 MoneyWi$er contribution ([Colorado Attorney General](https://coag.gov/press-releases/8-18-20/)).

### Does Meade v. Avant mean bank–fintech partnerships are illegal?

No. It was about federal jurisdiction, not the model's legality, and it preserved Avant's right to argue preemption, including valid-when-made, in state court ([Court opinion](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). The later settlement created a safe harbor for compliant bank-fintech programs ([Colorado Attorney General](https://coag.gov/press-releases/8-18-20/)).
