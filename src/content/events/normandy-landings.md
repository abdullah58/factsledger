## Overview

On 6 June 1944, Allied troops launched Operation Overlord across the English Channel and onto the beaches of Normandy, France — the largest amphibious invasion in history ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day)). In the months that followed, the western Allies drove the Germans out of France and western Europe while the Soviet Union pressed from the east, and Nazi Germany surrendered almost exactly a year later ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day)).

One number here carries an unusual honest warning: the National WWII Museum's own fact sheet cautions that "no exact figures are possible, either for the number of men landed or for casualties, for D-Day alone" — so the strength and casualty figures in this profile are given as ranges and approximations, the best anyone can do for a single day ([The National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/sites/default/files/2024-05/D-Day%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf)). The [World War II](/events/world-war-ii/) profile covers the war as a whole; the [attack on Pearl Harbor](/events/attack-on-pearl-harbor/) explains how the United States came to be planning this invasion.

## The invasion

Overlord's plan was set in motion after the United States entered the war in December 1941: with France occupied since May 1940, American and British planners began working toward a cross-Channel invasion from 1942 ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)). In January 1944 General Dwight D. Eisenhower took command, and the staging became a feat of logistics in itself — 7 million tons of supplies shipped to England, including 450,000 tons of ammunition ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)).

The Germans knew an invasion was coming but not where. In November 1943 Hitler put Erwin Rommel in charge of the coastal defenses, tasking him with finishing the Atlantic Wall — a 2,400-mile line of bunkers, landmines, and beach and water obstacles ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)). The Allies fed that uncertainty with an elaborate deception: fake equipment, a phantom army supposedly commanded by George Patton and based in England opposite Pas-de-Calais, double agents, and fraudulent radio transmissions — all meant to fix German attention on the narrowest point of the Channel and even on Norway ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)).

Eisenhower chose 5 June; bad weather forced a 24-hour delay, and on the morning of 5 June, after his meteorologist predicted improved conditions, he gave the go-ahead — with his "Great Crusade" message to the troops ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)). That night 23,000-plus American, British, and Canadian paratroopers dropped behind the German lines by parachute and glider; at 6:30am the amphibious assault began across five beaches — west to east, Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, Sword — a 50-mile stretch of Normandy coast ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day); [The National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/sites/default/files/2024-05/D-Day%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf)).

The American experience split sharply: light opposition at Utah, and at Omaha the bloodiest fighting of the day — more than 2,000 American casualties, with 34,000 Americans coming ashore there across the day ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day); [The National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/sites/default/files/2024-05/D-Day%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf)). British and Canadian units took Gold, Juno, and Sword over light opposition ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)). By day's end about 156,000 Allied troops had stormed the beaches ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)). Within days the bridgehead held 326,000 troops, 50,000-plus vehicles, and some 100,000 tons of equipment ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)).

The Germans were fighting with their hands tied by their own command. Rommel was away on leave; Hitler, believing the invasion was a feint covering a real attack north of the Seine, at first refused to release nearby divisions — reinforcements had to come from farther afield, causing delays — and he hesitated over calling in armored divisions ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)). Effective Allied air support took out key bridges and forced long German detours, while Allied naval support shielded the troops moving inland ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)).

## The beachhead and the breakout

D-Day itself was an incomplete success: the Allies failed to reach their planned objectives of linking the beachheads or driving nine miles inland, and the Germans contained them in a slowly expanding beachhead for six weeks — the Battle of Normandy ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day)). The five beaches were united into one large beachhead on 11 June; by nightfall on D-Day over 10,000 Allied troops were casualties (4,400 confirmed dead — about 3,700 British and Canadian, about 6,600 American) and the Germans had suffered between 4,000 and 9,000 ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day)).

The breakout came on 25 July, when Operation Cobra launched near Saint-Lô; by mid-August Allied troops had encircled and destroyed much of the German army in the region near Falaise, and Patton's Third Army raced across northern France, liberating Paris on 25 August with French forces taking a prominent role ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day)). By the end of August the Allies had reached the Seine and the Battle of Normandy was effectively over — a psychological blow to Nazi Germany that also kept Hitler from reinforcing his Eastern Front against the Soviets ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)).

## D-Day and the Holocaust

D-Day's meaning sharpens against the USHMM's own framing. By 6 June 1944, Nazi Germany and its collaborators had already murdered more than five million European Jews — and even as the troops stormed ashore, German and Hungarian officials were deporting 420,000 Jews from Hungary to Auschwitz-Birkenau (15 May–9 July 1944), most to be murdered in gas chambers ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day)). The museum states plainly that an Allied victory was the only way to stop [the Holocaust](/events/the-holocaust/), and that Overlord likely saved hundreds of thousands of Jews in western and central Europe — even though freeing the camps was not a primary Allied objective ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day)).

Among the storming troops were nearly 2,000 African Americans — a contribution HISTORY.com describes as "largely forgotten by history" ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is it called D-Day?**

In military terminology the "D" is simply a placeholder for the day an operation starts (H-Hour is the hour); the term has since become attached to 6 June 1944 ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day); [United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day)).

**How many troops landed on D-Day?**

Approximately 156,000–160,000 (HISTORY gives 156,000 by day's end; the National WWII Museum says nearly 160,000), supported by over 7,000 naval vessels and about 11,500–12,000 aircraft ([HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day); [The National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/sites/default/files/2024-05/D-Day%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf)).

**How many were killed on D-Day?**

Allied casualties exceeded 10,000 (killed, wounded, missing), with about 4,000–4,400 killed; the Germans lost between 4,000 and 9,000. Exact single figures are not possible ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day); [The National WWII Museum](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/sites/default/files/2024-05/D-Day%20Fact%20Sheet.pdf)).

**What happened after D-Day?**

Operation Cobra broke the beachhead on 25 July, Paris was liberated on 25 August, and northern France was cleared by the end of August 1944; Germany surrendered in May 1945 ([United States Holocaust Memorial Museum](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/d-day); [HISTORY](http://history.com/articles/d-day)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile gives no single "official" figure for D-Day strength or casualties — per the National WWII Museum, none exists. It does not adjudicate the Allied planning debates over landing sites; it reports the deception as executed. It does not quantify the French civilian cost of the campaign, which lies outside the sources read, and it does not claim that liberating the concentration camps was an Allied objective — the USHMM states plainly that it was not. The war's Pacific half is covered in the World War II profile.
