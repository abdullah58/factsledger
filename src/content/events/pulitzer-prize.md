## Overview

In 2010, the Pulitzer Prizes did something they had never done in nearly a century: they honored a newsroom with no printing press. Sheri Fink of [ProPublica](https://factsledger.com/organizations/propublica/) — a nonprofit, online-only news organization — won the Investigative Reporting prize for "The Deadly Choices at Memorial," her 13,000-word account of life-and-death decisions inside a New Orleans hospital during Hurricane Katrina, published in collaboration with The New York Times Magazine ([Editor & Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/pulitzers-2010-a-watershed-year,58293)). It was the first Pulitzer ever awarded for work from a non-print newsroom, and ProPublica was also named a finalist for the Public Service gold medal that same year ([Editor & Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/pulitzers-2010-a-watershed-year,58293)). The trade press called 2010 a watershed year for the prizes — the moment American journalism's highest honor formally admitted that the press no longer needed a press ([Editor & Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/pulitzers-2010-a-watershed-year,58293)).

## The will behind the prizes

The prizes exist because a newspaper publisher wrote them into his will. Joseph Pulitzer — born in Makó, Hungary, on April 10, 1847 — came to America as a Civil War substitute, built the St. Louis Post-Dispatch from 1878, bought the New York World from Jay Gould in 1883, and waged the circulation war with William Randolph Hearst that gave "yellow journalism" its name in 1896–98 ([The Pulitzer Prizes](http://www.pulitzer.org/page/biography-joseph-pulitzer)). He died aboard his yacht in 1911; his will endowed both the prizes and a journalism school at Columbia University, which opened in 1912, with the first prizes following in 1917 ([The Pulitzer Prizes](http://www.pulitzer.org/page/biography-joseph-pulitzer)). Pulitzer did not entirely trust the arrangement: his will provided that if Columbia's advisory board ever lost its powers over the prizes, the funds would move to Harvard instead ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/first-pulitzer-prizes-are-awarded)).

The first prizes, announced in June 1917 for work published in 1916, were modest by today's standards — $500 to $2,000 across four journalism prizes plus awards for fiction, drama, history, and biography, planned by Columbia's Nicholas Murray Butler and Frederick Fackenthal in May 1915 ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/first-pulitzer-prizes-are-awarded)). The inaugural winners included the New York Tribune's editorial on the Lusitania sinking, Herbert Bayard Swope's "Inside the German Empire," and Jean-Jules Jusserand's history of Franco-American relations — though no novel or drama was deemed worthy that first year ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/first-pulitzer-prizes-are-awarded)). The categories grew with the century: editorial cartooning in 1922, photography in 1942, and the novel prize renamed fiction in 1948 ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/history/first-pulitzer-prizes-are-awarded)).

## The medal and the money

The most coveted Pulitzer isn't a check — it's a medal, and it works differently from every other prize. The gold medal goes only to the Public Service winner, and it is awarded to a news organization, never an individual ([The Pulitzer Prizes](https://www.pulitzer.org/article/part-1-medal-all-seasons)). Pulitzer's will specified a "gold medal costing $500"; it was designed by Daniel Chester French — the sculptor who would later carve the seated Lincoln of the Lincoln Memorial — and measures under three inches across, with Benjamin Franklin's profile on the obverse and a printer at work on the reverse ([The Pulitzer Prizes](https://www.pulitzer.org/article/part-1-medal-all-seasons)). The medal is now gold-plated silver valued at around $2,000, while winners in other categories receive a cash award — listed at $10,000 in the Pulitzer site's 2016-era medal history ([The Pulitzer Prizes](https://www.pulitzer.org/article/part-1-medal-all-seasons)). The prizes are governed by a 19-member board and typically span 21 categories: 14 in journalism and 7 in letters, drama, and music ([The Pulitzer Prizes](https://www.pulitzer.org/article/part-1-medal-all-seasons)).

The [New York Times](https://factsledger.com/organizations/new-york-times/) threads through this story twice: Fink's prizewinning investigation reached readers through its magazine, and in that same watershed year the Times's own Matt Richtel won the National Reporting prize ([Editor & Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/pulitzers-2010-a-watershed-year,58293)). Old media and new shared the stage — which was precisely the point.

## What this profile does not claim

It does not give a current cash figure: the $10,000 number comes from the Pulitzer site's 2016-era medal history and is attributed as such, since the amount may have changed. It does not list recent winners or claim the category lineup is frozen — 21 is described as a typical year. It does not present Editor & Publisher's "watershed year" framing as the Pulitzer board's own language; it is the trade press's analysis of the 2010 results. And it does not claim Pulitzer founded the prizes to reward online journalism — the 2010 decision was the board adapting a 1911 will to a medium Pulitzer never imagined.
