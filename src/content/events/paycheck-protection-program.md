## Overview

The Paycheck Protection Program (PPP) was a roughly $800 billion United States federal loan program, created by the CARES Act in March 2020 and run by the [Small Business Administration](/organizations/small-business-administration/), that gave forgivable loans to small businesses so they could keep workers on the payroll during the COVID-19 pandemic ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)). It closed to new applications on May 31, 2021, having approved $799.8 billion in loans ([Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2021/june/post-mortem-of-paycheck-protection-program)), and by the end of 2022 about $755 billion of that had been forgiven ([Husch Blackwell](https://www.huschblackwell.com/getpdf.aspx?type=insight&show=97628&format=pdf)). The same speed that made it effective also made it the largest fraud target in the program's history: a University of Texas study found about 1.4 million PPP loans, worth roughly $64 billion, showed signs of fraud ([NPR / WMOT](https://www.wmot.org/2022-12-06/a-congressional-report-says-financial-technology-companies-fueled-rampant-ppp-fraud?_amp=true)).

The program worked through thousands of lenders rather than direct government payments: businesses applied to a bank or other approved lender, the Small Business Administration guaranteed the loan in full, and the loan was forgiven if the money was spent on payroll, rent, utilities and other eligible costs as the CARES Act required ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)). That design — money out fast, checks later — is the thread running through everything that followed: the repeated top-ups, the forgiveness backlog, the fintech fraud scandal, and the enforcement effort still running years after the program closed.

## Creation and expansion

President Trump signed the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security (CARES) Act on March 27, 2020, and its Section 1102 provided $349 billion for the PPP under Section 7(a) of the Small Business Act ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)). The first tranche ran out within weeks, and on April 24, 2020 the Paycheck Protection Program Health Care Enhancement Act added a further $310 billion ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)). Congress then reshaped the rules as the pandemic dragged on: the Paycheck Protection Program Flexibility Act, signed June 5, 2020, reduced the share of the loan that had to go to payroll and gave borrowers more time to spend the money and a longer maturity ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)), and on July 4, 2020 Congress extended the program to August 8, 2020 ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)).

A second round followed in the Economic Aid to Hard-Hit Small Businesses, Nonprofits, and Venues Act, signed December 27, 2020, which added $284 billion of funding for further forgivable loans ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)). The rules also tightened in the second round: eligibility for a repeat loan was limited to companies with 300 or fewer employees, and money was set aside specifically for very small firms and for businesses in low- and moderate-income communities ([Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2021/june/post-mortem-of-paycheck-protection-program)). Initially the program was open to firms with 500 or fewer employees, including contract workers, with loans of up to two months of payroll capped at $10 million, and businesses first had to spend 75% of the loan on salaries to qualify for forgiveness — a threshold later relaxed to 60% ([Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2021/june/post-mortem-of-paycheck-protection-program)).

## Scale, forgiveness, and who the money reached

By the time the first window closed on August 8, 2020, 5,460 lenders had approved about 5.2 million loans totalling $525 billion ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)); the SBA's own report put the precise figures at 5,212,128 loans for $525,012,201,124, with an overall average loan of $101,000 ([U.S. Small Business Administration](https://www.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2020-08/PPP_Report%20-%202020-08-10-508.pdf?_sp=491f8051-5ea1-488b-9abb-b8325fc0effe)). JPMorgan Chase was the largest single lender in that period, with 280,185 loans worth $29.35 billion ([U.S. Small Business Administration](https://www.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2020-08/PPP_Report%20-%202020-08-10-508.pdf?_sp=491f8051-5ea1-488b-9abb-b8325fc0effe)). The program's character changed sharply between rounds: the average loan approved in 2021 was $42,000, against $206,000 in the first round, and almost 90% of 2021 loans were for $50,000 or less ([Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2021/june/post-mortem-of-paycheck-protection-program)). In 2021, 96% of loans went to small businesses with fewer than 20 employees, 32% of loans went to low- and moderate-income communities, and community financial institutions made 1.5 million loans totalling $30 billion ([U.S. Small Business Administration](https://www.globenewswire.com/en/news-release/2021/06/01/2239756/0/en/SBA-Administrator-Issues-Statement-on-the-Closure-of-Paycheck-Protection-Program.html)).

Forgiveness was not automatic. A borrower applied through its lender, the lender had 60 days to decide, and the SBA then had 90 days to pay the lender — a deadline the CARES Act itself set ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)). The SBA Inspector General found the agency missed that 90-day requirement for loans totalling $66.4 billion, including 98.2% of loans over $2 million, and identified 1.9 million loans totalling $177.3 billion with no forgiveness application at all as of May 2021 — a possible indicator of fraud, since borrowers who obtained loans fraudulently are unlikely to apply for forgiveness ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)). The Federal Reserve supported the whole effort through the Paycheck Protection Program Liquidity Facility, launched in April 2020, which lent cheaply to PPP lenders against the government-guaranteed loans; banks that used it extended more than twice as much PPP lending as banks that did not, according to a Federal Reserve working paper ([Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2021/june/post-mortem-of-paycheck-protection-program)).

Reviewing that many loans meant automation first. The SBA screened all 5.2 million loans disbursed in 2020 through an automated tool, which flagged about 1.96 million with a hold code requiring follow-up; about 1.7 million of those were then resolved without a manual review, using data analytics and machine learning trained on 20,000 completed manual reviews ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)). Loans with unresolved hold codes — for example, ones involving a borrower's criminal record or business affiliation issues — went to manual review by government contractors, who recommended either no further action or further action on each file ([SBA Office of Inspector General](https://legacy.sba.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/SBA%20OIG%20Report%2022-09.pdf)).

## Fintechs, fraud, and the BlueVine case

The program's weakest point was how it was administered. Financial technology companies — firms using software to process lending at scale — promised Congress they could reach struggling businesses faster than traditional banks, and they did: they also reached more independent contractors and businesses run by women and people of color than established banks had ([NPR / WMOT](https://www.wmot.org/2022-12-06/a-congressional-report-says-financial-technology-companies-fueled-rampant-ppp-fraud?_amp=true)). But a December 1, 2022 staff report from the House Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Crisis, "'We Are Not the Fraud Police': How Fintechs Facilitated Fraud in the Paycheck Protection Program", found that many of these companies "failed to stop obvious and preventable fraud" ([House Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Crisis](http://coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/news/press-releases/clyburn-fintech-fraud-ppp-doj-sba)). The investigation, opened in May 2021 and expanded that November, rested on more than 83,000 pages of internal company documents and produced 11 recommendations ([House Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Crisis](http://coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/news/press-releases/clyburn-fintech-fraud-ppp-doj-sba)).

The report's examples were stark. Blueacorn received over $1 billion in taxpayer-funded processing fees, transferred nearly $300 million in profits to its owners, and spent only $8.6 million — under one percent of its fees — on fraud prevention, with a single direct employee assisting with the 1.7 million loans it reviewed ([House Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Crisis](http://coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/news/press-releases/clyburn-fintech-fraud-ppp-doj-sba)). Lenders paid Womply over $2 billion in fees for its "PPP Fast Lane" screening service, whose systems partners described as "put together with duct tape and gum", while its chief executive — convicted of insider trading in 2014 and permanently barred from the securities industry — led its fraud-prevention efforts ([House Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Crisis](http://coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/news/press-releases/clyburn-fintech-fraud-ppp-doj-sba)). Kabbage, which facilitated more than 310,000 loans, cut its risk and account-review teams by about half in May and June of 2020, at the height of the program; one of its managers told fraud specialists that "the risk here is not ours – it is SBA's risk" ([House Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Crisis](http://coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/news/press-releases/clyburn-fintech-fraud-ppp-doj-sba)). Partner banks largely delegated the checking to the fintechs, describing their own oversight as "spot checks" on a small random percentage of files ([NPR / WMOT](https://www.wmot.org/2022-12-06/a-congressional-report-says-financial-technology-companies-fueled-rampant-ppp-fraud?_amp=true)).

[BlueVine](/organizations/bluevine/) was the exception the report singled out in the other direction. It acted as a service provider — verifying, processing and supporting applications — for Cross River Bank and Celtic Bank, the direct SBA lenders, in both PPP rounds ([BlueVine Inc.](https://coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/files/documents/BlueVine_Batched_Docs_With_Redactions.pdf)). Celtic Bank conducted continuous oversight of BlueVine's anti-fraud controls and pushed it to introduce new software and manual review processes, after which fraud incidents fell steeply ([House Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Crisis](http://coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/news/press-releases/clyburn-fintech-fraud-ppp-doj-sba)). The report still faulted the arrangement: Celtic submitted late Suspicious Activity Reports because of delays at BlueVine, in possible violation of banking regulations ([House Select Subcommittee on the Coronavirus Crisis](http://coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/news/press-releases/clyburn-fintech-fraud-ppp-doj-sba)). BlueVine told the Subcommittee it received about 1.67 million applications, of which roughly 22% were ultimately approved and funded, and that it helped more than 300,000 small businesses receive $8.9 billion in PPP loans, about 75% of them sole proprietorships — figures that are BlueVine's own ([BlueVine Inc.](https://coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/coronavirus-democrats-oversight.house.gov/files/documents/BlueVine_Batched_Docs_With_Redactions.pdf)). On December 7, 2022, the SBA suspended Blueacorn and Womply from working with the agency in any capacity and opened investigations into several other fintechs and lenders, including Celtic Bank, Cross River Bank and Harvest ([NPR / WMOT](https://www.wmot.org/2022-12-06/a-congressional-report-says-financial-technology-companies-fueled-rampant-ppp-fraud?_amp=true)).

## How big was the fraud, and what happened next

No single figure for PPP fraud commands agreement, and the disagreement itself is part of the record. The SBA Inspector General estimated in June 2023 that the SBA disbursed more than $200 billion in potentially fraudulent COVID-EIDL and PPP loans — about 17% of the total for those programs — while the SBA disputed that estimate and published its own, of around $36 billion in likely fraud ([U.S. Government Accountability Office](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107267/index.html)). A University of Texas study put the PPP-only figure at about $64 billion across 1.4 million loans showing signs of fraud ([NPR / WMOT](https://www.wmot.org/2022-12-06/a-congressional-report-says-financial-technology-companies-fueled-rampant-ppp-fraud?_amp=true)). The Government Accountability Office added a structural explanation in March 2025: the SBA's four-step antifraud process — automated screening, data analytics, human review, and referral to the Inspector General — was not fully implemented until more than half the money was already out the door, with over $525 billion of an eventual $800 billion in PPP loans (about 66%) approved before the full process existed ([U.S. Government Accountability Office](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107267/index.html)). The GAO also found the referral step itself was weak: of almost 3 million COVID-EIDL referrals the SBA sent its Inspector General, about 2 million were not actionable because they lacked enough data or had quality problems such as duplicates ([U.S. Government Accountability Office](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-25-107267/index.html)).

Enforcement has continued long after the program closed. The Department of Justice reported in April 2024 that it had charged more than 3,500 defendants, seized or forfeited over $1.4 billion in stolen COVID-19 relief funds, and filed more than 400 civil lawsuits resulting in judgments and settlements ([Husch Blackwell](https://www.huschblackwell.com/getpdf.aspx?type=insight&show=97628&format=pdf)). Prosecutors were given up to 10 years to bring PPP fraud charges ([NPR / WMOT](https://www.wmot.org/2022-12-06/a-congressional-report-says-financial-technology-companies-fueled-rampant-ppp-fraud?_amp=true)), and 2025 brought fresh False Claims Act settlements of over $20 million in March and over $10 million in April ([Husch Blackwell](https://www.huschblackwell.com/getpdf.aspx?type=insight&show=97628&format=pdf)). For borrowers who simply never qualified for forgiveness, the bill eventually came due: the SBA referred more than 10,000 delinquent loans with balances over $100,000 to the Treasury Department for collection, and around $20 billion in delinquent loans under $100,000 — under 3% of the funds disbursed, but a large sum in absolute terms ([Husch Blackwell](https://www.huschblackwell.com/getpdf.aspx?type=insight&show=97628&format=pdf)). The SBA can review a PPP loan at any time, including after forgiveness has been granted ([Husch Blackwell](https://www.huschblackwell.com/getpdf.aspx?type=insight&show=97628&format=pdf)). If a borrower does not respond to an SBA audit inquiry, the agency may determine the borrower was ineligible for the loan or for the forgiveness claimed and seek repayment; a borrower who loses a final loan review decision has 30 calendar days to appeal to the SBA's Office of Hearings and Appeals ([Husch Blackwell](https://www.huschblackwell.com/getpdf.aspx?type=insight&show=97628&format=pdf)). The trade-off was deliberate: as a former Treasury official told NPR, speed took precedence over safeguarding against fraud when the program was designed ([NPR / WMOT](https://www.wmot.org/2022-12-06/a-congressional-report-says-financial-technology-companies-fueled-rampant-ppp-fraud?_amp=true)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim a settled total for PPP fraud: the Inspector General's estimate of over $200 billion across PPP and EIDL and the SBA's own estimate of around $36 billion use different methods and are reported side by side, not reconciled. It does not claim a total authorisation figure for the program, because the tranche figures in the Inspector General's report and the Congressional Research Service total cited in the site's Small Business Administration profile do not agree. Forgiveness totals are stated as of the end of 2022, not as a final figure. BlueVine's $8.9 billion and 300,000-business figures are the company's own statements to Congress, corroborated in role but not in amount by the House report, and are attributed accordingly. The profile also does not claim how many jobs the program saved: the St. Louis Fed noted that uncertainty about the number of jobs "saved" remained even as the program was widely considered a success ([Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis](https://www.stlouisfed.org/on-the-economy/2021/june/post-mortem-of-paycheck-protection-program)).
