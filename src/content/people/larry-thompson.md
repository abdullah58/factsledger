## Overview

Larry Thompson is a private Brooklyn resident who became the named petitioner in one of the decade's most consequential civil-rights rulings after a January 2014 arrest that the criminal system itself walked away from. In *Thompson v. Clark*, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled 6-3 on April 4, 2022 that a person suing police for malicious prosecution under federal civil-rights law does not have to show his prosecution ended with an affirmative indication of innocence — he need only show that the prosecution ended without a conviction ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)). The case's public meaning is the doctrine; its human engine was the two days Thompson spent in jail over charges that were dismissed without a word of explanation.

## A 911 call, a warrantless entry, and two days in jail

In January 2014, Thompson lived with his fiancée — described in the Court's opinion as "now wife" — and their newborn baby girl in an apartment in Brooklyn, New York. Thompson's sister-in-law was also staying there, and the opinion notes she "apparently suffered from a mental illness" ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)). One day that month, the sister-in-law called 911 and claimed that Thompson was sexually abusing his one-week-old daughter. Two Emergency Medical Technicians responded; Thompson denied that anyone had called 911, and the EMTs left and informed the police ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)).

The EMTs then returned with four police officers. Thompson told them they could not come in without a warrant. The officers entered anyway and, after a brief scuffle, handcuffed him. The EMTs followed the officers inside, examined the baby, found red marks on her body, and took her to the hospital for evaluation — where the marks turned out to be diaper rash, and medical professionals found no signs of abuse ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)).

Meanwhile the officers arrested Thompson for resisting their entry into the apartment. He was taken to a local hospital and then to jail, where one of the officers prepared and filed a criminal complaint charging him with obstructing governmental administration and resisting arrest. Thompson remained in custody for two days before a judge released him on his own recognizance ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)). Before trial, the prosecution moved to dismiss the charges and the trial judge dismissed the case — but neither the prosecutor nor the judge explained why ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)).

## The case for the right to sue

After the dismissal, Thompson sued the officers for damages under 42 U.S.C. §1983, the federal civil-rights statute, advancing among other claims a Fourth Amendment claim for malicious prosecution. To win that claim under Second Circuit precedent (*Lanning v. Glens Falls*), he had to show his prosecution ended not merely without a conviction but with some affirmative indication of his innocence — and he could not put forward substantial evidence of why the prosecutor or the court had dismissed the case, because no one had said ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)). The district court ruled for the officers while noting the precedent "can and should be changed"; the Second Circuit adhered to it and affirmed (794 Fed. Appx. 140 (2020)) ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)).

The Supreme Court granted certiorari to resolve a split among the Courts of Appeals and heard argument on October 12, 2021 ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)). Thompson's attorney in the case was Amir Ali, executive director of the MacArthur Justice Center, who told *Reason* that requiring a showing of innocence made no sense where dismissed charges leave no record at all: "When charges are dismissed, you generally have no opportunity to introduce evidence, let alone indicate your innocence" ([Reason](https://reason.com/2022/04/05/supreme-court-sue-police-false-charges-malicious-prosecution-brett-kavanaugh/printer/)).

On April 4, 2022, Justice Kavanaugh delivered the 6-3 majority opinion — joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Breyer, Sotomayor, Kagan, and Barrett — holding that a plaintiff "need only show that his prosecution ended without a conviction," a reading consistent with the American tort-law consensus as of 1871 and with "the values and purposes" of the Fourth Amendment ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)). The majority added that requiring an affirmative indication of innocence would paradoxically foreclose claims where the government's case was weakest and dismissed without explanation — which "would make little sense" ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)). Justice Alito dissented, joined by Justices Thomas and Gorsuch ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)).

The Court reversed the Second Circuit and remanded, but it did not hand Thompson an outright win: it expressed no view on whether Thompson had been "seized" under the Fourth Amendment, whether the officers had probable cause to enter his home without a warrant, or whether any officer was entitled to qualified immunity ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://badfaithprosecution.wiki/documents/opinions/thompson-clark.pdf)). As Ali put it to *Reason*, even after the ruling Thompson still had to convince the Second Circuit the officers lacked probable cause and overcome qualified immunity — "such claims are no longer dead on arrival," but they still faced "many barriers to getting before a jury" ([Reason](https://reason.com/2022/04/05/supreme-court-sue-police-false-charges-malicious-prosecution-brett-kavanaugh/printer/)). His case became the vehicle through which the federal standard itself changed, even as the human detail at its center — two unexplained days in jail — stayed intact.
