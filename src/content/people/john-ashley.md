## Overview

Colonel John Ashley (1709–1802) was the [Sheffield, Massachusetts](/places/sheffield-massachusetts/) landowner and judge who chaired the
January 1773 committee that drafted the [Sheffield Resolves](/events/sheffield-declaration/) — the
colonial petition declaring mankind "equal, free, and independent" — and who was also Sheffield's
largest enslaver. In 1781, two people he enslaved, [Elizabeth Freeman](/people/elizabeth-freeman/) (then called Bett) and [Brom](/people/brom-sheffield/), sued
him for their freedom under the new [Massachusetts Constitution](/events/massachusetts-constitution/) and won
([The Trustees of Reservations](https://thetrustees.org/content/ashley-house-history/);
[National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/elizabeth-freeman-her-case-for-freedom-and-the-massachusetts-constitution)).

Read the dates carefully, because two John Ashleys are routinely confused. This profile is about the
Colonel, who lived from 1709 to 1802
([Decorative Arts Trust](https://decorativeartstrust.org/ashley-house-article/)). It is **not** about
his son, General John Ashley Jr., who lived from 1736 to 1799 and married Mary Ballantine in 1769
([Decorative Arts Trust](https://decorativeartstrust.org/ashley-house-article/)). Any source that calls
a "Colonel John Ashley" born in 1736 has merged the father with the son. The Colonel built the Ashley
House in 1735 for his wife, Hannah Hogeboom
([The Trustees of Reservations](https://thetrustees.org/place/ashley-house/)), and he, not his son,
is the Ashley whom Freeman and Brom sued.

## Land, business, and public office

Ashley was, in the words of a 2025 journal study, the wealthiest and at times the most influential man
in the rural Berkshire town of Sheffield, and his working life wore many hats: gentleman farmer,
innholder, judge, lawyer, mill owner, selectman, and shopkeeper
([Commonplace](https://commonplace.online/article/glimpses-of-their-lives/)). The surviving Ashley
family account books show the base beneath those titles — general stores, a grist mill, an ironworks, a
sawmill, and farming operations, with daily business recorded from 1768 onward
([Commonplace](https://commonplace.online/article/glimpses-of-their-lives/)). He also served as a judge
of the Berkshire Court of Common Pleas
([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/elizabeth-freeman-her-case-for-freedom-and-the-massachusetts-constitution)).
In his own home, John and Hannah raised four children and held at least eight people in bondage
([The Trustees of Reservations](https://thetrustees.org/content/ashley-house-history/)). By his death
in 1802 he had amassed wealth and land of over 3,000 acres — a figure that rests on a single tier3
source among those fully read here — and the land that is now Bartholomew's Cobble was originally part
of his holdings
([Berkshire 18th Century Trail](https://berkshire18thcenturytrail.org/?p=102)).

## The Sheffield Resolves and the contradiction at the center

In January 1773, eleven Sheffield men drafted the town's grievances against Britain, possibly in the
Ashley House's newly remodeled upstairs study. Ashley chaired the committee, while the young lawyer
[Theodore Sedgwick](/people/theodore-sedgwick/) wrote the Resolves declaring that "mankind in a state of nature are equal, free, and
independent"
([The Trustees of Reservations](https://thetrustees.org/content/ashley-house-history/)). The same
equality language later reappeared in the [Declaration of Independence](/events/declaration-of-independence/) of 1776 and in the Massachusetts
Constitution of 1780
([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/elizabeth-freeman-her-case-for-freedom-and-the-massachusetts-constitution)).
The Trustees, who steward the house today, describe the contradiction bluntly: Ashley became a wealthy
and powerful man whose success relied largely on slavery, yet he "hypocritically joined calls declaring
mankind equal, free, and independent of each other"
([The Trustees of Reservations](https://thetrustees.org/place/ashley-house/)). Freeman, who served in
the household, overheard Ashley and his dinner guests discussing those very principles of liberty and
equality
([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/elizabeth-freeman-her-case-for-freedom-and-the-massachusetts-constitution)).

## Enslaver: the household Freeman sued

The Trustees call Ashley the largest enslaver in Sheffield, and possibly in all of Berkshire County
([The Trustees of Reservations](https://thetrustees.org/content/ashley-house-history/)). Older summaries
say he held five enslaved people, but the surviving records support a fuller and more careful count. In
1771, the Colonel owned five enslaved people over the age of fourteen, while his son General John
Ashley Jr. enslaved three others over fourteen
([Commonplace](https://commonplace.online/article/glimpses-of-their-lives/)). Recent scholarship names
seven people enslaved by the Colonel, and it warns that the record is uneven: no surviving account book
mentions the enslaved labor of any women in either Ashley household, so women's work is systematically
under-recorded
([Decorative Arts Trust](https://decorativeartstrust.org/ashley-house-article/);
[Commonplace](https://commonplace.online/article/glimpses-of-their-lives/)). The older five-name list —
John, Zack, Harry, Brom, and Bett — still appears in several summaries
([People Not Property](https://peoplenotproperty.hudsonvalley.org/ashley-house.php)).

## The freedom suit and its aftermath

Freeman sought out Sedgwick, who brought the case on constitutional grounds: the Constitution's
equality language meant slavery was outlawed, and Freeman and Brom were not Ashley's legitimate
property
([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/elizabeth-freeman-her-case-for-freedom-and-the-massachusetts-constitution)).
He first obtained a writ of replevin ordering Ashley to release them; the Colonel refused
([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/elizabeth-freeman-her-case-for-freedom-and-the-massachusetts-constitution)).
Argued on 21 August 1781, the case ended the next day with a jury verdict for Brom and Bett: 30
shillings damages each, plus nearly six pounds in costs against Ashley
([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/elizabeth-freeman-her-case-for-freedom-and-the-massachusetts-constitution);
[Mass Moments](https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/jury-decides-in-favor-of-elizabeth-mum-bett-freeman.html)).
Ashley appealed, then dropped the appeal that October
([Mass Moments](https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/jury-decides-in-favor-of-elizabeth-mum-bett-freeman.html)).
He asked Freeman several times to return as a paid employee; she declined and went to work for the
Sedgwicks
([Mass Moments](https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/jury-decides-in-favor-of-elizabeth-mum-bett-freeman.html)).
The litigation continued through the Quock Walker cases, culminating in 1783 when Chief Justice
William Cushing charged that slavery was incompatible with the constitution
([Boston Bar Association](https://bostonbar.org/news/the-landmark-legal-battles-that-abolished-slavery-in-massachusetts/)).
Ashley died in 1802; his will provided for Harry, Zach Mullen, and John Sheldon — no other white
person's will in Sheffield did this for people they formerly enslaved
([Commonplace](https://commonplace.online/article/glimpses-of-their-lives/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Was this John Ashley born in 1736?** No. The Colonel lived from 1709 to 1802. The dates 1736 to
1799 belong to his son, General John Ashley Jr., who married Mary Ballantine in 1769. The house-builder,
committee chair, and defendant of 1781 was the father, not the son
([Decorative Arts Trust](https://decorativeartstrust.org/ashley-house-article/)).

**Why is Ashley remembered at all?** Because his house hosted the drafting of the Sheffield Resolves in
1773, and because his household produced the freedom suit testing the new constitution's equality
language. He is remembered less as a hero than as the central figure in a contradiction — liberty
rhetoric above stairs, bondage below
([The Trustees of Reservations](https://thetrustees.org/place/ashley-house/)).

**How many people did Ashley enslave?** No single verified total exists. In 1771 he owned five enslaved
people over fourteen, his son three more, and recent scholarship names seven people enslaved by the
Colonel. Older "five" summaries and the Trustees' "at least eight" count different households and age
cutoffs ([Commonplace](https://commonplace.online/article/glimpses-of-their-lives/)).

**What happened to the Ashley House?** The Ashley family sold it in 1838. In 1930 an Ashley descendant
moved it about a quarter mile and restored it. It opened as a museum in 1959, and the Trustees of
Reservations have been its stewards since 1972. The house is closed for tours until Spring 2027
([The Trustees of Reservations](https://thetrustees.org/content/ashley-house-history/)).

**Did Ashley ever free anyone himself?** No manumission by Ashley is documented in the sources read
for this profile. Freeman and Brom won freedom by suing him, Zach Mullen by settling his own 1781 suit,
and Adam Mullen by gaining freedom that year. In his will, Ashley provided for Harry, Zach Mullen, and
John Sheldon ([Commonplace](https://commonplace.online/article/glimpses-of-their-lives/)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim that the Colonel was born in 1736 or died in 1799 — those are his son's
dates. It does not state Ashley's exact birth date, his college, or the exact years of his judgeship,
because those details could not be verified from a fully-read source in this pass. It does not present a
single definitive total for the people he enslaved, because the sources count different households, age
groups, and record sets. It does not claim that the 1781 verdict alone ended slavery in [Massachusetts](/places/massachusetts/);
the end came through the following Quock Walker litigation and Chief Justice Cushing's 1783 charge. It
does not claim that the Sheffield Resolves directly shaped Jefferson's wording — only that the same
natural-rights language appears in both, in that order. And it does not resolve whether Lizzie, the woman
in the hot-shovel incident, was Bett's sister or daughter, because the sources themselves leave that
uncertain.
