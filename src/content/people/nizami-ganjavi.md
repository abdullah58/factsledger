## Overview

Nizami Ganjavi (c. 1141–1209) was a Persian poet born, lifelong resident, and buried figure of [Ganja](/places/ganja/), in what is now Azerbaijan, whose five-poem cycle the Khamsa made him the greatest romantic epic poet in Persian literature ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nezami)). Writing in masnavi rhymed couplets across about 30,000 couplets, he brought a colloquial and realistic style to the Persian epic and became the first great dramatic poet of Persian literature ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nezami)).

Little is known of his personal life, and almost everything that is known comes from his own poetry rather than from court records, because he did not serve a royal court ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). His reputation rests on the Khamsa: the didactic Makhzan al-Asrar, the romances Khosrow and Shirin, Layla and Majnun, and Haft Paykar, and the Alexander poem Eskandarnameh ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nezami)). Two copies of the Khamseh preserved in Iran were inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2011, and the poems are still read, analysed, and translated today ([IBNA](https://en.ibna.ir/news/507630/Eminent-Persian-poet-Nezami-Ganjavi-commemorated); [World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)).

## Life in Ganja

Nizami was born in Ganja around 1141, in the Seljuq empire, and died there in 1209, having spent his entire life in the city and leaving it only once to meet the ruling prince ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nezami)). Britannica records that he was orphaned at a young age, yet distinguished by a simple life and straightforward character despite the patronage of rulers and princes ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nezami)). He lived under the Seljuq Dynasty, dated c. 1037–1194, a vast Turkish tribal confederation then ruling over Persia, in a period of conspicuous cultural growth ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). His contemporaries included Khaqani Shirvani, Awhad al-Din Anvari, and Farid al-Din Attar, all eminent in Persian literature ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). Because he did not serve the royal court, his patrons were local Seljuq princes ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). He was married and widowed three times, but only one wife's name, Afaq, is known, and one theory holds that Khosrow and Shirin was a tribute to her ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)).

## The Khamsa

The Khamsa, also called the Quintet or Panj Ganj, is Nizami's best-known work and one of the most prominent works of Persian literature, written during the last decades of the 12th century with five poems and approximately 30,000 couplets ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). It is a pentalogy in masnavi verse (rhymed couplets), and its five poems are Makhzan al-Asrar, Khosrow and Shirin, Layla and Majnun, Haft Peykar, and Eskandarnameh ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nezami)). Makhzan al-Asrar, the first and shortest poem, is a collection of tales with spiritual and moral lessons that set the pattern for the introductory chapters of Nizami's later masnavis and of almost all masnavis written thereafter ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). Those introductions move through praise of God, praise of the Prophet Muhammad and his ascension, a prayer for the author, praise of the ruler, and an account of why the poem was created ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). Only a handful of Nizami's shorter qasidahs and ghazals survive, so the Khamsa carries his reputation ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nezami)).

## What made Nizami's romances different

Nizami drew on Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, Ayyuqi's Varqa and Gulshah, and Gorgani's Vis and Ramin, yet avoided mere imitation and created new material in his own style ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). Britannica likewise traces his inspiration to Ferdowsi and Sana'i and calls him the first great dramatic poet of Persian literature ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nezami)). In Khosrow and Shirin he shifted emphasis from the historical events Ferdowsi had covered toward the romantic relationship itself, in a poem of 100 chapters, described as the first poem in Persian literature to achieve structural and artistic unity ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). In Layla and Majnun, Qays is afflicted by the madness of love and is known as Majnun, meaning madman, while the lovers remain separated for life yet endure as emblems of strong, passionate, and true love ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). Haft Paykar follows Bahram-e Gur through his fight for the throne, visits to seven princesses in seven pavilions of seven colours, and return to order after pleasure distracted him from ruling ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)).

## Legacy and modern commemoration

Nizami's influence reached beyond poetry into painting: the Khamsa's storylines and language gave Persian miniature painting rich material and made it one of the most frequently illustrated works of Persian literature, alongside Ferdowsi's Shahnameh ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). Britannica records that he is admired in Persian-speaking lands for originality and clarity of style, though his love of language and of philosophical and scientific learning makes his work difficult for the average reader ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Nezami)). The Eskandarnameh, his final poem, divides into the Sharafnameh, relating Alexander's conquest from Macedonia to India, and the Iqbalnameh, following his intellectual growth into a sage ([World History Encyclopedia](http://worldhistory.org/Khamsa_of_Nizami/)). Modern commemoration has treated him as a shared inheritance: organisers described his role as a common cultural heritage for Iranians and Persian-speaking neighbours, and introduced the Ganjavi Award for the best doctoral dissertations in Nezami studies ([IBNA](https://en.ibna.ir/news/507630/Eminent-Persian-poet-Nezami-Ganjavi-commemorated)). Two Khamseh copies held at the Central Library of the University of Tehran and the Shahid Motahhari library in Tehran were registered by UNESCO in 2011 ([IBNA](https://en.ibna.ir/news/507630/Eminent-Persian-poet-Nezami-Ganjavi-commemorated)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Who was Nizami Ganjavi?**
Nizami Ganjavi (c. 1141–1209) was a Persian poet of Ganja, in what is now Azerbaijan, regarded as the greatest romantic epic poet in Persian literature. He spent almost his entire life in Ganja, did not serve a royal court, and built his reputation on the five-poem Khamsa in rhymed couplets.

**What is the Khamsa?**
The Khamsa, also called the Quintet or Panj Ganj, is Nizami's five-poem cycle of about 30,000 couplets in masnavi rhymed verse. It contains Makhzan al-Asrar, Khosrow and Shirin, Layla and Majnun, Haft Paykar, and Eskandarnameh, written during the last decades of the 12th century.

**What is Khosrow and Shirin about?**
Khosrow and Shirin narrates the love affair between Khosrow II of the Sassanian Empire and the Armenian princess Shirin. Ferdowsi had treated the story earlier in the Shahnameh, but Nizami centred the poem on the romantic relationship itself rather than on historical events.

**What is Layla and Majnun about?**
Layla and Majnun tells of Qays and Layla, who fall in love after meeting at school. Qays is consumed by the madness of love and becomes known as Majnun, meaning madman. The lovers are separated after their love is revealed and remain apart for life, yet endure as emblems of true love.

**Why is Nizami still commemorated?**
Nizami is commemorated because the Khamsa is still read, analysed, and translated, and because it shaped Persian miniature painting and later literature. Two Khamseh copies in Tehran were inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2011, and a Ganjavi Award now honours doctoral work in Nezami studies.
