## Overview

Doug Burgum is the 55th United States Secretary of the Interior, in office since 1 February 2025, after two terms as governor of North Dakota (2016–2024) and a software career that made him wealthy when [Microsoft](/organizations/microsoft/) bought Great Plains Software for $1.1 billion in 2001 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)). He chairs the administration's National Energy Council and has made expanding fossil-fuel production on federal lands his signature mission, a stance now tested most visibly in the fight over the Greater Chaco oil-and-gas buffer ([Associated Press](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/30696819/senate-confirms-doug-burgum-as-interior-secretary-after-trump-tasked-him-to-boost-drilling)).

Burgum's path to that mission runs through a small North Dakota farming town, a mortgaged farm, and a software fortune — and then through eight years running one of America's most oil-dependent states ([Miller Center](https://millercenter.org/doug-burgum-2025)). That combination, businessman turned governor turned energy secretary, is what made him [Donald Trump](/people/donald-trump/)'s pick to deliver "energy dominance" — and what puts him at the centre of a dispute in which the same department must answer both to Tribal nations that want the Chaco landscape left alone and to a Navajo Nation government that wants the buffer gone ([KJZZ](https://www.ksut.org/news/2026-07-24/in-search-of-oil-and-gas-trump-could-axe-biden-era-buffer-around-chaco-canyon)).

## From Arthur to Microsoft

Burgum was born on 1 August 1956 in Arthur, North Dakota, one of three sons of Katherine (née Kilbourne) and Joey Burgum, and grew up on a farm where the family operated a grain elevator ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)). His father, a Second World War veteran, died of a brain tumour when Burgum was a high-school freshman ([Miller Center](https://millercenter.org/doug-burgum-2025)). At North Dakota State University he served as student-body president and, in his senior year, started a chimney-sweeping business in Fargo, graduating in 1978 with a bachelor's degree in university studies ([Miller Center](https://millercenter.org/doug-burgum-2025)). He then earned an MBA from Stanford University's Graduate School of Business in 1980 and worked as a management consultant at McKinsey & Company in Chicago ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)).

The bet that made his career came in 1983, when he mortgaged a section of farmland his father had left him and invested $250,000 in Great Plains Software, a fledgling Fargo company developing accounting software ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)). Stanford's business school tells the same story as a "bet the farm" moment, noting that the company grew to more than 2,000 employees before its acquisition ([Stanford Graduate School of Business](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/alumni/news/catalyst/doug-burgum-mba-80)). Burgum led the company through its public offering in 1997 and its sale to Microsoft for $1.1 billion in stock in 2001, then spent six years at Microsoft as a senior vice president ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)). Afterwards he founded the Fargo real-estate firm Kilbourne Group and co-founded Arthur Ventures, a venture-capital firm investing in software companies, and in 2009 received the Theodore Roosevelt Rough Rider Award, North Dakota's highest citizen honour ([North Dakota State Historical Society](https://history.nd.gov/exhibits/governors/governors33.html)). He also chaired the boards of SuccessFactors and later Atlassian, the project-management software firm ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)).

## Governor of North Dakota and the 2024 presidential run

Burgum entered politics with no prior experience in elected office, winning the North Dakota governorship in 2016 after beating the party-endorsed candidate in the [Republican Party](/organizations/republican-party/) primary and taking nearly 77 percent of the general-election vote ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)). He took office on 15 December 2016, as Dakota Access Pipeline protests were under way on the Standing Rock Reservation, and went on to lead the state through drought, flooding and the COVID-19 pandemic ([North Dakota Monitor](https://northdakotamonitor.com/2024/01/22/gov-doug-burgum-not-running-for-third-term/)). He was re-elected in 2020 with more than 69 percent of the vote ([North Dakota Monitor](https://northdakotamonitor.com/2024/01/22/gov-doug-burgum-not-running-for-third-term/)).

As governor he pursued major income-tax cuts and increased energy production, in a state the North Dakota Monitor ranks as America's third-largest oil producer, behind Texas and New Mexico ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum); [North Dakota Monitor](https://northdakotamonitor.com/2024/01/22/gov-doug-burgum-not-running-for-third-term/)). At the same time he broke with his party's usual posture on the environment: he established the state's Department of Environmental Quality in 2017 and in 2021 launched an initiative to make North Dakota carbon neutral by 2030 — pursued through carbon-capture technology rather than cuts to fossil-fuel production ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)). His social-issue record moved in the other direction late in his tenure: in 2023 he signed a near-total ban on abortions and backed bills restricting transgender girls and women from female school sports teams ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)). His stated governing motto, "innovation, not regulation," summed up an approach he himself described as driven by data rather than ideology ([North Dakota Monitor](https://northdakotamonitor.com/2024/01/22/gov-doug-burgum-not-running-for-third-term/)).

In June 2023 Burgum announced a run for the Republican Party presidential nomination, emphasising his business background and economic record, but his poll numbers never rose above single digits and he dropped out at the end of the year ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)). On the eve of the Iowa caucuses he endorsed Donald Trump, then advised Trump's campaign on energy, and during 2024 he was widely discussed as a possible running mate before Trump selected [JD Vance](/people/jd-vance/) ([Miller Center](https://millercenter.org/doug-burgum-2025)). In January 2024 he announced he would not seek a third term as governor, citing family, and his term ended in December 2024 ([North Dakota Monitor](https://northdakotamonitor.com/2024/01/22/gov-doug-burgum-not-running-for-third-term/)).

## Secretary of the Interior: energy dominance and the Chaco test

After Trump won the 2024 election he nominated Burgum as Secretary of the Interior, and the [US Senate](/organizations/us-senate/) confirmed him on 30 January 2025 by 79 votes to 18, with more than half of Senate Democrats joining all 53 Republicans; he took office on 1 February 2025 ([Associated Press](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/30696819/senate-confirms-doug-burgum-as-interior-secretary-after-trump-tasked-him-to-boost-drilling); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Doug-Burgum)). Trump also picked him to chair a new National Energy Council tasked with achieving American "energy dominance," and gave him a seat on the National Security Council — a first for an interior secretary ([Associated Press](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/30696819/senate-confirms-doug-burgum-as-interior-secretary-after-trump-tasked-him-to-boost-drilling)). His directive, as the Associated Press reported it, was to make it easier for energy companies to tap fossil-fuel resources, including on public lands ([Associated Press](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/30696819/senate-confirms-doug-burgum-as-interior-secretary-after-trump-tasked-him-to-boost-drilling)). The scale of that brief is large: the [Interior Department](/organizations/us-department-of-the-interior/) has jurisdiction over a half-billion acres of federal land and vast offshore areas that produce about one-quarter of American oil each year, and the secretary oversees the [National Park Service](/organizations/national-park-service/), the Fish and Wildlife Service, the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the Bureau of Land Management ([Associated Press](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/30696819/senate-confirms-doug-burgum-as-interior-secretary-after-trump-tasked-him-to-boost-drilling)). At his confirmation hearing Burgum told senators that energy development could be used as leverage to promote peace and lower consumer costs, raised doubts about the reliability of renewable power, and argued for more electricity from coal and nuclear sources that can run constantly ([Associated Press](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/30696819/senate-confirms-doug-burgum-as-interior-secretary-after-trump-tasked-him-to-boost-drilling)). The same Associated Press reporting notes a tension in his record: as governor he eagerly assisted the energy industry while also profiting from the lease of family land to oil companies, according to records the agency obtained ([Associated Press](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/30696819/senate-confirms-doug-burgum-as-interior-secretary-after-trump-tasked-him-to-boost-drilling)).

No file on Burgum's desk illustrates the tension better than Greater Chaco. In 2023, under President [Joe Biden](/people/joe-biden/), the federal government issued Public Land Order No. 7923, withdrawing federal lands within 10 miles of Chaco Culture National Historical Park from new oil and gas development for 20 years, in a landscape sacred to Pueblo peoples and recognised by [UNESCO](/organizations/unesco/) as a World Heritage Site ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/in-the-news/source-new-mexico-nm-delegation-presses-feds-on-chaco-protections); [KJZZ](https://www.ksut.org/news/2026-07-24/in-search-of-oil-and-gas-trump-could-axe-biden-era-buffer-around-chaco-canyon)). In July 2026 Burgum's department published a 140-page draft environmental assessment proposing to revoke that order, after a weeklong April scoping window that drew roughly 70,000 comments, and allowed a further 14 days of comment ending 29 July 2026 ([KJZZ](https://www.ksut.org/news/2026-07-24/in-search-of-oil-and-gas-trump-could-axe-biden-era-buffer-around-chaco-canyon)). The response was far larger than the window: the Bureau of Land Management received more than 200,000 comments on the draft, the vast majority urging rejection, and more than 370 former National Park leaders wrote directly to Burgum urging him to maintain the order ([New Mexico Wild](https://nmwild.org/press-release-hundreds-of-thousands-of-americans-file-comments-on-trump-administration-plan-to-open-greater-chaco-landscape-to-drilling/)). If the revocation is finalised, new leasing, drilling and uranium mining could occur within the 10-mile zone, including on federal lands immediately adjacent to the park ([New Mexico Wild](https://nmwild.org/press-release-hundreds-of-thousands-of-americans-file-comments-on-trump-administration-plan-to-open-greater-chaco-landscape-to-drilling/)).

The Indigenous response to Burgum himself is split, and that split is the part standard biographies miss. Navajo Nation leaders met Burgum in Washington in April 2025 specifically about Chaco, and Council Delegate Brenda Jesus, who chairs the Navajo Nation's Resource and Development Committee, said Burgum "was very open and sat down to actually listen to Navajo Nation" — the Navajo Nation government, notably, wants no buffer at all ([KJZZ](https://www.ksut.org/news/2026-07-24/in-search-of-oil-and-gas-trump-could-axe-biden-era-buffer-around-chaco-canyon)). The Pueblo account is different: Acoma Pueblo's general legal counsel, Aaron Sims, said that under the current administration there had been no visits involving Pueblo leadership at [Chaco Canyon](/places/chaco-canyon/) and that he was not aware of any other visits generally, and the Interior Department would neither deny that claim nor comment on it ([KJZZ](https://www.ksut.org/news/2026-07-24/in-search-of-oil-and-gas-trump-could-axe-biden-era-buffer-around-chaco-canyon)). The [All Pueblo Council of Governors](/organizations/all-pueblo-council-of-governors/) has campaigned against the rollback: its leaders travelled to Washington to press federal leaders to uphold the order, and Joey Sanchez of Santa Ana Pueblo called Chaco "a living homeland that remains central to the identity and traditions of Pueblo people today" ([New Mexico Wild](https://nmwild.org/press-release-hundreds-of-thousands-of-americans-file-comments-on-trump-administration-plan-to-open-greater-chaco-landscape-to-drilling/)). New Mexico's full congressional delegation, all Democrats, has pressed Burgum in writing since his confirmation, invoking the government's trust obligation to Tribal leaders and the consultation duty under the National Historic Preservation Act, and has introduced legislation to make the buffer permanent ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/in-the-news/source-new-mexico-nm-delegation-presses-feds-on-chaco-protections)). The outcome, as of the sources read, remains undecided: a draft assessment under comment, not a final revocation — which makes Chaco the clearest live test of whether Burgum's energy-dominance brief can coexist with Interior's trust responsibility to Tribal nations ([KJZZ](https://www.ksut.org/news/2026-07-24/in-search-of-oil-and-gas-trump-could-axe-biden-era-buffer-around-chaco-canyon)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim a net worth for Burgum, a final outcome for the Chaco revocation, or that Burgum personally met or failed to meet Pueblo leadership: the sources read establish a Pueblo claim of no visits and Interior's refusal to confirm or deny it, and this profile reports both without resolving them. It does not settle the Kilbourne Group founding year (2006 or 2007), the year Burgum became CEO of Great Plains Software, the precise Chaco acreage (336,400 or approximately 338,690), or the later styling of the National Energy Council — each is flagged in the open items rather than smoothed over. It makes no claim about Burgum's private life, and it does not predict how the Chaco decision, or the wider energy-dominance programme, will ultimately be resolved.
