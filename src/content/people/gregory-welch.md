Gregory Welch is one of those people whose name is famous in law school classrooms while the person himself remains almost entirely out of view — and the path his case took to the [U.S. Supreme Court](/organizations/us-supreme-court/) is the part of the story the standard case summaries mostly skip. In 2010, Welch pleaded guilty to one count of being a felon in possession of a firearm; he had three prior violent felony convictions, including a Florida conviction for a February 1996 "strong-arm robbery," and he was sentenced under the Armed Career Criminal Act ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)). Welch argued that the Florida conviction should not count as a violent felony, but the federal district court disagreed and the Eleventh Circuit affirmed ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)).

What happened next is the under-told part. Welch filed a habeas petition under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 on his own, without a lawyer, alleging that the Florida strong-arm robbery statute was vague and that his attorney had rendered ineffective assistance by failing to object to his ACCA sentence ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)). The district court denied the petition and denied a certificate of appealability; his motion to the Eleventh Circuit for a certificate of appealability was also rejected ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)). Then, less than three weeks later, the Supreme Court decided *Johnson v. United States* — the very vagueness argument Welch had been making ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)). The Eleventh Circuit returned his motion for reconsideration as late, so Welch filed a pro se petition for certiorari — and the Supreme Court granted it ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)).

The case presented an unusual posture. The federal government agreed with Welch that *Johnson* should have retroactive effect, so the Court appointed an attorney "in support of the judgment of the Court of Appeals" — essentially, someone to argue the side no party was arguing ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)). For Welch himself, [Amir Ali](/people/amir-ali/) argued the case, and Ali later stated under oath in a federal court filing that in *Welch v. United States* he "obtained a 7-1 decision in my client's favor" ([U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, via CourtListener](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.464027/gov.uscourts.nysd.464027.145.0.pdf)).

The legal question the case existed to answer was whether *Johnson* applied retroactively — and that required understanding what *Johnson* had done. Federal law makes firearm possession by a felon punishable by up to 10 years, but the ACCA of 1984 raises that to a mandatory 15 years to life when the offender has three or more prior convictions for a "serious drug offense" or a "violent felony" ([Constitutional Law Reporter](https://constitutionallawreporter.com/2016/05/19/welch-v-united-states/)). The definition of "violent felony" included a residual clause covering any felony that "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another" ([Constitutional Law Reporter](https://constitutionallawreporter.com/2016/05/19/welch-v-united-states/)). In *Johnson*, the Court held that clause unconstitutional under the void-for-vagueness doctrine, which bars sanctions under a criminal law "so vague that it fails to give ordinary people fair notice of the conduct it punishes, or so standardless that it invites arbitrary enforcement" ([Constitutional Law Reporter](https://constitutionallawreporter.com/2016/05/19/welch-v-united-states/)).

On April 18, 2016, the justices decided *Welch v. United States* by a vote of 7-1 ([Constitutional Law Reporter](https://constitutionallawreporter.com/2016/05/19/welch-v-united-states/)). Writing for the majority, Justice Anthony Kennedy held that "*Johnson* announced a new substantive rule that has retroactive effect in cases on collateral review" ([Constitutional Law Reporter](https://constitutionallawreporter.com/2016/05/19/welch-v-united-states/)). The reasoning ran through the Teague framework for retroactivity: new constitutional rules of criminal procedure generally do not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review, but new substantive rules do ([Constitutional Law Reporter](https://constitutionallawreporter.com/2016/05/19/welch-v-united-states/)). Substantive rules alter "the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes," and *Johnson* qualified — before *Johnson*, the residual clause could push an offender's sentence from at most 10 years to at least 15 ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)). "By striking down the residual clause as void for vagueness, *Johnson* changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act, altering 'the range of conduct or the class of persons that the [Act] punishes'" ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)). The majority also rejected the idea that *Johnson* was merely procedural because the vagueness doctrine rests on procedural due process: the Teague framework turns on whether the rule's function is substantive or procedural, not on the constitutional source it came from ([Constitutional Law Reporter](https://constitutionallawreporter.com/2016/05/19/welch-v-united-states/)). Justice Thomas dissented, taking the position that the Court should not consider the claim and that it applied Teague incorrectly ([Maryland Criminal Law and Policy](https://www.mdcrime.org/2016/04/20/welch-v-united-states/)).

The Court vacated the Eleventh Circuit's ruling on Welch's appeal and remanded the case for further proceedings ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)). Beyond Welch himself, the decision reached thousands of federal prisoners serving lengthy sentences imposed under the ACCA's residual clause, giving them a route to resentencing ([Prison Legal News](https://www.prisonlegalnews.org/news/2016/jun/3/us-supreme-court-holds-prior-acca-ruling-retroactive/)).
