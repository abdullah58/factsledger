## Overview

Vladimir Nabokov (1899–1977) was a Russian-born American novelist, poet and lepidopterist, the foremost of the post-1917 Russian émigré authors, best known for *Lolita* (1955) and *Pale Fire* (1962) ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov)). Born in St Petersburg, exiled after the 1917 revolution, he taught at Cornell University from 1948 to 1959, became a U.S. citizen in 1945, and spent his final years in Montreux, Switzerland ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov)).

He was born on 22 April 1899 in St Petersburg into an old aristocratic family, the eldest of five children ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)). His father, V.D. Nabokov, was a leader of the pre-Revolutionary liberal Constitutional Democratic Party (Kadets) ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov)). Nabokov published two collections of verse, *Poems* (1916) and *Two Paths* (1918), before leaving Russia in 1919 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov)). Unusually for a major novelist, he had two careers at once: fiction writer, and butterfly scientist whose taxonomic work DNA research vindicated decades after he did it ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/nabokovs-net/feature/nabokov-s-blues)).

## From St Petersburg to Ithaca: exile, Cambridge and Cornell

Nabokov's family fled Russia in the aftermath of the Russian Revolution, first living in Germany and then in England, where he entered Cambridge University on a scholarship set up for the sons of prominent Russian exiles ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states)). At Trinity College, Cambridge, he first studied zoology but soon switched to French and Russian literature, graduating with first-class honours in 1922 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov)). That same year his father was assassinated in Berlin by a reactionary rightist while shielding another man at a public meeting ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov)). Between 1922 and 1940 Nabokov lived in Germany and France, writing for the Russian émigré press in Berlin under the pseudonym Vladimir Sirin; his early novels earned him respect among Russian émigrés but were not financially successful ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states)).

The European years were poor ones. Nabokov lived in what Britannica calls "happy and continual semipenury", his Russian novels published in very small editions in Berlin and [Paris](/places/paris/), and he made his living giving lessons in tennis, Russian and English, with occasional walk-on parts in films ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov)). He married Véra Evseyevna Slonim in 1925, and their only child, Dmitri, was born on 10 May 1934 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)). In 1937 the family moved to Paris, and in 1940, when France was occupied by the Nazis during [World War II](/events/world-war-ii/), they fled to New York, carrying the manuscript of *The Real Life of Sebastian Knight* (1941), his first novel in English and his first to be published under his given name ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)).

America gave him, at last, a salary and a laboratory: Wellesley College from 1941 to 1948, then Cornell University from July 1948 to February 1959, where he completed both *Lolita* and *Pnin* ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states); [Cornell University](https://events.cornell.edu/event/vladimir-nabokov-letters-drawings-and-first-editions)). He became a naturalized U.S. citizen on 12 July 1945 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)). The income from *Lolita* eventually let him resign his Cornell post and become a full-time man of letters ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)). From 1959 he lived in genteelly shabby quarters in a Swiss hotel, the Palace Hotel in Montreux, though he retained his American citizenship ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)). He died there on 2 July 1977, aged 78, and is buried in Montreux ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)). His papers are divided between the Vladimir Nabokov archive in Montreux and the [Library of Congress](/organizations/library-of-congress/) ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)).

## Lolita: rejection, bans and a bestseller

*Lolita* tells the story of Humbert Humbert, a middle-aged European man with an obsessive interest in young girls, particularly an American girl Nabokov terms a "nymphet" ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states)). Four prominent American publishers — Viking, Simon and Schuster, New Directions, and Farrar, Strauss — rejected the manuscript before the French firm Olympia Press issued it with a first printing of 5,000 copies in 1955 ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states)). It was his wife Véra who convinced him to proceed with publication ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/lolita/feature/publishing-lolita)).

The book appeared in Paris in September 1955, published by a press better known for its pornographic stock than for making the works of Jean Genet, Samuel Beckett and Henry Miller available ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/lolita/feature/publishing-lolita)). At first it drew little attention, until the English novelist Graham Greene reviewed it for the London *Times* and proclaimed it one of the best books of the year ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states)). The praise also provoked condemnation: many European critics called the book filthy and accused it of bordering on pedophilia ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states)). France banned it as a "dangerous book" until 1958, and it was eventually banned in England, Australia, Burma, Belgium and Austria, and at the local level in some American communities ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/lolita/feature/publishing-lolita)).

No publisher in America was willing to risk fines or jail, so *Lolita* did not appear in the [United States](/places/united-states/) until 1958 — three years after its publication in Paris ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/lolita/feature/publishing-lolita)). Its first American printing, by G.P. Putnam's Sons, came on 18 August 1958, and the book sold 100,000 copies in the first three weeks — a feat previously accomplished only by Margaret Mitchell's *Gone With the Wind* in 1934 ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states)). The Cincinnati Public Library banned *Lolita* on 17 September 1958, and the following week it reached number one on the bestseller list ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/lolita/feature/publishing-lolita)). Morris Bishop, Nabokov's friend and chairman of Cornell's Department of Romance Languages, had feared angry letters from alumni, and later said the three-year delay saved Nabokov, Cornell and himself "from a noisy and perhaps disastrous confrontation" ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/lolita/feature/publishing-lolita)). Nabokov himself never wavered: in a letter to Bishop dated 6 March 1956 he wrote, "I know that *Lolita* is my best book so far... a serious work of art, and... no court could prove it to be 'lewd and libertine'." ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/lolita/feature/publishing-lolita))

The controversy outlived the bans. A British edition appeared in 1959, and by 1960 the words "Lolita" and "nymphet" had entered common usage ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)). Stanley Kubrick's film of *Lolita* appeared in 1962, and Nabokov was offered $75,000 to write the screenplay ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir)). His works would not be published in the [Soviet Union](/places/soviet-union/) until 1986 ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/vladimir-nabokovs-lolita-published-united-states)). The censorship story continues into the present: works by Nabokov are among the roughly 400 titles from more than 100 countries held by the [Banned Books Museum](/organizations/banned-books-museum/) in Tallinn, Estonia.

## The lepidopterist: butterflies, the Blues and a theory vindicated

Nabokov's second career began almost as soon as he reached America. He emigrated to the United States in 1940 and within a year moved to Cambridge, Massachusetts, where he volunteered to organize the disheveled butterfly collections of the Museum of Comparative Zoology's Entomology Department ([Harvard University, Museum of Comparative Zoology](https://library.mcz.harvard.edu/blog/lepidoptera-love-nabokovs-untold-story)). His lepidoptery — the study of butterflies and moths — won him a research fellowship at [Harvard University](/universities/harvard-university/)'s Museum of Comparative Zoology, and he was eventually appointed to the paid post of de-facto Curator of Lepidoptera, which he held from 1942 to 1948 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/nabokov-vladimir); [Harvard University, Museum of Comparative Zoology](https://library.mcz.harvard.edu/blog/lepidoptera-love-nabokovs-untold-story)). With little formal scientific training, he nonetheless became a serious lepidopterist, collector and taxonomist, specializing in the subfamily *Polyommatinae*, "the Blues" ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/nabokovs-net/feature/nabokov-s-blues)).

His most important scientific paper, *Notes on Neotropical Plebejinæ*, appeared in *Psyche* in 1945 ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/nabokovs-net/feature/nabokov-s-blues)). In it he classified an array of South American Blue butterflies, successfully recognizing ten of the eleven now-known genera of American Blues — and five still bear the names he gave them in 1945: *Paralycaeides, Cyclargus, Pseudolucia, Pseudochrysops* and *Echinargus* ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/nabokovs-net/feature/nabokov-s-blues)). He also ventured a theory few taxonomists would attempt: that the American Blues came from a common ancestor in Asia about 10 million years ago and migrated via the Bering Strait to Chile in five distinct sequenced waves ([Harvard University, Museum of Comparative Zoology](https://library.mcz.harvard.edu/blog/lepidoptera-love-nabokovs-untold-story)). Few contemporary scientists took the theory seriously, and most full-time lepidopterists dismissed his findings as those of a dilettante ([Harvard University, Museum of Comparative Zoology](https://library.mcz.harvard.edu/blog/lepidoptera-love-nabokovs-untold-story)).

The vindication came decades after his death. In the 1990s, *Nabokov's Blues* (1999) revisited his work, inspiring Harvard's Naomi E. Pierce, then Curator of Lepidoptera at the MCZ, to test the hypothesis with gene sequencing ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/nabokovs-net/feature/nabokov-s-blues)). The resulting 2011 paper proved that the American *Polyommatus* Blues are indeed descended from different ancestors and were introduced in the Americas through a series of migrations — in exactly the order Nabokov had hypothesized ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/nabokovs-net/feature/nabokov-s-blues)). Pierce said she was "blown away... because he made these five predictions about what our time traveler would see, and he was spot-on correct about all of them" ([Harvard University, Museum of Comparative Zoology](https://library.mcz.harvard.edu/blog/lepidoptera-love-nabokovs-untold-story)).

The scale of the work is easy to understate: he published 18 scientific papers on entomology, and after dissecting 350 specimens he produced a complete revision of the North American members of the genus *Lycaeides* ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Nabokov); [Harvard University, Museum of Comparative Zoology](https://library.mcz.harvard.edu/blog/lepidoptera-love-nabokovs-untold-story)). His fiction and his science fed each other: 570 mentions of butterflies have been counted in his fictional work, and entomologists have named over 20 species of butterfly after characters from his novels ([Harvard University, Museum of Comparative Zoology](https://library.mcz.harvard.edu/blog/lepidoptera-love-nabokovs-untold-story)). One butterfly he first recognized as distinct, the Karner Blue, was federally listed as endangered in 1992 as its habitat disappeared ([Harvard University, Museum of Comparative Zoology](https://library.mcz.harvard.edu/blog/lepidoptera-love-nabokovs-untold-story)). Another, *Plebejus idas nabokovi* — Nabokov's Blue, named to honor him in 1972 — survives in the Great Lakes region on a single host plant, the Dwarf Bilberry ([Cornell University Library](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/nabokovs-net/feature/nabokov-s-blues)).
