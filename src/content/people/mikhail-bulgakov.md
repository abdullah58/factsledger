## Overview

Mikhail Bulgakov (1891–1940) was a Soviet novelist, playwright and former doctor whose satires of Soviet life were banned in his lifetime, and whose masterpiece, *The Master and Margarita*, was written between 1928 and 1940 but published only in 1966–67, in heavily censored form ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Master-and-Margarita)). Born in Kyiv, then part of the Russian Empire and now in [Ukraine](/places/ukraine/), he became one of the most widely read Russian writers of the twentieth century only after his death ([Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism](https://www.rem.routledge.com/articles/bulgakov-mikhail-afanasevich-1891-1940)).

Bulgakov was born on May 15 [May 3, Old Style], 1891, in Kiev, and died on March 10, 1940, in Moscow, in what was then the [Soviet Union](/places/soviet-union/) ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062)). Britannica describes him as a Soviet playwright, novelist and short-story writer "best known for his humour and penetrating satire", and his career traces the full arc of the artist under Stalin: early success on the Moscow stage, a decade of bans and silence, a famous telephone call from Stalin himself, and a masterpiece that survived only because his widow hid the manuscript ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062); [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/the-symbolic-survival-of-the-master-and-margarita)).

## From doctor to writer

Bulgakov began his adult life as a doctor, not a writer ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062)). He studied medicine at the University of Kiev and graduated with distinction in 1916, the son of a professor at the Kiev Theological Academy ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). Assigned to noncombat duty in the Russian army during World War I, he worked for several months in frontline military hospitals and then transferred to a remote village, where he served as the only doctor for an entire district — experiences he later recorded in the autobiographical stories of *A Country Doctor's Notebooks* ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). The New World adds that his civil-war field-hospital work took him from the Caucasus, where he nearly died of typhoid, to western Ukraine and rural Smolensk ([The New World](https://www.thenewworld.co.uk/mikhail-bulgakov-great-european-lives/)).

Discharged in 1918, he returned to a Kyiv fought over by the Red Army, the White Army, German occupation forces and Ukrainian nationalists, a city that experienced fourteen violent changes of government in two years ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). Bulgakov himself counted fourteen changes and said he personally witnessed ten of them ([The New World](https://www.thenewworld.co.uk/mikhail-bulgakov-great-european-lives/)). He published his first story in 1919, abandoned medicine in 1920 to write for newspapers and local theatres in Vladikavkaz, and moved to Moscow in 1921, where he struggled to support himself by editing and writing for newspapers ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). His first major work, *The White Guard*, was serialized in 1925: a realistic and sympathetic portrayal of anti-Bolshevik White officers during the civil war, it was met by a storm of official criticism for its lack of a communist hero and was never published in book form ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062)).

## Theatre, censorship and Stalin's telephone call

Bulgakov reworked *The White Guard* into the play *The Days of the Turbins*, staged with great success in 1926 but subsequently banned ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062)). The play became a fixture of the Soviet stage, and Stalin — who viewed it as ultimately favourable to the Bolsheviks — attended the production fifteen times ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). From 1925 to 1928 Bulgakov worked in close association with the Moscow Art Theater as a writer, producer and occasionally an actor; his plays were well received by audiences but denounced by party critics ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). His satirical fantasies of the same years were officially denounced too: *Diaboliad*, published in 1925, was implicitly critical of Soviet communist society, and *Heart of a Dog*, written the same year, was a scathing comic satire on pseudoscience in which a scientist transplants human organs into a dog ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)).

The contradiction at the centre of his career was Stalin's personal favour. Routledge notes that *The Days of the Turbins* became Stalin's favourite play, and that Bulgakov survived Stalinist repression that took the lives and productivity of many of his peers, even as his works were repeatedly banned ([Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism](https://www.rem.routledge.com/articles/bulgakov-mikhail-afanasevich-1891-1940)). By 1930 he was, in effect, prohibited from publishing, and his plea for permission to emigrate was rejected by Stalin ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062)). In 1929 his works had been banned for ideological nonconformity, and in 1930, frustrated, depressed and penniless, he wrote to the Soviet government asking to be allowed either to work or to emigrate ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). Stalin personally telephoned him three weeks later and arranged his appointment to the Art Theater as a producer ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). The New World, recounting the same call, says Stalin advised him to reapply for a Moscow Arts Theatre post he had been rejected for; he got the job, and the personal meeting Stalin suggested never happened ([The New World](https://www.thenewworld.co.uk/mikhail-bulgakov-great-european-lives/)).

The reprieve was partial. In 1932, reportedly at Stalin's request, *The Days of the Turbins* returned to the stage ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). But his tragedy on the death of Molière, staged in a revised version in 1936, ran only seven nights before it was banned for its thinly disguised attack on Stalin and the Communist Party ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062)). He remained with the Art Theater until 1936, when he resigned in protest over the mishandling of his drama *A Cabal of Hypocrites*, and became a librettist for the Bolshoi Theater ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). In 1939, hoping to win back favour, he agreed to write *Batum*, a play praising Stalin for his 60th birthday — and Stalin himself banned it ([The New World](https://www.thenewworld.co.uk/mikhail-bulgakov-great-european-lives/)).

## The Master and Margarita: written in secret, published after death

Bulgakov began *The Master and Margarita* in 1928 and worked on it until shortly before his death in 1940, through twelve years and eight separate versions ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Master-and-Margarita); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). He burned the first manuscript in March 1930, after learning that his play *Cabal of Hypocrites* had been banned — and the burning of a manuscript became a central image of the novel itself, in the line "Manuscripts don't burn", spoken by the Devil as he returns the Master's destroyed work ([New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Mikhail_Bulgakov); [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/the-symbolic-survival-of-the-master-and-margarita)). The novel juxtaposes two planes of action — 1930s Moscow and Jerusalem at the time of Christ — with the Devil, disguised as Professor Woland, descending on Moscow to expose the corruption and hypocrisy of the Soviet cultural elite, while the repressed novelist known as the Master and his lover Margarita fight for his destroyed book ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Master-and-Margarita)).

Bulgakov died on March 10, 1940, aged 48, of nephrosclerosis, a kidney disease, having become blind the year before ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/bulgakov-mikhail)). His third wife, Elena — the inspiration for Margarita — had sworn at his bedside to make the novel her life's mission, and she preserved the manuscript, editing and retyping a new version of it in 1963 ([JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/the-symbolic-survival-of-the-master-and-margarita)). The journal *Moskva* finally published the novel in 1966, but censored "to the point of butchery", cutting Margarita's sexual liberty and the mockery of Soviet corruption ([JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/the-symbolic-survival-of-the-master-and-margarita)). The first unedited edition appeared in 1967 — as an Estonian translation from the publisher Eesti Raamat, then in Russian from the Italian publisher Einaudi later the same year — and the novel was not published properly in the Soviet Union until 1973, three years after Elena's death ([JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/the-symbolic-survival-of-the-master-and-margarita)).

The posthumous reversal was complete. Britannica calls the novel a twentieth-century masterpiece and a penetrating philosophical work on good and evil, and Routledge records that in the last 25 years of the Soviet Union's existence Bulgakov was one of its most widely read and performed writers — a reception that rescued him from near obscurity ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Master-and-Margarita); [Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism](https://www.rem.routledge.com/articles/bulgakov-mikhail-afanasevich-1891-1940)). His rehabilitation had begun slowly in the late 1950s, and from 1962 several volumes of his plays, novels, short stories and his biography of Molière were published — but none of his three culminating masterpieces appeared in the Soviet Union during his lifetime ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/84062)). When communism fell, people took to the streets carrying copies of *The Master and Margarita* as a symbol of their new-found freedom ([The New World](https://www.thenewworld.co.uk/mikhail-bulgakov-great-european-lives/)).
