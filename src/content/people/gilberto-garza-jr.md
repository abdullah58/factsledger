## Overview

Gilberto Garza Jr. is an Idaho man who became a footnote in constitutional law by the strangest of routes: he negotiated a plea deal that traded away a potential life sentence for a guaranteed ten years, and then asked his own lawyer to challenge the very sentence he had bargained for. The lawyer said no — an appeal would have been "problematic" given the waiver, and it could have unraveled the deal and exposed Garza to that life sentence ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). Garza's appeal over the appeal that never happened reached the [U.S. Supreme Court](/organizations/us-supreme-court/), which ruled 6–3 in his favor on February 27, 2019 ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza%2C_Jr._v._Idaho)). The case, *Garza v. Idaho*, No. 17–1026, was argued on October 30, 2018, and decided February 27, 2019 ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza%2C_Jr._v._Idaho)).

## The 2015 plea agreements

In 2015, under two plea agreements, Garza entered an Alford plea to aggravated assault and pleaded guilty to possession with intent to deliver methamphetamine ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). In exchange, Idaho agreed not to file additional burglary and grand theft charges, not to refer him for federal prosecution on a charge of unlawful possession of ammunition by a felon, and not to seek a "Persistent violator" sentencing enhancement that would have exposed him to a potential life sentence ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). Both sides agreed to specific sentences totaling 10 years' imprisonment, binding on the district court if it accepted the agreements — the judge could impose no sentence other than the ten years Garza had bargained for ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). Garza also waived his right to appeal and his right to file a motion to correct or reduce his sentence ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). The trial court accepted the agreements and imposed the 10 years, while noting that the cases, considered individually, might have warranted a harsher sentence given Garza's "history of violent crime" and the "gratuitous aggression" displayed in the aggravated-assault case ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)).

## The appeal that never was

About four months after sentencing, Garza told his attorney he wanted to appeal ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). Counsel declined: Garza knew he had agreed not to appeal his sentence but wanted to appeal it anyway, and the lawyer told him an appeal was "problematic" because he had waived that right in his plea agreements ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). Garza filed petitions for postconviction relief, claiming among other things that his counsel had been constitutionally ineffective for failing to file an appeal despite repeated requests; for relief, he asked that his sentences "run concurrent" ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). Through his newly appointed counsel, Garza admitted the waiver "was by the book," that he had received exactly what he bargained for, that there was "no ambiguity" about the sentence — and that the only issue he could identify for appeal was sentencing review ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). The trial court granted summary judgment to Idaho, and both the Idaho Court of Appeals and the Idaho Supreme Court affirmed, the latter reasoning that once a defendant waives appellate rights, the *Roe v. Flores-Ortega* presumption of prejudice no longer applies ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)).

## The Supreme Court's ruling

The Supreme Court reversed. In a majority opinion by Justice Sotomayor, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, Kagan and Kavanaugh, the Court held that the *Flores-Ortega* presumption of prejudice — the rule that a defendant deprived of an appeal he would otherwise have taken need not show the appeal would have succeeded — applies regardless of whether the defendant signed an appeal waiver ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza%2C_Jr._v._Idaho)). "We hold that the presumption of prejudice recognized in *Flores-Ortega* applies regardless of whether the defendant has signed an appeal waiver," the majority wrote ([Constitutional Law Reporter](https://constitutionallawreporter.com/2019/03/28/garza-v-idaho-clarifies-ineffective-assistance-of-counsel/)). A waiver is not an absolute bar, the Court explained, because a defendant may still appeal claims outside the waiver's scope, claims that cannot be waived, or whether the waiver itself was knowing and voluntary — and "a defendant who has signed an appeal waiver does not, in directing counsel to file a notice of appeal, necessarily undertake a quixotic or frivolous quest" ([Constitutional Law Reporter](https://constitutionallawreporter.com/2019/03/28/garza-v-idaho-clarifies-ineffective-assistance-of-counsel/)). Since filing a notice of appeal is "a purely ministerial task," counsel's failure to perform it when the client asks is deficient ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza%2C_Jr._v._Idaho)). The case was reversed and remanded to Idaho's courts ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza%2C_Jr._v._Idaho)).

Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Justice Gorsuch and by Justice Alito in Parts I and II ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). Garza "avoided a potential life sentence by negotiating with the State of Idaho for reduced charges and a 10-year sentence," Thomas wrote, and his counsel "quite reasonably declined" to appeal the bargained-for sentence, recognizing that an appeal would potentially jeopardize the plea bargain ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). In the dissent's view, the majority had adopted a "defendant-always-wins" rule: "an attorney's performance is per se deficient and per se prejudicial any time the attorney declines a criminal defendant's request to appeal an issue that the defendant has waived" ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)). The dissent also warned that an appeal-triggered resentencing might have left Garza facing life in prison ([U.S. Supreme Court via Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Gilberto_Garza,_Jr._v._Idaho/Opinion_of_Justice_Thomas)).
