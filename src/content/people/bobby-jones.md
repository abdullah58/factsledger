## Overview

Robert Tyre "Bobby" Jones Jr. (March 17, 1902 – December 18, 1971) was an American amateur golfer from Atlanta, Georgia, and remains the only player to win golf's single-season Grand Slam — all four of the era's major championships in 1930 ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702), [Columbia Electronic Encyclopedia](https://www.factmonster.com/encyclopedia/people/sports/athletes/jones-robert-tyre-jr)). His fame usually stops there, but Jones was also a trained mechanical engineer who turned that training on the game itself: he redesigned the clubs everyone played with, pioneered golf instruction on film, and — after retiring from competition at age 28 — co-designed Augusta National Golf Club and founded the Masters Tournament ([SCOREGolf](https://dev.scoregolf.com/opinion/rick-young/a-master-of-more-than-the-game/), [LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)).

## The amateur who beat the professionals

Jones was born into a well-to-do Atlanta family — his father was a partner in the law firm Jones himself would eventually join — and the family bought a house on Atlanta's East Lake Country Club, where his health improved as he took up sports, including golf ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). He never had formal lessons; he built his swing by studying the East Lake professional, began winning tournaments at age six, and was playing in national championships by 14 ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)).

His career is often split into two parts: the "Seven Lean Years" (ages 14 to 21), when the prodigy's fame outran his results and his temper was notorious — at the 1921 British Open he picked up his ball and walked off the course — and the "Seven Fat Years" (ages 21 to 28), launched by his 1923 U.S. Open breakthrough ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). From 1923 to 1930 he played in 21 national championships and won 13 of them, a record built part-time: Jones typically played competitive golf only about three months a year, traveling to the biggest tournaments each summer, though he practiced hard year-round ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)).

His major record: seven open championships won while competing as an amateur — U.S. Opens in 1923, 1926, 1929 and 1930; British Opens in 1926, 1927 and 1930 — plus six amateur majors: the U.S. Amateur five times (1924, 1925, 1927, 1928, 1930) and the British Amateur once (1930) ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). Because he competed as an amateur, Jones was ineligible for the PGA Championship, and the Masters did not yet exist during his competitive career ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). He also played on five U.S. Walker Cup teams (1922, 1924, 1926, 1928, 1930), captaining the side in 1928 and 1930 ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)).

The 1930 Grand Slam remains unmatched ([Columbia Electronic Encyclopedia](https://www.factmonster.com/encyclopedia/people/sports/athletes/jones-robert-tyre-jr)). Jones won the four in order: the British Amateur at St. Andrews (May 26–31), the Open Championship at Royal Liverpool (June 18–20), the U.S. Open at Interlachen (July 10–12), and the U.S. Amateur at Merion (Sept. 22–27), where he beat Eugene Homans 8-and-7 in the final ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). One sportswriter called it "the impregnable quadrilateral"; history calls it the only single-season grand slam in golf ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)).

## An inventor's second act

Jones retired at 28, tired of the grind and the mental drain of championship golf ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). What followed was less a retirement than a second career built on his engineering background — a mechanical engineering degree from the Georgia School of Technology that his grandson Bob Jones IV says is "one thing often overlooked about Bobby Jones" ([SCOREGolf](https://dev.scoregolf.com/opinion/rick-young/a-master-of-more-than-the-game/)).

Working with Australian clubmaker J. Victor East at A.G. Spalding & Co., Jones set out to duplicate the feel of his own hickory-shafted irons in steel — then an absolute novelty — redesigning the longer heel-to-toe American iron into a more compact clubhead with a thicker top line and flange weighting on the bottoms of the irons to make them more playable ([SCOREGolf](https://dev.scoregolf.com/opinion/rick-young/a-master-of-more-than-the-game/)). The first set of Robert T. Jones Jr. irons reached the market in 1932 and, across 15 iterations, sold more than two million clubs before the brand was retired in 1973 ([SCOREGolf](https://dev.scoregolf.com/opinion/rick-young/a-master-of-more-than-the-game/)). One claim Jones explicitly rejected: that he produced the first matched set of clubs. "I did not participate in the production of the first matched set of golf clubs. These had been produced some years before by Spalding," he wrote to golf writer Charles Price. "I only claim to have aided in the production of the best clubs that had heretofore been wholly made in this country." Golf historian Peter Kessler suggests the confusion stems from a different first of Jones's: he built the first set of clubs with *numbers* instead of the standard names of the day — driver, brassie, midiron, mashie, niblick ([SCOREGolf](https://dev.scoregolf.com/opinion/rick-young/a-master-of-more-than-the-game/)).

Augusta National was the same engineer's mind turned to course design. Jones and Clifford Roberts co-founded the club, purchasing the Fruitland Nurseries site in Augusta, Georgia, in 1931 on a friend's tip, and hired the renowned architect Dr. Alister MacKenzie — who had the entire course designed from topographic maps before ever setting foot on the property ([GOLF.com](https://golf.com/travel/masters-who-designed-augusta-national-golf-club/)). Construction began in 1931 and the formal opening came at the beginning of 1933 ([GOLF.com](https://golf.com/travel/masters-who-designed-augusta-national-golf-club/)). In a 1967 letter, Jones explained the intent: "The Augusta National Golf Club was conceived by Clifford Roberts and me together and was not in the beginning to be the site of any sort of tournament. ... The main idea was to bring to inland Georgia the closest possible approximation to British seaside golf," and to get away from the small, well-watered greens that had "reduced American golf to target practice" ([SCOREGolf](https://dev.scoregolf.com/opinion/rick-young/a-master-of-more-than-the-game/)). The club began staging the Masters Tournament in 1934 ([Columbia Electronic Encyclopedia](https://www.factmonster.com/encyclopedia/people/sports/athletes/jones-robert-tyre-jr)). Jones himself played the early Masters as a ceremonial competitor; his best finish was 13th in the inaugural 1934 edition ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)).

Jones also pioneered golf instruction on film. After the Grand Slam made him a national celebrity, Warner Brothers signed him for the series *How I Play Golf* — 12 shorts released in 1931, directed by George Marshall and written with O.B. Keeler, Jones's personal beat writer and biographer — followed in 1932 by the six-part *How to Break 90* ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702), [HowStuffWorks](https://entertainment.howstuffworks.com/sports/golf/basics/bobby-jones.htm)). The 10-minute episodes paired skits starring figures like W.C. Fields, Edward G. Robinson, James Cagney and Douglas Fairbanks with Jones's lessons, and employed strikingly modern teaching devices for 1931: freeze-frame and manually shot slow motion to break down his swing, and black-studio setups using the color of his clothing — a white turtleneck for shoulder pivot, white trousers for hip turn — to isolate parts of the body ([HowStuffWorks](https://entertainment.howstuffworks.com/sports/golf/basics/bobby-jones.htm)). They are considered the first golf instructional videos ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)).

## Later life, Hale America, and honors

Jones returned to his Atlanta law practice after competition ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). During World War II he accepted one of the "legendary golfer" exemptions from qualifying for the 1942 wartime [Hale America National Open Golf Tournament](https://factsledger.com/events/hale-america-national-open-golf-tournament), played a Wednesday exhibition four-ball with Bob Hope, and went on record endorsing the USGA's decision not to count the event as a championship: "With fellows like Sam Snead and Bud Ward... unable to play, it wouldn't be fair to call it a championship tournament and put the results in the record book" ([Global Golf Post](https://www.globalgolfpost.com/featured/ben-hogan-hale-america-and-the-myth-of-the-fifth-open-title-2/)).

In 1948 Jones was diagnosed with syringomyelia, a rare condition in which a fluid-filled cyst (a syrinx) develops within the spinal cord ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). He played his last round of golf that year and spent most of his remaining years confined to a wheelchair, though he continued to act as host of the Masters ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). The disease reduced him from canes to wheelchair to bedridden within a few years, and he died in December 1971 ([SCOREGolf](https://dev.scoregolf.com/opinion/rick-young/a-master-of-more-than-the-game/)).

The honors that accumulated around him speak to both the player and the sportsman. The USGA named its annual sportsmanship honor the Bob Jones Award ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)); in 2021 the USGA's Golf Museum and Library opened a permanent Bob Jones Room at the Bobby Jones Golf Course in Atlanta, exhibiting the 1930 U.S. Amateur scorecard from Merion where he completed the Grand Slam alongside his Georgia Tech commencement program, Harvard class album page and Georgia State Bar certificate ([USGA](https://www.walkercup.org/content/usga/home-page/articles/2021/09/jones-room-opens-atlanta-bobby-jones-golf-course.html)). The USGA Museum also holds his famous putter Calamity Jane, his complete collection of national championship medals, and the world's largest collection of his correspondence ([USGA](https://www.walkercup.org/content/usga/home-page/articles/2021/09/jones-room-opens-atlanta-bobby-jones-golf-course.html)). In 1958 the city of St. Andrews named him a Freeman of the City — only the second American so honored after Benjamin Franklin in 1759 — he appeared on a U.S. postage stamp in 1981, and he was among the first class inducted into the World Golf Hall of Fame in 1974 ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). He died December 18, 1971, in Atlanta at 69, after years of declining health ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)). His wife Mary, whom he married in 1924, died in 1975; they had three children ([LiveAbout](https://www.liveabout.com/bobby-jones-profile-1563702)).
