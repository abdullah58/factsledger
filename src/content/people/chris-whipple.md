## Overview

When Tucker Carlson wanted to say, on the record, that Donald Trump personally ordered the [Jeffrey Epstein](/people/jeffrey-epstein/) files kept secret, he said it to Chris Whipple. And when Susie Wiles — Donald Trump's White House chief of staff and one of the most guarded figures in Washington — decided to speak plainly about her boss's "alcoholic's personality" and her colleagues' failings, she gave eleven on-the-record interviews to Whipple over the course of a year. [2][3]

That is the pattern of Whipple's career: he is the rare interviewer to whom powerful people tell the truth while the tape is running. He told PBS NewsHour that "senior White House officials hardly ever speak to you on the record" — yet his reporting keeps producing the exceptions. [3] Wiles later referred to the resulting Vanity Fair profile as a hit piece, but the White House failed to challenge a single assertion or quotation in it, which Whipple said is what tells you the story is rock solid. [3]

Whipple is a contributing writer for *Vanity Fair*, an author of multiple *New York Times* bestsellers about American power, and a documentary filmmaker whose career began at *60 Minutes* — a combination that has made him something close to the White House's confessional chamber. [1]

## Eleven interviews and a bombshell

The Wiles project began in January 2025, when Whipple started talking to the chief of staff on the record. From the start, he said, she was "remarkably unguarded and open," with only a handful of mutually agreed off-the-record exceptions over nearly a year of conversations. [3] The result was a 9,500-word, two-part *Vanity Fair* profile that Whipple described as both a portrait of Wiles and "a remarkable inside look at Trump 2.0." [3]

What Wiles said on the record was extraordinary by the standards of any administration. She described Trump as having "an alcoholic's personality," called Vice President J.D. Vance a "conspiracy theorist," referred to OMB director Russell Vogt as a "right-wing absolute zealot," and said Attorney General Pam Bondi had "completely whiffed" in her handling of the Epstein files. [3] She was also candid about her own doubts: she had been initially aghast at the shuttering of USAID, questioned the blanket pardons of January 6th defendants, and disagreed with the administration's deportation process. [3]

Whipple offered two explanations for why she talked. First, she believed Trump had been unfairly characterized during his first term and wanted a fair hearing, which she thought she would get from him. [3] Second, working inside what he called the insular world of the Trump White House, "you forget that some of the things you say might sound crazy outside that perimeter." [3] As he noted, Vance has since confirmed that he is a conspiracy theorist, and Trump has confirmed the "alcoholic's personality" description. [3]

After the piece ran, Wiles called it a hit piece and said she had been selectively quoted — but Whipple insisted she had understood from the start that they were on the record and had confirmed it multiple times. [3] He also said the episode did not damage her standing: her bond with Trump, which he traced back to their first meeting in 2015 — when Trump was so impressed that she was the daughter of sportscaster Pat Summerall — remained solid. [3]

## "Carlson's words, not mine"

Whipple's next bombshell arrived on September 16, 2026, with "God And Vance At Yale," his *Vanity Fair* profile of the vice president. [2] Reporting the piece, he spoke to Tucker Carlson — described as a onetime MAGA ally who appeared alongside Trump and Vance during the 2024 campaign before breaking with Trump over issues including the Iran war. [4]

Carlson told Whipple that he had pressed Trump repeatedly to release the Epstein files, beginning in the summer of 2025 as frustration built among Trump's base over an unkept campaign promise. "My position from the beginning with Trump, I said it to Trump, was, 'I don't understand why you would ever keep any of this stuff secret,'" Carlson said. "And I got like a bunch of different answers." [4] Then came the sentence that traveled around the world: "I don't feel any need to cover for Epstein. So I said, 'You shouldn't either.' In the end, it came from Trump directly." [4] When Whipple pressed him on what exactly had come directly from the president, Carlson answered: "The order to cover it up came from Trump." [4] Asked why, Carlson said Trump gave him "a long, very emotional explanation that this was all a plot against him, like Russiagate." [4]

Appearing on NPR after publication, Whipple confirmed the exchange was entirely on the record and distanced himself from the claim's substance: "Trump, on the other hand, according to Carlson, ordered the cover-up. Carlson's words, not mine. I asked him again, what are you saying? He said that Trump ordered the cover-up." [2] He added that, according to Carlson, Vance favored transparency and wanted the files released — though Vance himself declined to sit down for the piece. [2]

The White House pushed back through spokeswoman Lauren Bis, who said Trump had been "totally exonerated on anything relating to Epstein" and credited him with releasing thousands of pages of documents, cooperating with a [House Oversight Committee](/organizations/house-oversight-committee/) subpoena, and signing the transparency act. [4] The context is a real legislative arc: Trump had said in a June 2024 Fox News interview that he would likely declassify the files, then reversed course before signing the Epstein Files Transparency Act on November 19, 2025; a major Justice Department release followed on January 30, 2026, though millions of records remained unsealed or heavily redacted. [4] The trove names Trump thousands of times, but no criminal wrongdoing has been alleged against him in connection with Epstein. [4]

The Carlson–Trump rupture kept widening. On September 24, 2026, Carlson told NPR's [Steve Inskeep](/people/steve-inskeep/) that Trump should be removed from office under the 25th Amendment over his threats of nuclear war against Iran, calling him "flamboyantly corrupt." [4]

## From *60 Minutes* to the Gatekeepers

Whipple's access is not an accident; it is the product of a long career spent interviewing people who do not usually talk. After stints at *Foreign Policy* and *Newsweek*, he spent eight years as a reporter at *LIFE*, covering stories from famine in Somalia to apartheid in South Africa. [1] In 1986 he became a producer at CBS News *60 Minutes*, where he won a George Foster Peabody award. [1]

His own site fills in the texture the institutional bios skip. The Peabody was for "Mr. Snow Goes to Washington," a segment that resulted in a national ban on lawn darts. [5] He interviewed Winnie Mandela, Desmond Tutu, Ferdinand and Imelda Marcos, and Ted Kennedy; at *60 Minutes* he produced segments on Imelda Marcos, Corazon Aquino, John Connally, George H.W. Bush, and John McEnroe. [5] He later moved to ABC News *PrimeTime Live*, where he won multiple Emmys — including one for investigative reporting on "Morgan Medical," a medical clinic rigged with hidden cameras — plus a Peabody for the network's September 11 coverage, a Columbia DuPont Gold Baton, a Christopher Award, and six CINE Golden Eagles. [1][5]

In an odd detour that says something about his instincts as a producer, he created and was executive producer of the hit ABC News series *What Would You Do?* with John Quiñones, which captures ordinary people's reactions to staged ethical dilemmas. [5] As the producer of *Pastor to Power: Billy Graham and the Presidents* with Charles Gibson, he directed interviews about faith and the presidency with all the living ex-presidents. [5] In 2010 he founded his own documentary company, CCWHIP Productions, and he is the executive producer and writer of *The Spymasters: CIA in the Crosshairs* on Showtime. [1]

The books followed the same method as the interviews: deep, repeated access to people at the center of power. *The Gatekeepers: How the White House Chiefs of Staff Define Every Presidency* became a *New York Times* bestseller, as did *The Spymasters: How the CIA Directors Shape History and the Future*; *The Fight of His Life: Inside Joe Biden's White House* was published January 17, 2023. [1][5] His most recent book, *Uncharted: How Trump Beat Biden, Harris, and the Odds in the Wildest Campaign in History*, is another *New York Times* bestseller. [1] Peter Baker, the *Times*' chief White House correspondent, calls him "a premier journalist and historian of the White House as well as the intelligence community." [5]

He appears frequently on MS NOW, CNN, NPR, FOX News, and Sirius XM POTUS, ran an Institute of Politics study group at Harvard's Kennedy School on chiefs of staff, CIA directors, and campaign managers, and is an associate fellow of Timothy Dwight College at Yale University. [1]
