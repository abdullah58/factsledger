## Overview

George Orwell — born Eric Arthur Blair on 25 June 1903 in Motihari, Bengal, British [India](/places/india/), and dead in [London](/places/london/) on 21 January 1950 at 46 — was an English novelist, essayist and critic whose two late novels, *Animal Farm* (1945) and *Nineteen Eighty-Four* (1949), made him one of the most influential writers of the twentieth century ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). He took his pen name from the River Orwell in East Anglia, first using it for *Down and Out in Paris and London* in 1933 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

Orwell was born into what he called "impoverished snobbery": his father was a minor British official in the Indian Civil Service and his mother the daughter of an unsuccessful teak merchant in Burma ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). He won scholarships to Wellington and Eton, staying at Eton from 1917 to 1921, where Aldous Huxley was one of his teachers ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). Instead of going to university he followed family tradition into imperial service ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

## From Burma policeman to literary rebel

In 1922 Orwell went to Burma as assistant district superintendent in the Indian Imperial Police, serving in a number of country stations ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). At first he appeared a model imperial servant, but he came to feel ashamed of his role once he saw how much against their will the Burmese were ruled, and he later wrote that his police years had shown him the "dirty work of Empire at close quarters" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell); [The Orwell Foundation](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/about/about-george-orwell/)). The experience made him, in the Foundation's words, "a lifelong foe of imperialism," and supplied *Burmese Days* (1934) and the essays "A Hanging" (1931) and "Shooting an Elephant" (1936) ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

On leave in [England](/places/england/) in 1927 he decided not to return to Burma, and on 1 January 1928 he resigned from the imperial police ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). Feeling guilty that barriers of race and caste had kept him from mingling with the Burmese, he immersed himself in the life of the poor: cheap lodging houses in London's East End, dishwashing in [Paris](/places/paris/) hotels and restaurants, tramping the roads of England and working in the hopfields of Kent ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). Those years produced *Down and Out in Paris and London* (1933), then *A Clergyman's Daughter* (1935) and *Keep the Aspidistra Flying* (1936), the last drawn on his work in a Hampstead bookshop, where he met his first wife, Eileen O'Shaughnessy ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). They married in 1936; Eileen worked as his typist and copy editor and influenced his ideas, including his work on *Animal Farm* ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

His politics moved with his experience. After Burma he called himself an anarchist for several years, then in the 1930s began to consider himself a socialist — though, Britannica stresses, he was too libertarian ever to declare himself a communist ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). *The Road to Wigan Pier* (1937), written after living among the destitute miners of northern England, was his first socialist book ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

The Spanish Civil War completed his political education. Orwell went to [Spain](/places/spain/) to report on the war between the fascist-backed Nationalists and the leftist Republican government, and stayed to fight for the Republicans in the militia of the Party of Marxist Unification (POUM), serving on the Aragon and Teruel fronts and rising to second lieutenant ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). He was seriously wounded at Teruel, a sniper's shot to the throat permanently affecting his voice, and in May 1937, after fighting in Barcelona against pro-Stalinist communists suppressing their political opponents on the left, he and Eileen were forced to flee Spain in fear of their lives ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). The experience left him with a lifelong dread of communism, first expressed in *Homage to Catalonia* (1938) ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

## Animal Farm and Nineteen Eighty-Four

When the Second World War came, Orwell was rejected for military service and instead headed the Indian service of the BBC; he left in 1943 to become literary editor of the *Tribune*, a left-wing socialist paper associated with Labour leader Aneurin Bevan ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

*Animal Farm*, finished in 1944, is a political fable of the Russian Revolution and its betrayal by [Joseph Stalin](/people/joseph-stalin/): barnyard animals overthrow their exploitative human masters, until the pigs subvert the revolution into a dictatorship more oppressive than the old masters, amending "All animals are equal" to "All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). The book was hard to place with a publisher, because Russia was then allied with Britain and the United States against [Adolf Hitler](/people/adolf-hitler/) and many on the British left sympathised with the communist cause; when it appeared in August 1945 it made Orwell famous and, for the first time, prosperous ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). By then he was a widower: Eileen had died of heart failure in March 1945 during an operation ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

Between the two great novels Orwell wrote his most famous essays. "Politics and the English Language" (1946) ties political orthodoxy to the debasement of language — "In our time, political speech and writing are largely the defence of the indefensible," he wrote, noting that the bombing of defenceless villages "is called *pacification*" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). In "Why I Write" (1946) he stated his credo: "What I have most wanted to do throughout the past ten years is to make political writing into an art" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

*Nineteen Eighty-Four* (1949) was written as a warning after years of brooding on the twin menaces of Nazism and Stalinism: a future world dominated by three perpetually warring totalitarian police states, where the minor party functionary Winston Smith is arrested by the Thought Police and broken until he can love only Big Brother ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). Orwell wrote its last pages at his remote house on the Hebridean island of Jura, bought from the proceeds of *Animal Farm*, working between bouts of hospitalisation for tuberculosis ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). Shortly before he died he married Sonia Brownell, an editorial assistant at *Horizon*; transferred to a London hospital in September 1949, he died there in January 1950, aged 46, of tuberculosis ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)).

## Censorship, bans and legacy

Orwell's warning made a deep impression, and the book's coined words — "Big Brother is watching you," "newspeak," "doublethink" — became bywords for modern political abuses ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell)). "Orwellian" now describes the chilling dystopian society he depicted in *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, and the Foundation quotes his own standard for liberty: "If liberty means anything at all it means the right to tell people what they do not want to hear" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Orwell); [The Orwell Foundation](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/about/about-george-orwell/)).

The books themselves were censored as he had feared. *Animal Farm* was forbidden by the Soviet government, and *Animal Farm* along with Orwell's other writings were forbidden in Russia until the fall of the [Soviet Union](/places/soviet-union/) in 1991 ([The Culture Trip](https://theculturetrip.com/europe/russia/articles/10-books-that-were-banned-in-the-soviet-union)). He was not alone on the Soviet banned list: the same account records that [Mikhail Bulgakov](/people/mikhail-bulgakov/)'s *The Master and Margarita* circulated only in secret, self-published copies, and that [Vladimir Nabokov](/people/vladimir-nabokov/)'s *Lolita* was likewise banned ([The Culture Trip](https://theculturetrip.com/europe/russia/articles/10-books-that-were-banned-in-the-soviet-union)). The bans outlived the Cold War in Burma, too: in 2013, an Orwell Foundation delegation to the Irrawaddy Literary Festival distributed hundreds of copies of *Burmese Days*, *Animal Farm* and *Nineteen Eighty-Four* that had previously been banned in the country ([The Orwell Foundation](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/about/history/)). That history of banning is why Orwell's books belong in the story of the [Banned Books Museum](/organizations/banned-books-museum/) in Tallinn, a museum devoted to books banned, censored or restricted around the world ([The Culture Trip](https://theculturetrip.com/europe/russia/articles/10-books-that-were-banned-in-the-soviet-union)).

His name also lives on in the prize founded in his memory. The Orwell Prize was the brainchild of Sir Bernard Crick, Orwell's first biographer, who used the royalties of his *George Orwell: A Life* (1980) to fund it; announced in 1993 with the support of *Political Quarterly*, it gives annual awards for books and journalism ([The Orwell Foundation](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/about/history/)). Its first winners were Anatol Lieven's *The Baltic Revolution* for books and Neil Ascherson for journalism; an Orwell Youth Prize followed in 2014, and in 2017 the Prize became The Orwell Foundation ([The Orwell Foundation](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/about/history/)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim a single settled date for the end of the Soviet ban on Orwell's books: The Culture Trip says they were forbidden until the fall of the Soviet Union in 1991, while other accounts suggest open circulation from the late 1980s. It does not claim a precise day for the publication of *Nineteen Eighty-Four* in 1949, because the sources read in full give only the year. It does not claim the Orwell Foundation's charity registration number, because the Foundation's own history page and other records give different numbers. It does not describe Orwell's burial place or his final illness beyond Britannica's account of hospitalisation for tuberculosis and death in January 1950. It does not present the Foundation's praise of Orwell as an independent judgement, since the Foundation is the charity authorised by the Orwell Estate. And it does not present the statement that Orwell's works are in the Banned Books Museum's collection as freshly verified here: that claim rests on the sibling museum profile's earlier reading of a Prospect Magazine feature, which could not be re-fetched in this session, and the museum link above is made on the independently verified facts of the bans on Orwell's books.
