## Overview

Rory Eakin is the entrepreneur who co-founded [CircleUp](/organizations/circleup/) in 2011 with [Ryan Caldbeck](/people/ryan-caldbeck/) and ran it as chief operating officer through its marketplace years, its pivot into a data-driven fund, and its sale in 2023. Before CircleUp he had spent his career supporting entrepreneurs: a startup providing financial training to small businesses in Cape Town, South Africa; director of investments at Humanity United, part of the Omidyar family enterprises; and consultant with the Boston Consulting Group ([Crowdfund Insider](https://www.crowdfundinsider.com/2015/08/72530-qa-rory-eakin-coo-co-founder-of-circleup-talks-new-consumer-growth-fund-online-marketplaces-effect-on-access-to-capital/)). He and Caldbeck are both alumni of the Stanford Graduate School of Business ([Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/index%2Ephp/faculty-research/case-studies/circleup)).

Eakin's idea, shared with Caldbeck, was that consumer brands were stranded between two kinds of capital: too small for private equity, which generally wanted at least $10 million in annual revenue before considering a stake, and too regulated to raise money from their own customers. CircleUp's answer was an online platform where vetted consumer product and retail companies could raise growth equity from accredited investors — an AngelList for businesses that sold energy bars and pet food rather than software ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2012/04/18/circleup/)). Eakin stayed with the company for twelve years, through a near-death pivot in 2016, a $125 million fundraise, and a sale he later described as the price of being too early.

## Before CircleUp: Cape Town, philanthropy, and consulting

Eakin's career before CircleUp ran through three different ways of backing small businesses. He began at a startup providing financial training to small businesses in Cape Town, then worked as a consultant with the Boston Consulting Group, and most recently before founding CircleUp was director of investments at Humanity United, part of the Omidyar family enterprises, where he invested in small businesses and social enterprises to stimulate job creation and promote stability in fragile economies ([Crowdfund Insider](https://www.crowdfundinsider.com/2015/08/72530-qa-rory-eakin-coo-co-founder-of-circleup-talks-new-consumer-growth-fund-online-marketplaces-effect-on-access-to-capital/)).

That problem became the founding thesis. Caldbeck, who had spent years in private equity investing in consumer and retail, kept finding strong companies that were simply too small for a private equity firm to invest in; he and Eakin set out to build something to help those entrepreneurs thrive. The two started talking about CircleUp in 2011 and launched it in April 2012 ([Harvard Business Review](https://HBR.Org/2021/02/what-i-learned-when-i-was-burned-out?ab=seriesnav-bigidea)).

## Building the marketplace, 2012–2016

CircleUp launched in public beta in April 2012 from [San Francisco](/places/san-francisco/), backed with $1.5 million in seed funding from Starbucks CEO Howard Schultz's Maveron venture firm, Triple Point Investors, and JP Morgan veteran David Topper, among other angels. At launch the company had four full-time employees: co-founders Caldbeck as CEO and Eakin as chief operating officer, plus two engineers and designers ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2012/04/18/circleup/)). The model was deliberately narrow: companies had to be in consumer product or retail/restaurant space and have booked at least $1 million in annual revenue the previous year, and could raise $100,000 to $1 million in growth equity from investors contributing $1,000 to $25,000 each. CircleUp acted as a broker-dealer, through a partnership with WR Hambrecht while its own FINRA application was pending, taking a commission on funding raised from investors met on the site ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2012/04/18/circleup/)).

On September 13, 2012, five months after launch, he testified at a joint session of the House Committees on Oversight and Government Reform and Financial Services, urging the government to rapidly implement the JOBS Act — above all Title II, lifting the ban on general solicitation, and Title III, equity crowdfunding. He argued the Act would spread early-stage investment beyond technology startups and a few favoured geographies ([Engine](https://www.engine.is/news/issues/circleup-cofounder-rory-eakin-on-jobs-act/139)).

Growth in the marketplace years was fast and, by Eakin's own account, deliberately selective. By January 2015, 70 businesses with $1–10 million in yearly revenue had raised an average of $1 million each from more than 10,000 accredited backers, who wrote cheques averaging about $30,000; the company employed 40 people. More than 6,000 companies had applied, and Eakin said CircleUp passed on most — for valuation, for the management team's experience, or for the brand itself — scoring applicants on proprietary data plus third-party data. Companies typically sold 10 to 30 percent of their equity in a round, and CircleUp had announced partnerships with General Mills, Procter & Gamble, and Johnson & Johnson to mentor funded founders ([StrictlyVC](https://strictlyvc.com/2015/01/15/circleup-carves-niche-angellist-private-equity/)).

The numbers kept climbing through 2015: by November, CircleUp had helped 120 companies raise $140 million, accepting about 5 percent of applicants, with an average investment of $100,000 ([Food Dive](https://www.fooddive.com/news/circleup-makes-big-impact-on-startup-investments-140m-for-120-companies/409009/)). Eakin also put numbers to the access argument: citing CircleUp's data, he said women were nine times more successful raising capital online than with traditional banks and five times more successful than with venture capital funding ([Crowdfund Insider](https://www.crowdfundinsider.com/2015/08/72530-qa-rory-eakin-coo-co-founder-of-circleup-talks-new-consumer-growth-fund-online-marketplaces-effect-on-access-to-capital/)).

That year CircleUp also began turning itself from a marketplace into a fund manager. In July 2015 it closed a $22 million Consumer Growth Fund, managed by Jayson Yuan with about 50 limited partners, to match investments made on its platform by others — which Eakin said was a first, since no other crowdfunding marketplace had raised a fund to invest in deals on its own platform. The fund had been intended to be $20 million but was oversubscribed ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/07/13/circleup-raises-22m-to-invest-in-consumer-brands-on-its-crowdfunding-platform/?via=indexdotco)). Two months earlier it had launched the Circles B Corporation product, described as the first private-equity fund dedicated exclusively to B Corps, whose first investment was in Ruby Rockets, a healthy-snack producer based in New York ([Crowdfund Insider](https://www.crowdfundinsider.com/2015/08/72530-qa-rory-eakin-coo-co-founder-of-circleup-talks-new-consumer-growth-fund-online-marketplaces-effect-on-access-to-capital/)). The performance claims Eakin made for this period — an 81 percent unrealized internal rate of return for platform investors, and average funded-company growth of over 90 percent a year after raising — are company-reported figures, not independently audited results.

## The pivot, the $125 million fund, and the sale

By mid-2016 the marketplace model was no longer enough. Caldbeck later wrote that he and Eakin realized CircleUp would need to pivot to survive — from marketplace to direct investor — and that working it out was "agonizingly stressful," prompting the first layoffs he had ever navigated as CEO, followed by another financing round and the launch of the company's first investment fund ([Harvard Business Review](https://HBR.Org/2021/02/what-i-learned-when-i-was-burned-out?ab=seriesnav-bigidea)). Eakin was on the road raising that fund: on January 30, 2017 he flew overnight from San Francisco to London to pitch institutional investors on one of the first data-driven venture capital funds, seeking $100 million. The pitch rested on Helio, CircleUp's technology, which aggregated and scanned billions of hidden data points — customer reviews, sales data, ingredients, packaging, distribution points — to predict which small private companies would grow. Lesser Evil, a small popcorn company, was the centrepiece: Eakin's team showed investors three popcorn bags and asked which was the better investment, then revealed there were 900 popcorn companies in North America and that Helio had picked Lesser Evil as the one to watch. The fund eventually closed at $125 million ([Rory Eakin](https://www.roryeakin.com/being-early/)).

The pivot remade the company. The $125 million [CircleUp Growth Partners](/organizations/circleup-growth-partners/) fund's first two investments were supplement brand [HUM](/organizations/hum-nutrition/) and meat-snack producer [4505](/organizations/4505-meats/), and CircleUp also began a loan fund for emerging brands while sunsetting the original crowdfunding marketplace ([NOSH](https://www.nosh.com/news/2023/circleup-breakup-lending-fund-sold-vc-fund-in-transit-ai-play-fate-unknown/)). Caldbeck stayed on as CEO until October 2020, when a new CEO took over and he moved to executive chairman ([Harvard Business Review](https://HBR.Org/2021/02/what-i-learned-when-i-was-burned-out?ab=seriesnav-bigidea)). What Eakin's own title was after that handover is not stated in the sources read for this profile.

The ending came in 2023. After Covid hit in 2020, Eakin wrote, "the company was not able to execute the way we needed to, resulting in the sale a few years later, after twelve years of building." The company was broken up: its [Credit Advisors](/organizations/circleup-credit-advisors/) loan fund went to [Brightflow AI](/organizations/brightflow-ai/), which also absorbed several CircleUp team members, and the Growth Partners fund was put up for sale to an unidentified multi-strategy asset manager ([NOSH](https://www.nosh.com/news/2023/circleup-breakup-lending-fund-sold-vc-fund-in-transit-ai-play-fate-unknown/)). Eakin's own verdict, written in April 2025, was unsparing and affectionate at once: "We sold for less than we should have for an idea that was right, but too early." The $125 million fund, he added, is now managed by a large multi-asset manager, with the investment professionals moving to that firm ([Rory Eakin](https://www.roryeakin.com/being-early/)).

## After CircleUp

In November 2023, the same year CircleUp was sold, Eakin returned to teaching, with a course called "Decisions Making for Purpose Driven Leaders", exploring the challenge of leading organisations that seek to balance stakeholder interests rather than defaulting to shareholder primacy ([Rory Eakin](https://www.roryeakin.com/)). The institution where he teaches is not named in the material read for this profile. His writing since the sale has returned to the same theme as his investing career: being early is the same as being wrong in venture capital, he wrote, "but also incomplete" — the opportunity he and Caldbeck chased is still out there, and he hopes someone will build it ([Rory Eakin](https://www.roryeakin.com/being-early/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Who is Rory Eakin?**
Rory Eakin is an American entrepreneur and investor best known as the co-founder and chief operating officer of CircleUp, the San Francisco platform that let accredited investors back emerging consumer brands. Before CircleUp he worked in small-business finance in Cape Town, consulted for the Boston Consulting Group, and ran investments at Humanity United.

**What was Eakin's role at CircleUp?**
Eakin was co-founder and chief operating officer, working alongside co-founder Ryan Caldbeck, who was CEO. He was the company's public voice on its model and on policy, testifying to the US House of Representatives on the JOBS Act in September 2012, and he helped raise the $22 million Consumer Growth Fund in 2015 and the $125 million Growth Partners fund in 2017.

**What happened to CircleUp?**
CircleUp began in 2011 as an equity crowdfunding marketplace for consumer brands, pivoted in 2016–2017 into a direct investor built around its Helio data platform, and was broken up and sold in 2023: its loan fund went to Brightflow AI and its venture fund was sold to a multi-strategy asset manager. Eakin later said the company sold for less than it should have, for an idea that was right but too early.

**What does Rory Eakin do now?**
In November 2023 Eakin returned to teaching, with a course called "Decisions Making for Purpose Driven Leaders" about leading organisations that balance stakeholder interests. The institution is not named in the sources read for this profile, and no post-2023 business venture of his is documented in them.
