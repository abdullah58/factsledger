# Ron Ziegler

## The youngest at the podium — until 2025

Ronald Louis Ziegler was born May 12, 1939, in Covington, Kentucky. He grew
up in Cincinnati, moved to California, and enrolled at the University of
Southern California — where, between classes, he took a job at Disneyland as
a guide on the Jungle Tour, later joking that it was good experience for his
political career
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).

He became the youngest White House press secretary in history when he joined
Nixon's 1968 presidential campaign at 29, having first worked with Nixon as a
press aide on his unsuccessful 1962 California gubernatorial campaign. He
held the title until 1974, when he was named an assistant to the president
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).
For more than half a century that age record stood. In November 2024,
27-year-old [Karoline Leavitt](/people/karoline-leavitt) was named to take
the press secretary role under President-elect Trump — "Previously that
distinction went to Ronald Ziegler, who was 29 when he took the position in
1969 in Richard Nixon's administration"
([myRepublica](https://www.myrepublica.nagariknetwork.com/news/trump-names-karoline-leavitt-as-youngest-ever-white-house-press-secretary-67381367e33ab.html)).

## The voice of a scandal

Ziegler spoke for the White House on historic events including the opening of
relations with China and the Vietnam War, but his name is most commonly
associated with the Watergate scandal
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).
The Associated Press called him "the pugnacious press secretary who famously
called the Watergate break-in a 'third-rate burglary'" — a line he stood by
years later. Two days after the June 17, 1972, burglary of the Democratic
National Committee headquarters, he told reporters: "Certain elements may
try to stretch this beyond what it is." He was routinely combative with the
media and repeatedly dismissed the reporting of *Washington Post* reporters
Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein as they tied the scandal to top officials in
the Nixon administration
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).

He was a strident defender of Nixon until the public release of the White
House tapes, which made clear that the president and his top aides had engaged
in a vast cover-up. Ziegler would later say he had not been told about their
efforts to hide the truth. He publicly apologized to Woodward, Bernstein, and
their newspaper the day after the April 30, 1973, resignations of John Dean
and aides John Ehrlichman and H.R. Haldeman: "When we are wrong, we are wrong,
as we were in that case." He started to add "But... ," and was cut off by a
reporter who said, "Now, don't take it back, Ron"
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).

In a 1981 interview with *The Washington Post*, he defended both the phrase
and himself: "I was right. It was a third-rate burglary. Who knew it was
going to be anything more than that?" And he offered the line that, more than
any policy statement, captured his years at the podium: "It's necessary to
fudge sometimes. You have to give political answers. You have to give
non-answers. But I never walked out on that podium and lied"
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).

## Loyalty under pressure

His relationship with Nixon was close and at times physically charged. During
a visit to New Orleans, Nixon shoved Ziegler and snapped, "I don't want any
press with me, and you take care of it." On another occasion, Nixon told
Ziegler that anyone in the White House who talked to *Time* reporters "should
have their resignations requested within one minute"
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).
Yet Ziegler stayed with the president even after Nixon's fall from grace:
"I was the only one on that plane to San Clemente with Nixon when power
changed hands," he said. "I was there with Nixon in exile. ... I'm proud of
what I did as press secretary. I don't feel the need to apologize. There are
some things, however, I would have done differently." Asked for examples, he
said, "Well, I don't want to go into that."

His loyalty was remembered by those closest to Nixon. The president's
daughter, Tricia Nixon Cox, praised him as "a most capable and loyal public
servant who served the White House and my father with distinction," while
his deputy press secretary Gerald Warren said, "Deep down he was a wonderful
person. ... It wasn't easy for him, but he did his best and he was very
loyal"
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).

## A legacy beyond the caricature

At his death, historian David Greenberg — author of *Nixon's Shadow: The
History of an Image* — argued in the *Boston Globe* that Ziegler deserved a
more serious accounting than the fleeting obituary notices gave him. Thirty
years on, Greenberg wrote, Ziegler was considered "as innovative and
irritating in his deceptions" as the White House spokesman of that day, and
"Ziegler's passing deserves more reflection than the fleeting notices it has
received, for his tenure betokened the arrival of a new era in the
contentious relationship between presidents and the news media"
([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2003/ari-fleischer-has-nothing-on-ron-ziegler-says-greenberg/)).

The shadow he cast also took stranger shapes. Former White House counsel John
Dean, who helped expose the scandal, said in an e-book published on
Salon.com that Ziegler — despite his complaints about Woodward and
Bernstein's reporting — was one of the people who may have been Deep Throat,
the mysterious late-night source who gave Woodward crucial information.
Woodward said he would not reveal Deep Throat's identity until that person's
death; Ziegler said he believed Deep Throat was a composite of several
sources, which Woodward denied. In *All the President's Men*, Woodward and
Bernstein wrote that Deep Throat did a mean imitation of Ziegler
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).

After leaving government service, Ziegler held a number of private-sector
positions, most recently as chief executive of the National Association of
Chain Drug Stores from 1987. He retired in 1998 and divided his time between
Alexandria, Virginia, and Coronado, California, where he owned a condominium
overlooking the ocean. He died of a heart attack at his home in Coronado at
63, survived by his wife Nancy, his mother Ruby, and two daughters
([Editor and Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/nixon-press-secretary-ron-ziegler-dies,23120)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim Ziegler was the first press secretary to use the
White House Press Briefing Room (completed 1970), a detail found only on
encyclopedia mirrors that were not read in full this session. It does not
claim how many times he appeared before Congress, nor name film or TV
portrayals of him, for the same reason. It gives his death as February 2003
as the AP obituary framed it ("died Monday"), rather than asserting an exact
day from an un-read source. The Deep Throat speculation is presented as John
Dean's claim, not as fact. It does not cover Leavitt's actual tenure as press
secretary beyond the verified, sourced fact that she was named to the role,
ending Ziegler's age record.
