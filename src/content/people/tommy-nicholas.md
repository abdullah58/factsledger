## Overview

Tommy Nicholas is the co-founder and chief executive officer of [Alloy](/organizations/alloy/), the [New York City](/places/new-york-city/)-based financial technology company that sells an identity-decisioning platform to banks and fintechs — software that automates the identity checks, fraud screening, and credit decisions involved in opening and managing financial accounts ([Finovate](https://finovate.com/alloy-earns-1-35-billion-valuation-after-securing-series-c-investment/)). He founded Alloy in 2015 with [Laura Spiekerman](/people/laura-spiekerman/) and [Charles Hearn](/people/charles-hearn/), and has led it from a seed-stage startup that struggled for four years to a company valued at $1.55 billion after its September 2022 financing ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/regulation/2022/fraud-prevention-fintech-alloy-nets-52m-on-1-55b-valuation/)). In his own telling, his career is essentially a single project: "I've been working on fraud prevention, AML, decisioning, and credit decisioning for the last nine years," he said in 2024, "and then prior to that really was doing a few smaller things that inspired that journey" ([Fintech Nexus](https://europeantech.news/tommy-nicholas-ceo-co-founder-of-alloy-on-fighting-fraud/)).

## From payments plumbing to a founding gap

Nicholas and his co-founders met working in payments. They were colleagues at Knox Payments, an ACH payments company based in Richmond, Virginia, when they decided to leave and start their own financial technology company, moving to Manhattan in the summer of 2015 after Alloy was accepted into the Techstars fintech accelerator ([Technical.ly](https://technical.ly/uncategorized/alloy-techstars-venture-capital/)). The founding insight, as Nicholas later described it, came from that payments work: whenever a digital product onboarded customers and let them transact, the compliance, fraud, and credit decisions about those customers were something each company was expected to build from scratch, even though nobody would expect a payments company to build its own card-processing rails ([Fintech Nexus](https://europeantech.news/tommy-nicholas-ceo-co-founder-of-alloy-on-fighting-fraud/)). There were fraud vendors, AML vendors, and credit bureaus, he said, but no system a company could simply buy, install, and configure to make the underlying decisions — and that gap became Alloy.

The early years were slow. In the company's telling, the seed round took about twenty months and hundreds of rejections, and the team won its first customers through hands-on, labour-intensive sales implementations rather than any self-serve motion ([The Peel](https://www.thespl.it/p/alloys-unconventional-path-to-15b)). By September 2016 Alloy had raised only about $1.5 million and had five customers, ranging from payment companies to small lenders ([Technical.ly](https://technical.ly/uncategorized/alloy-techstars-venture-capital/)). Nicholas has described the first four years as a period of near-closures and heavy demoralization, and has said the moment of wanting to give up tends to arrive just as things start getting better ([Fintech Leaders](https://fintechleaders.substack.com/p/tommy-nicholas-ceoco-founder-of-alloy)). The early division of labour, in the founders' own account, had Hearn building the product with a couple of engineers, Spiekerman talking to prospects and customers, and Nicholas implementing the solution and gathering product feedback ([AWS Startups Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/startups/alloys-global-identity-decisioning-platform-built-on-aws/)).

## The unicorn years and the "decision system of record"

Alloy's breakthrough came with its Series C. In September 2021 the company raised $100 million led by Lightspeed Venture Partners at a $1.35 billion valuation, by which point its platform had evolved from automating onboarding identity decisions to adding transaction monitoring, processing more than 455,000 decisions a day across more than 120 data sources ([Finovate](https://finovate.com/alloy-earns-1-35-billion-valuation-after-securing-series-c-investment/)). Eleven months later it raised a further $52 million at a $1.55 billion valuation ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/regulation/2022/fraud-prevention-fintech-alloy-nets-52m-on-1-55b-valuation/)). Customers named in the 2021 coverage included [Novo](/organizations/novo/), [Brex](/organizations/brex/), and HMBradley ([Finovate](https://finovate.com/alloy-earns-1-35-billion-valuation-after-securing-series-c-investment/)).

Nicholas's account of what makes the product hard to build is more specific than the funding headlines. A deployment of Alloy, he argued in 2024, has to deliver two things: a single unified "decision system of record" that a company can show to its own analysts and to regulators — no separate system for the AML decision and another for the fraud decision — and a system that is "future-proofed," so that adding a data source, changing a rule, or removing a model is a configuration change rather than a new engineering project ([Fintech Nexus](https://europeantech.news/tommy-nicholas-ceo-co-founder-of-alloy-on-fighting-fraud/)). By 2023 the company had more than 400 customers ([Fintech Leaders](https://fintechleaders.substack.com/p/tommy-nicholas-ceoco-founder-of-alloy)); by 2025 Nicholas put the figure at more than 700 customers, over half of them regulated banks or credit unions, with 258 employees ([The Peel](https://www.thespl.it/p/alloys-unconventional-path-to-15b)). In 2024 he was also discussing Alloy for Embedded Finance, a newly announced platform for banks and fintechs offering embedded compliance risk tools ([Fintech Nexus](https://europeantech.news/tommy-nicholas-ceo-co-founder-of-alloy-on-fighting-fraud/)).
