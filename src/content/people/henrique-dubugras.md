## Overview

Henrique Dubugras is a Brazilian fintech entrepreneur who co-founded [Brex](/organizations/brex/), the corporate-card and spend-management company, with [Pedro Franceschi](/people/pedro-franceschi/) in 2017. He served as Brex's co-chief executive from its founding until June 2024, when Franceschi became sole chief executive and Dubugras became chairman of the board. Before Brex, the pair built and sold the Brazilian payments processor [Pagar.me](/organizations/pagar-me/) as teenagers ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/05/how-the-22-year-old-founders-of-brex-built-a-billion-dollar-business-in-less-than-2-years/)).

Dubugras was born in São Paulo, [Brazil](/places/brazil/), in 1995 and started coding at about 12, teaching himself so he could play a paid online game for free ([FinTech Magazine](https://fintechmagazine.com/articles/henrique-dubugras-the-27-year-old-billionaire-behind-brex)). He enrolled at [Stanford University](/universities/stanford-university/) in the fall of 2016 and left after about three months to join [Y Combinator](/organizations/y-combinator/)'s Winter 2017 batch, dropping out of college to build Brex full time ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/05/how-the-22-year-old-founders-of-brex-built-a-billion-dollar-business-in-less-than-2-years/)). Brex grew from a card for venture-backed startups into a spend-management platform valued at $12.3 billion in 2022, and in April 2026 [Capital One](https://www.capitalone.com/about/newsroom/capital-one-completes-acquisition-of-brex/) completed its $5.15 billion acquisition of the company ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/12/fintech-brex-abandons-co-ceo-model-talks-ipo-cash-burn-and-plans-for-a-secondary-sale/)).

## Teenage founder: a game, a classroom, and Pagar.me

Dubugras's first company was an online game he built as a young teenager — and was forced to shut down at 14 after receiving patent-infringement notices ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/05/how-the-22-year-old-founders-of-brex-built-a-billion-dollar-business-in-less-than-2-years/)). He used the money the game had earned to start an education company, EduqueMe, meant to help Brazilian students apply to American schools; it reached 800,000 users but never made any money, and Dubugras later said there were few Brazilian venture investors willing to fund a 15-year-old ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/05/how-the-22-year-old-founders-of-brex-built-a-billion-dollar-business-in-less-than-2-years/)). The pattern was set early: build something, learn the market the hard way, and move on to the next one.

Shortly after folding the education startup, he met Franceschi — a teenager from Rio de Janeiro who shared his appetite for coding — and, because of Franceschi's interest in payments, the two started Pagar.me, a Brazilian online payments company often described as the "Stripe of Brazil" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/05/how-the-22-year-old-founders-of-brex-built-a-billion-dollar-business-in-less-than-2-years/)). Pagar.me raised $30 million, grew to about 100 people, and was processing up to $1.5 billion in transactions when it was sold to [Stone Pagamentos](/organizations/stone-pagamentos/) in September 2016 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/05/how-the-22-year-old-founders-of-brex-built-a-billion-dollar-business-in-less-than-2-years/); [SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/how-brex-went-from-1b/)). The sale, for what TechCrunch reported as "tens of millions of dollars", came before either founder had finished college ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/12/fintech-brex-abandons-co-ceo-model-talks-ipo-cash-burn-and-plans-for-a-secondary-sale/)).

## Stanford, Y Combinator, and the birth of Brex

After the sale, the pair moved to the [United States](/places/united-states/) and enrolled at Stanford in the fall of 2016. Within about three months they decided to start another company, applied to Y Combinator with a virtual-reality startup called Beyond, and — about three weeks into the programme — abandoned it, concluding they were not the right founders for a hardware business ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/05/how-the-22-year-old-founders-of-brex-built-a-billion-dollar-business-in-less-than-2-years/); [SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/how-brex-went-from-1b/)). Y Combinator's partners pushed them toward the area where they had a genuine edge: payments. As founders themselves, they knew the problem first-hand — startups that had raised millions could not get a corporate card, because banks wanted personal guarantees and founder credit history the young companies did not have ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/how-brex-went-from-1b/)).

So they scrapped Beyond and, in April 2017, Brex was born. The card underwrote companies on their cash balance and funding rather than their founders' credit, required no personal guarantee or deposit, and could issue virtual cards moments after an online application, with limits as much as ten times higher than elsewhere ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/05/how-the-22-year-old-founders-of-brex-built-a-billion-dollar-business-in-less-than-2-years/)). The startup launched publicly in June 2018, by which time it had about 100 customers, and in October 2018 announced a $125 million Series C at a $1.1 billion valuation, led by Greenoaks Capital, DST Global and IVP, with backing from [Peter Thiel](/people/peter-thiel/), Max Levchin and former Visa chief executive Carl Pascarella ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/05/how-the-22-year-old-founders-of-brex-built-a-billion-dollar-business-in-less-than-2-years/); [SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/how-brex-went-from-1b/)). The pair featured on Forbes' 2019 "30 under 30: Finance" list, and were among the youngest self-made billionaires added to the 2022 Forbes Billionaires list ([FinTech Magazine](https://fintechmagazine.com/articles/henrique-dubugras-the-27-year-old-billionaire-behind-brex)).

## The own-the-stack doctrine

The idea most profiles of Dubugras flatten into "built from scratch" was, in his own telling, a specific lesson carried over from Pagar.me: "if you rely on a banking partner to do something, you will be slowed down more than you think you will. So, try own everything yourself, otherwise your product will be slowed down by the bank" ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/how-brex-went-from-1b/)). At Brex that meant doing the credit, the underwriting and the technology in-house, with a banking partner in the background but not in the critical path. It also shaped the earliest hiring: Brex's first employee was its chief financial officer, because a financial business needs to raise debt and underwrite, and its third hire was its general counsel, so that banks would trust two 22-year-olds to run their own regulated stack instead of relying on an outside lawyer ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/how-brex-went-from-1b/)).

The doctrine had visible product consequences. Brex's five-minute onboarding — the feature Dubugras called the toughest to build — took about six months of work on the know-your-customer, anti-money-laundering and regulatory infrastructure behind it ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/how-brex-went-from-1b/)). And the vertical integration became Brex's stated competitive edge: Franceschi later contrasted building "vertically integrated down to the Mastercard rails and the ACH rails and the money movement rails" with competitors such as [Ramp](/organizations/ramp/) that built on top of other platforms such as Stripe or Marqeta — fine for simple use cases, he argued, but limiting for global coverage and depth of integration ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/12/fintech-brex-abandons-co-ceo-model-talks-ipo-cash-burn-and-plans-for-a-secondary-sale/)). The same instinct showed up in hiring: having lost good people at cash-constrained Pagar.me by refusing to pay up, Dubugras said Brex would "do whatever it takes" to land someone it wanted ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/how-brex-went-from-1b/)).

## From co-CEO to chairman — and the Capital One exit

Dubugras and Franceschi ran Brex as co-chief executives from the start, but not in the usual split: rather than one running product and the other sales, Dubugras was the external co-CEO — fundraising (over $1.5 billion across primary and secondary transactions), banking partners, regulators, and personally selling to the company's largest customers — while Franceschi was the internal co-CEO, building the core financial infrastructure and leading the whole organisation for six years ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/12/fintech-brex-abandons-co-ceo-model-talks-ipo-cash-burn-and-plans-for-a-secondary-sale/); [Brex](https://www.brex.com/journal/evolving-brexs-co-ceo-model)). Dubugras put the upside plainly: "we had twice as much time as other CEOs" ([Brex](https://www.brex.com/journal/evolving-brexs-co-ceo-model)).

On June 12, 2024, the model ended. Franceschi became sole chief executive and Dubugras became chairman of the board, with both arguing that a single leader would make faster decisions at the company's scale and that investors would prefer a traditional structure ahead of a possible public offering, which they did not expect before 2025 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/12/fintech-brex-abandons-co-ceo-model-talks-ipo-cash-burn-and-plans-for-a-secondary-sale/)). Dubugras insisted he was not stepping away — "Brex remains my main and only thing" — and kept many of his external responsibilities from the chairman's seat ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/12/fintech-brex-abandons-co-ceo-model-talks-ipo-cash-burn-and-plans-for-a-secondary-sale/); [Brex](https://www.brex.com/journal/evolving-brexs-co-ceo-model)). The company he left the chief executive's chair of had more than 30,000 customers, including over 130 publicly traded companies, and a product suite spanning corporate cards, banking, expense management, travel and bill pay — but it had also cut 282 jobs in January 2024, halved its cash burn over the prior year, and was targeting cash-flow positivity in 2025 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/12/fintech-brex-abandons-co-ceo-model-talks-ipo-cash-burn-and-plans-for-a-secondary-sale/)).

The public offering never came. On January 22, 2026, Capital One announced it would acquire Brex in a stock-and-cash transaction valued at $5.15 billion — less than half the $12.3 billion valuation of the January 2022 raise — and completed the acquisition on April 7, 2026, with Franceschi continuing as chief executive ([Capital One](https://www.capitalone.com/about/newsroom/capital-one-to-acquire-brex/); [Capital One](https://www.capitalone.com/about/newsroom/capital-one-completes-acquisition-of-brex/)). Beyond Brex, Dubugras sits on the boards of Expedia Group and Mercado Libre and volunteers as an adviser at Instituto Alpha Lumen, helping with fundraising and education near his hometown ([FinTech Magazine](https://fintechmagazine.com/articles/henrique-dubugras-the-27-year-old-billionaire-behind-brex)).

**What is Henrique Dubugras doing now?**
