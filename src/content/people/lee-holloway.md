## Overview

Lee Holloway is the co-founder of [Cloudflare](/organizations/cloudflare/) most people have never heard of — the engineer who designed the platform the company still runs on. Prince and Zatlyn, his fellow founders, called him "the genius who architected our platform and recruited and led our early technical team" ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/founders-letter/)). He built Cloudflare's first working prototype, led its early engineers, and then stepped down in 2015 after developing frontotemporal dementia, a rare neurological disease ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/founders-letter/)). When Cloudflare went public in 2019, the founders named the IPO "Project Holloway" in his honour ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/founders-letter/)).

## The engineer behind Project Honey Pot

Holloway attended Monte Vista High School in Cupertino, [California](/places/california/), and worked as an engineer at HomeWarehouse.com, a bubble-era home-improvement site whose assets were sold to Walmart.com in 2000 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/08/15/cloudflare-has-a-third-cofounder-lee-holloway-whos-credited-with-making-the-company-what-is-today/)). He then studied computer science at the University of California, Santa Cruz, where a professor introduced him to [Matthew Prince](/people/matthew-prince/), a young entrepreneur working on anti-spam software ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/08/15/cloudflare-has-a-third-cofounder-lee-holloway-whos-credited-with-making-the-company-what-is-today/)).

The two began building an anti-spam startup, Unspam Technologies, with Holloway as its chief software architect ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/08/15/cloudflare-has-a-third-cofounder-lee-holloway-whos-credited-with-making-the-company-what-is-today/)). In 2004 they built [Project Honey Pot](/organizations/project-honey-pot/), an open-source system that let anyone with a website track how spammers harvested email addresses; thousands of websites in more than 185 countries signed up, and Holloway's flexible architecture kept adapting it to new threats ([Cloudflare](https://www.cloudflare.com/our-story/)). The project's users kept asking for the same thing — don't just track the bad actors, stop them — and that request became Cloudflare ([Cloudflare](https://www.cloudflare.com/our-story/)).

## Architecting Cloudflare

While Prince and [Michelle Zatlyn](/people/michelle-zatlyn/) worked on the Cloudflare business plan at [Harvard Business School](/universities/harvard-business-school/), Holloway built the first working prototype in his spare time ([Cloudflare](https://www.cloudflare.com/our-story/)). The three co-founders won the Harvard Business School Business Plan Competition in April 2009, closed Series A funding that November, and launched Cloudflare publicly on 27 September 2010 at TechCrunch Disrupt in San Francisco ([Cloudflare](https://www.cloudflare.com/our-story/)). Holloway recruited and led the early technical team that turned the prototype into a platform ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/founders-letter/)).

His contribution was recognised in the company's IPO filing in unusually personal terms. Prince and Zatlyn wrote that they chose "Project Holloway" as the code name for the offering because "the technical decisions Lee made, and the engineering team he built, are fundamental to the business we have become" ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/founders-letter/)). The filing also showed the material reward: trusts affiliated with Holloway owned 18% of the company's Series A shares and 3.2% of all shares outstanding — which TechCrunch estimated could be worth upwards of $100 million if Cloudflare went public at the $3.2 billion valuation private investors had last assigned the company ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/08/15/cloudflare-has-a-third-cofounder-lee-holloway-whos-credited-with-making-the-company-what-is-today/)).

## Illness, and the fund that followed

The founders' letter states plainly: "Tragically, Lee stepped down from Cloudflare in 2015, suffering the debilitating effects of Frontotemporal Dementia, a rare neurological disease" ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/founders-letter/)). He was diagnosed with behavioral-variant frontotemporal degeneration (bvFTD) in 2017, at the age of 36, after a brain scan showed atrophy inconsistent with his age and a three-day evaluation at the University of California, San Francisco's Memory and Aging Center ([BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/article/releases/holloway-family-fund-established-to-expand-aftd-s-research-initiatives/?s=97)). He now lives in a long-term care facility, visited regularly by his wife Kristin and their son ([Association for Frontotemporal Degeneration](https://www.theaftd.org/1ftd-in-the-news/b-k-holloway-ftd-story-self/)).

The dates do not line up cleanly between the company's account and the family's, and the difference matters enough to state rather than smooth over:

| | Company's IPO filing (2019) | Family's account (via AFTD, 2024) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| When he stepped down | 2015, "suffering the debilitating effects" of FTD | "That summer" — after behavioural changes first noticed in May 2016 |
| When diagnosed | Not stated | Early 2017, bvFTD, age 36 |

One possible reading is that symptoms began earlier than the family first recognised them; another is that the filing's year is wrong. Neither source resolves it, so this profile reports the filing's 2015 date in the timeline and records the conflict here and in the open items.

What followed is better documented. In 2019 Kristin Holloway set up the Holloway Family Fund at the Association for Frontotemporal Degeneration (AFTD), and in April 2021 it was announced with a $2 million initial donation and a plan to donate $1 million a year for 10 years to research into frontotemporal degeneration ([BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/article/releases/holloway-family-fund-established-to-expand-aftd-s-research-initiatives/?s=97)). FTD is the most common dementia in people under 60; there are no disease-modifying treatments and no way to prevent or cure it, and an accurate diagnosis takes 3.6 years on average ([BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/article/releases/holloway-family-fund-established-to-expand-aftd-s-research-initiatives/?s=97)). The fund supports AFTD postdoctoral fellowships, a Clinical Research Training Scholarship run with the American Brain Foundation and the American Academy of Neurology, and an annual Holloway Summit bringing researchers together ([Association for Frontotemporal Degeneration](https://www.theaftd.org/1ftd-in-the-news/b-k-holloway-ftd-story-self/)). Kristin joined AFTD's board in 2020 ([Association for Frontotemporal Degeneration](https://www.theaftd.org/1ftd-in-the-news/b-k-holloway-ftd-story-self/)).

## FAQ

### Who was Lee Holloway?

Lee Holloway was a co-founder of Cloudflare and the architect of its platform. He studied computer science at UC Santa Cruz, co-created the anti-spam system Project Honey Pot with Matthew Prince in 2004, built Cloudflare's first prototype in 2009, and recruited and led its early engineering team.

### Why did Lee Holloway leave Cloudflare?

He stepped down in 2015 because of frontotemporal dementia, a rare neurological disease, according to Cloudflare's IPO filing. He was diagnosed with behavioral-variant FTD in 2017, at age 36. Note that his family's later account places the first behavioural changes in May 2016 — a discrepancy this profile flags openly.

### What is the Holloway Family Fund?

It is a research fund set up by his wife Kristin Holloway at the Association for Frontotemporal Degeneration in 2019, announced in 2021 with a $2 million initial donation and a plan to give $1 million a year for 10 years. It funds early-career FTD researchers and an annual research summit.
