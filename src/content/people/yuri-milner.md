## Overview

Yuri Milner is a Russian-born Israeli investor who founded [DST Global](/organizations/dst-global/), the late-stage internet fund behind one of the most famous bets in venture capital: about 2% of Facebook for $200 million in 2009, taken without a board seat, more than two years before its IPO ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). A Moscow-trained theoretical physicist who left research for finance, he built Russia's Mail.ru first, then spent the returns on science, creating the $3 million Breakthrough Prizes. His career has also carried lasting scrutiny: the 2017 Paradise Papers showed Russian state-linked money behind his Facebook and Twitter deals, and in August 2022 he renounced his Russian citizenship ([International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/); [Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/silicon-valleys-richest-russian-renounces-citizenship-after-war/)).

## From physics to the Russian internet

Milner was born on 11 November 1961 in Moscow, then part of the U.S.S.R., into a Jewish family: his father was a university professor who wrote or edited some 50 books on American business management practices, and his mother was a physician ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). As a teenager he took night courses in computer programming, then earned a degree in theoretical physics from Moscow State University in 1985 and began a Ph.D. in particle physics ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). While a graduate student he found success selling personal computers on the gray market, and with his father's support he abandoned physics in 1990 to undertake MBA studies at the University of Pennsylvania's Wharton School ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). He did not complete that degree, but went on to work for three years in the financial-sector branch of the World Bank ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)).

On returning to Moscow in the mid-1990s, Milner sought a place in post-Soviet Russia's emerging capitalist economy, working first for Mikhail Khodorkovsky's Alliance-Menatep brokerage firm ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). In 1998 he and other investors bought a macaroni manufacturer and transformed it into a very successful concern, and the next year he co-founded the venture-capital firm NetBridge and began investing in Russian internet companies ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). When the internet bubble burst in 2000-01, NetBridge merged with Port.ru, which as Mail.ru became one of Russia's most successful internet companies, and Milner was its chief executive officer from 2001 to 2003 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). Under his leadership the company grew into Russia's primary email provider and its most prominent tech firm, according to the ICIJ ([International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)).

## The Facebook bet and the DST playbook

In 2005 Milner co-founded the holding company Digital Sky Technologies, which acquired control of Mail.ru and between 2005 and 2011 invested about $1 billion in more than two dozen businesses ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). During this time he founded DST Global and began broadening his investment horizon beyond Russia ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). In 2009 he shocked Silicon Valley by buying a 2% stake in Facebook for DST and Mail.ru for $200 million, more than two years before the social network's initial public offering ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). The buy-in was widely considered wildly overvalued, and Milner also gave up the perks other investors treated as prerequisites, most notably a seat on the company's board ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). The refusal of board seats and voting rights became the DST signature: Milner argued it let him invest in competing companies without creating conflicts of interest, telling Forbes the approach "sent a strong message that we are not seeking any influence over Facebook's operations" ([International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)).

The maths vindicated the model fast. By 2010 the initial investment had swelled to about $1 billion, and a total Facebook investment of $800 million was worth about $5 billion by 2011 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). Four days after Facebook's IPO on 18 May 2012, a DST Global subsidiary sold more than 27 million Facebook shares, yielding roughly $1 billion, according to SEC filings cited by the ICIJ ([International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). DST Global and Mail.ru's Facebook stake had reportedly grown to roughly $7 billion, making Milner's firms Facebook's second-largest group of outside shareholders ([International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). Similar well-timed early investments followed in Groupon, Zynga, Spotify and Twitter, and Milner's personal fortune grew to an estimated $1.1 billion by 2013 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). The playbook kept working into the fintech era: [Chime](/organizations/chime/) counts Yuri Milner's DST Global, [General Atlantic](/organizations/general-atlantic/) and [ICONIQ](/organizations/iconiq-capital/) among its backers, having raised $2.65 billion in total from private investors before its June 2025 [Nasdaq](/organizations/nasdaq/) debut, when shares opened at $43 against a $27 IPO price and valued the company at $18.4 billion ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/chime-set-long-awaited-market-debut-after-864-million-us-ipo-2025-06-12/)). [Morgan Stanley](/organizations/morgan-stanley/), [Goldman Sachs](/organizations/goldman-sachs/) and [J.P. Morgan](/organizations/jpmorgan-chase/) were the lead underwriters for that offering ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/chime-set-long-awaited-market-debut-after-864-million-us-ipo-2025-06-12/)).

## Science philanthropy: prizes bigger than the Nobel

Milner spent a share of the Facebook-era fortune on the field he had left. In 2012 he established the Fundamental Physics Prize, which annually awarded $3 million each to nine physicists for their achievements in fundamental physics ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). The Milner Foundation announced the prize on 31 July 2012, awarding nine prizes in its inaugural year to Nima Arkani-Hamed, Alan Guth, Alexei Kitaev, Maxim Kontsevich, Andrei Linde, Juan Maldacena, Nathan Seiberg, Ashoke Sen and Edward Witten, with the recipients agreeing to serve on the foundation's selection committee for future prizes and Nobel laureate Steven Weinberg joining its board ([Breakthrough Prize](https://breakthroughprize.org/News/15)). Milner said he hoped the prize would "bring long overdue recognition to the greatest minds working in the field of fundamental physics, and if this helps encourage young people to be inspired by science, I will be deeply gratified" ([Breakthrough Prize](https://breakthroughprize.org/News/15)). At $3 million, each prize was more than twice the cash value of a Nobel Prize at the time. In 2013 he joined Facebook co-founder Mark Zuckerberg, Google co-founder Sergey Brin and others in establishing the similarly generous Breakthrough Prize in Life Sciences ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). The prizes grew into the Breakthrough Prizes, covering fundamental physics, life sciences and mathematics, and they remain the most visible part of Milner's public identity after his investing record.

## Scrutiny, and the break with Russia

In early November 2017 the sheen came off Milner's prescient investments when the Paradise Papers investigation showed they had been directly and indirectly funded by a pair of Russian government-controlled financial institutions, Gazprom Investholding and VTB Bank ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). VTB Bank had quietly directed $191 million into a DST Global investment vehicle used to buy a large Twitter stake in 2011, and a Gazprom Investholding-funded offshore company, Kanton Services, had partnered with DST Global in the large Facebook investment ([International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). The revelation came as US congressional committees and special counsel Robert Mueller were investigating Russian interference in the 2016 US presidential election ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)).

Milner's response, carried by the ICIJ, was that the investments his firm makes have always been based on business merits and have nothing to do with politics, that VTB was a passive investor with no control over Twitter or DST Global's investment vehicles, that less than 5% of his firm's investment funding came from Russian government institutions, and that he had not known of the Gazprom link until investigators contacted him ([International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). The ICIJ itself stated there is no indication the Kremlin gained influence over Twitter or Facebook or received inside information from the investments, and noted DST had sold its Twitter stake before the United States sanctioned VTB in 2014, while Facebook emphasised DST had no voting rights or board seat and had sold all its Facebook holdings ([International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). Alisher Usmanov, the politically connected Uzbek-Russian billionaire who managed Gazprom Investholding for more than a decade, was a major DST shareholder and a key partner in the Facebook and Twitter deals; his spokesman said none of his Facebook and JD.com investments used money borrowed from state institutions ([International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). The Paradise Papers also pointed to Milner's financial involvement in Cadre, a real-estate technology company co-founded by Donald Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner, though there was no implication that link was related to the Russian government's investments; Milner said he invested $850,000 of his own money, none of it from DST Global, and made no additional investments in Cadre ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768); [International Consortium of Investigative Journalists](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)).

The break with Russia then became personal and public. Milner and his family left Russia for good in 2014, after the annexation of Crimea, and he has not been to Russia since ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/silicon-valleys-richest-russian-renounces-citizenship-after-war/)). When Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, DST Global released a statement condemning "Russia's war against Ukraine, its sovereign neighbour," and his foundation spoke out against "unprovoked and brutal assaults against the civilian population"; Milner said he stood behind those statements ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/silicon-valleys-richest-russian-renounces-citizenship-after-war/)). In August 2022 he completed the process of renouncing his Russian citizenship ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/silicon-valleys-richest-russian-renounces-citizenship-after-war/)). He has been an Israeli citizen since 1999, and he told Bloomberg that DST has not taken money from Russia since 2011 and has made no investments in the country since then ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/silicon-valleys-richest-russian-renounces-citizenship-after-war/)). The Bloomberg Billionaires Index estimated his wealth at about $3.5 billion in October 2022 ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/silicon-valleys-richest-russian-renounces-citizenship-after-war/)). He bought a house in Los Altos, California, in 2011 for $100 million and holds a US O-1 visa, a type reserved for people of "extraordinary ability" ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/silicon-valleys-richest-russian-renounces-citizenship-after-war/)).
