## Overview

Bruce R. Chizen was the American business executive who led [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/) as president and chief executive from 2000 to 2007, the first non-founder to run the company ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/188115/adobeceo.html)). He joined Adobe in 1994 and, as CEO, bought [Macromedia](/organizations/macromedia/) in 2005 for approximately $3.4 billion — a deal he summed up in a single word, "[Flash](/creative-works/flash/)" ([Joint Venture](https://jointventure.org/bruce-chizen-bio); [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). He stepped down effective December 1, 2007, and was succeeded by [Shantanu Narayen](/people/shantanu-narayen/), then Adobe's president and chief operating officer ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/188115/adobeceo.html)).

Chizen grew up in the Canarsie neighborhood of Brooklyn and earned a bachelor's degree from Brooklyn College, City University of New York ([Joint Venture](https://jointventure.org/bruce-chizen-bio)). His route to Adobe ran through the consumer-electronics and software sales world, not through engineering: he worked in Mattel Electronics' merchandising group from 1980 to 1983, helping grow it to a $500 million business, joined Microsoft in 1983 and became its eastern region sales director, and in 1987 joined Claris Corporation, an [Apple](/organizations/apple/) subsidiary, as a founding senior manager, later serving as vice president of sales and worldwide marketing ([Joint Venture](https://jointventure.org/bruce-chizen-bio)).

## The Aldus route into Adobe

Chizen did not join Adobe directly. He had been vice president and general manager at Aldus's Silicon Beach Software studio, and he arrived at Adobe in 1994 when Aldus merged into Adobe — the same merger that brought PageMaker into Adobe's lineup ([Joint Venture](https://jointventure.org/bruce-chizen-bio)). At Adobe he served as vice president and general manager of both the professional graphics division and the consumer division, then as executive vice president of worldwide products and marketing, before being appointed CEO in 2000 ([Joint Venture](https://jointventure.org/bruce-chizen-bio); [Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/188115/adobeceo.html)). The founders stayed on as board co-chairmen: [Charles Geschke](/people/charles-geschke/) and [John Warnock](/people/john-warnock/) publicly thanked Chizen on his departure for his "vision" in transforming Adobe "from one known mainly for its design tools to one of the largest and most diversified software companies in the world" ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1570046/update-chizen-out-at-month-s-end-as-adobe-ceo.html)).

That diversification claim has a number behind it, though it should be read as the biographer's claim rather than an audited figure: Joint Venture's bio says that as CEO from 2000 to 2007, Chizen tripled Adobe's revenue and turned a company known mainly for popular design products into one of the most significant forces in the software industry ([Joint Venture](https://jointventure.org/bruce-chizen-bio)). The expansion went beyond design and publishing into enterprise, knowledge-worker, and consumer markets under his leadership ([Joint Venture](https://jointventure.org/bruce-chizen-bio)).

## The Macromedia deal and a personal departure

The signature event of Chizen's tenure was the December 2005 acquisition of Macromedia, valued at approximately $3.4 billion ([Joint Venture](https://jointventure.org/bruce-chizen-bio)). When Knowledge at Wharton asked him for the primary motivation behind the deal, his one-word response was "Flash" — the interactive-web format Macromedia owned, which Adobe lacked ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Macworld's contemporaneous account frames the deal the same way: it made Adobe a leader in tools for building rich Internet applications, and raised Adobe's profile enough to make it a target of larger vendors such as Microsoft, which had just released its rival Silverlight web tool ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/188115/adobeceo.html)).

Chizen's exit, announced November 12, 2007, was presented by Adobe and by contemporaneous reporting as a personal decision, not a dismissal. He stayed on the board through the second quarter of 2008 and continued as a strategic adviser until the end of fiscal 2008 ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/188115/adobeceo.html)). On a conference call, executives stressed the point — Narayen said the positive fourth-quarter guidance was announced alongside the news "to make it completely clear" that Chizen had decided to leave on his own — and Chizen himself said passing the leadership on was "one of the hardest [decisions] I've ever made," adding that he was "at a point in my life that I'd like to take a step back and think about what I would like to do with the rest of my life" ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1570046/update-chizen-out-at-month-s-end-as-adobe-ceo.html)). Macworld noted the human hints behind that framing: at Adobe's MAX conference weeks earlier, Chizen had called dealing with the financial community "going to the dentist without Novocaine" and described the transparency of a public CEO's life as "weird" and "awkward" ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/188115/adobeceo.html)).
