## Overview

On 13 September 2026, Kosovo's parliament re-elected Albin Kurti as prime minister with the backing of 62 of its 120 lawmakers, breaking three months of government paralysis — while leaving the presidency still unfilled and the threat of another snap election on the table ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/13/kosovo-approves-new-government-after-months-of-political-deadlock)). It is the latest turn in a career that most English-language biographies describe as if it stopped in 2021. Kurti is a former political prisoner who went from leading student protests in 1990s Pristina to leading the Vetëvendosje (Self-Determination) movement, saw his first government toppled by a no-confidence vote in March 2020 after less than two months, and then governed Kosovo through the institutional deadlock of 2025-2026 ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/); [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-kosovo-government/kosovo-lawmakers-dismiss-government-in-no-confidence-vote-idUSKBN21C3OO/?il=0)).

## From student protests to a Serbian prison

Kurti was born in Pristina on 24 March 1975 and first became a public name in 1997 through the Independent Students Union at the University of Pristina, where he was studying electronics ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)). The union demanded the return of university buildings from which ethnic Albanian students and professors had been forced out in the early 1990s under Slobodan Milošević's regime; Kurti travelled to Washington, New York, Brussels and Copenhagen to press the students' case, meeting officials including Britain's Robin Cook and France's Hubert Védrine ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)). He was first arrested at a demonstration in Pristina in October 1997 ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)).

In August 1998 he began working in the office of Adem Demaçi, the chief political representative of the Kosovo Liberation Army (KLA) ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)). Serbian police arrested him again in April 1999 during the NATO bombing of Yugoslavia, and when Serbian forces withdrew from Kosovo on 10 June 1999 he was among the thousands of Kosovo Albanian prisoners transferred to Serbia proper ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)). In March 2000 a court in Niš sentenced him to 15 years' imprisonment for crimes against Yugoslavia's territorial integrity and terrorist activity; he refused to be defended, telling the court: "I can only be tried by a court of my own people. I do not recognise this court, just as I do not recognize Serbia or Yugoslavia." He was released from Požarevac prison in Serbia in 2001 ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)).

## Building Vetëvendosje and the first, short-lived premiership

On 12 June 2005, activists of the Action for Kosovo Network wrote "No negotiations, Self-Determination" on UNMIK buildings; the network then became the Self-Determination Movement — Vetëvendosje ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)). The movement demanded a referendum on Kosovo's final status and opposed any continued United Nations supervisory role in the country ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)). In 2010 it entered electoral politics for the first time, polling above 10 percent with a chance of becoming Kosovo's third-largest party ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)). Kurti's confrontational profile was already set: earlier that year he had been convicted of obstructing officials during 2007 Pristina protests in which two Vetëvendosje demonstrators were killed, and after he failed to appear before the court in February the EU's EULEX rule-of-law mission issued an arrest warrant; he was arrested in June, convicted, and immediately released for time already spent under house arrest, after almost 150,000 people signed a petition urging the charges be dropped ([Balkan Insight](https://balkaninsight.com/2010/03/09/albin-kurti-guardian-of-flame-of-kosovo-nationalism/bi/all-balkan-countries/)).

Kurti was elected prime minister on 3 February 2020 ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-kosovo-government/kosovo-lawmakers-dismiss-government-in-no-confidence-vote-idUSKBN21C3OO/?il=0)). During that first, short-lived government he appointed anti-corruption activist [Avni Zogiani](/people/avni-zogiani/) as Deputy Minister of Infrastructure and Environment, serving under Minister Lumir Abdixhiku ([Telegrafi](https://telegrafi.com/en/Kurti-appoints-Avni-Zogiani-as-Deputy-Minister-of-Infrastructure-and-Environment/)). Less than two months later, on 25 March 2020, parliament dismissed his cabinet in a no-confidence vote, with 82 votes against it in the 120-seat parliament ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-kosovo-government/kosovo-lawmakers-dismiss-government-in-no-confidence-vote-idUSKBN21C3OO/?il=0)). The motion was initiated by the Democratic League of Kosovo (LDK), Kurti's own coalition partner, after he sacked LDK interior minister Agim Veliu, who had backed President Hashim Thaçi's call for a coronavirus state of emergency that Kurti called unjustified ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-kosovo-government/kosovo-lawmakers-dismiss-government-in-no-confidence-vote-idUSKBN21C3OO/?il=0)). Underneath lay a second fight: whether to abolish the 100% tariff on Serbian goods imposed in November 2018; Kurti's partial removal of the tariffs prompted the United States to halt $50 million in aid ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-kosovo-government/kosovo-lawmakers-dismiss-government-in-no-confidence-vote-idUSKBN21C3OO/?il=0)). Kurti told MPs the real reason for his dismissal was to clear the way for a Thaçi-Vučić territory-swap deal ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-kosovo-government/kosovo-lawmakers-dismiss-government-in-no-confidence-vote-idUSKBN21C3OO/?il=0)).

## The 2021 landslide and a confrontational second term

In the February 2021 snap election the Vetëvendosje-led coalition won more than 50% of the vote and a seven-seat majority in the 120-seat parliament, returning Kurti to the premiership ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/kosovos-ruling-party-set-to-come-first-in-election)). Kosovo had declared independence from Serbia in 2008 with US backing after the 78-day NATO bombing campaign of 1999 ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/kosovos-ruling-party-set-to-come-first-in-election)). His second government pushed Pristina's authority into the ethnic Serb-majority north — a policy analysts say bolstered his popularity — while critics said he neglected education and health and alienated Kosovo's traditional allies, the EU and the United States ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/kosovos-ruling-party-set-to-come-first-in-election)). In 2023 the EU imposed economic curbs over Pristina's role in stoking tensions with ethnic Serbs, cutting at least €150 million in funding, according to Reuters' reporting ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/kosovos-ruling-party-set-to-come-first-in-election)). The government pointed to economic wins: unemployment down from 30% to around 10%, a higher minimum wage, and 2024 growth faster than the Western Balkans average ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/kosovos-ruling-party-set-to-come-first-in-election)).

The sharpest crisis came on 24 September 2023, when "masked professionals armed with heavy weapons" attacked a police patrol in the village of Banjska, 55 kilometres north of Pristina, killing one officer and wounding another; Kurti blamed "organized crime, which is politically, financially and logistically supported from Belgrade" ([Associated Press](https://www.wcbe.org/npr-news/2023-09-24/one-kosovo-police-officer-killed-and-one-wounded-raising-tensions-with-serbia)). The US ambassador in Pristina condemned the attack, while Serbia's parliamentary speaker accused Kurti of rushing to blame Serbs ([Associated Press](https://www.wcbe.org/npr-news/2023-09-24/one-kosovo-police-officer-killed-and-one-wounded-raising-tensions-with-serbia)). The attack followed May 2023 riots in northern Kosovo that left 93 peacekeepers hurt, and came after an EU-facilitated dialogue meeting between Kurti and Serbian President Aleksandar Vučić ended in acrimony earlier that month ([Associated Press](https://www.wcbe.org/npr-news/2023-09-24/one-kosovo-police-officer-killed-and-one-wounded-raising-tensions-with-serbia)).

## Deadlock, snap elections and a new government

In the February 2025 parliamentary election Vetëvendosje came first but the result was inconclusive, and months of deadlock froze some EU and World Bank funding and stalled reforms ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/kosovos-ruling-party-fails-to-form-government)). Kurti failed to win a mandate in an October 2025 parliamentary vote; his handpicked ally Glauk Konjufca then won only 56 votes in a second attempt, and President Vjosa Osmani moved to call a snap election ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/kosovos-ruling-party-fails-to-form-government)). Kurti's party came out on top in three legislative elections held since February 2025, including snap polls in December 2025 and June 2026 — but it could not muster the two-thirds majority needed to elect a president, and the deepening deadlock was widely tied to Kurti's hardline approach toward Serbia, which opposition parties said had alienated Kosovo's Western backers ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/13/kosovo-approves-new-government-after-months-of-political-deadlock)). Months of paralysis stalled the legislative reforms required for EU integration, threatening Kosovo's access to pre-accession funds ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/13/kosovo-approves-new-government-after-months-of-political-deadlock)).

In late August 2026 Kurti struck a deal with the opposition Democratic League of Kosovo (LDK), which holds 18 seats, to back law professor Bekim Sejdiu for the presidency — only for six LDK lawmakers to say a day later that they would not respect their leader's decision ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/13/kosovo-approves-new-government-after-months-of-political-deadlock)). On 13 September 2026 parliament approved a new Kurti-led government backed by 62 lawmakers, breaking three months of paralysis — but with the presidency still unresolved, another snap election, the fourth in only two years, remains possible if parliament fails to elect a president ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/13/kosovo-approves-new-government-after-months-of-political-deadlock)).
