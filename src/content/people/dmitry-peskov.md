## Overview

Dmitry Peskov is the longest-serving presidential press secretary of modern Russia, and the unusual thing about him is how he got the microphone: through translation. His official government biography says he graduated from Moscow State University's Institute of Asian and African Countries in 1989 with a degree in Oriental History and Translation, and walked into the Soviet Foreign Ministry that same year ([Russian Government archive](http://archive.government.ru/eng/persons/100/print/)). A decade later he was working inside the Kremlin's press service, and since May 2012 he has been the voice [Vladimir Putin](/people/vladimir-putin) uses to speak to the world ([OCCRP](https://www.occrp.org/interactives/russian-asset-tracker/en/person/5/dmitry-peskov)).

That is the thread standard biographies bury. Most profiles list "Turkologist" as a single word and move on to the sanctions lists. But the translation training is the whole explanation for Peskov's career: a man trained to render other people's words precisely became the man who decides which words get rendered at all. And it is not a closed chapter — in November 2017, at a Turkey-Russia-Iran summit in Sochi, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan walked up to him asking in English, "Peskov, how are you?" Peskov answered "Welcome" in Turkish, and Erdoğan laughed: "Why am I not saying 'iyi misin' to you?" — having momentarily forgotten the Kremlin aide's fluent Turkish ([Daily Sabah](https://www.dailysabah.com/diplomacy/2017/11/27/peskov-how-are-you-erdogan-forgets-about-kremlin-aides-fluent-turkish)).

## From Ankara to the press service

Peskov's career runs in two clean halves: ten years abroad as a diplomat, then the Kremlin. His first posting came in 1990, when he joined the Soviet embassy in Ankara as an administrative assistant on duty, later rising to attaché and third secretary through 1994 ([Russian Government archive](http://archive.government.ru/eng/persons/100/print/)). After two years back in Moscow at the Foreign Ministry (1994–1996), he returned to Ankara as second secretary and then first secretary, staying until 2000 ([Russian Government archive](http://archive.government.ru/eng/persons/100/print/)). That is a full decade of his working life spent in Turkey, the country whose language he trained in at university.

In 2000 he crossed from diplomacy into communications, becoming head of the department for relations with the mass media and deputy press secretary of the Russian president ([Russian Government archive](http://archive.government.ru/eng/persons/100/print/)). From 2004 to 2008 he served as first deputy press secretary ([Russian Government archive](http://archive.government.ru/eng/persons/100/print/)). Then came the 2008 presidency-to-premiership swap: when Putin moved to the prime minister's office under Dmitry Medvedev, Peskov followed, appointed press secretary of the prime minister on April 25, 2008 ([Russian Government archive](http://archive.government.ru/eng/persons/100/print/)).

## The dual role

When Putin returned to the presidency in May 2012, Peskov did not just get the top press job — he got two jobs. The OCCRP's Russian Asset Tracker describes him as presidential press secretary *and* deputy chief of the Presidential Executive Office, a pairing that makes him both the public mouthpiece and a senior administrator inside Putin's machine ([OCCRP](https://www.occrp.org/interactives/russian-asset-tracker/en/person/5/dmitry-peskov)). This matters because it explains his authority: he is not a hired announcer reading prepared lines. He has been inside Putin's press operations since April 2000, according to his career record, which means he has framed the president's public image through the Yukos era, the 2008 Georgia war, the Medvedev interlude, and everything since ([Russian Government archive](http://archive.government.ru/eng/persons/100/print/)).

## Why Putin talked to Carlson

The profile's most-cited moment as press secretary may be his February 7, 2024 briefing explaining why Putin had granted an interview to [Tucker Carlson](/people/tucker-carlson) the day before — the first Putin had given to a Western journalist since the invasion of Ukraine nearly two years earlier ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/putin-gave-tucker-carlson-an-interview-because-he-differs-from-one-sided-media-kremlin)). Peskov's reasoning was pure media criticism: the large Western networks, he said, "can in no way boast of even trying to at least look impartial in terms of coverage," and "these are all media outlets that take an exceptionally one-sided position" ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/putin-gave-tucker-carlson-an-interview-because-he-differs-from-one-sided-media-kremlin)). Asked directly why Carlson had won the slot, he said the American journalist's position "is in no way pro-Russian, it is not pro-Ukrainian — it is pro-American," and that this "starkly contrasts with the stance of traditional Anglo-Saxon media" ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/putin-gave-tucker-carlson-an-interview-because-he-differs-from-one-sided-media-kremlin)).

The formulation is worth reading carefully because it is how the Kremlin now defines interview-worthiness: not friendliness to Russia, but hostility to the Western media consensus. Putin's last formal interview with a US outlet before Carlson had been with CNBC's Hadley Gamble in October 2021 ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/putin-gave-tucker-carlson-an-interview-because-he-differs-from-one-sided-media-kremlin)).

## The September 2026 ceasefire arc

As of this writing, Peskov's most recent major statements trace a telling two-week arc on the question of an energy ceasefire. On September 15, 2026, he welcomed US President Donald Trump's proposal for a mutual halt to strikes on energy infrastructure, calling it "a very good idea, a good initiative" — but attached two conditions: strikes on Russian tankers must stop, and sanctions on energy supplies must be lifted ([The New Voice of Ukraine](https://english.nv.ua/nation/kremlin-backs-energy-ceasefire-but-ties-it-to-tanker-safety-and-sanctions-relief-50641600.html)). The context was Trump's September 14 claim that both Ukraine and Russia had already agreed to such a halt ([The New Voice of Ukraine](https://english.nv.ua/nation/kremlin-backs-energy-ceasefire-but-ties-it-to-tanker-safety-and-sanctions-relief-50641600.html)).

Nine days later the door closed. On September 24, Peskov said Russia wanted a peace settlement on Ukraine, not a temporary ceasefire — "we believe that it is peace that should be sought, rather than a temporary ceasefire," he said, adding that Russia's position "has not changed" ([The New Voice of Ukraine](https://english.nv.ua/nation/kremlin-rejects-ceasefire-says-it-seeks-no-temporary-settlement-50644280.html)). This came in direct response to Ukraine's offer of a full, immediate, unconditional ceasefire, which [Volodymyr Zelenskyy](/people/volodymyr-zelenskyy) had framed around three types: energy, grain, and maritime ([The New Voice of Ukraine](https://english.nv.ua/nation/kremlin-rejects-ceasefire-says-it-seeks-no-temporary-settlement-50644280.html)). The Institute for the Study of War, citing statements by Putin and Peskov, assessed that the Kremlin remained unwilling to agree to any deal short of its maximalist demands ([The New Voice of Ukraine](https://english.nv.ua/nation/kremlin-rejects-ceasefire-says-it-seeks-no-temporary-settlement-50644280.html)).

## Sanctions and controversies

Peskov was among the first wave of individuals sanctioned after the 2022 invasion: the EU blacklisted him on February 28, 2022, with the United States following on March 3 and Canada on March 6 ([OCCRP](https://www.occrp.org/interactives/russian-asset-tracker/en/person/5/dmitry-peskov)). On March 11, the US extended the measures to his wife and two adult children, with the Treasury saying they led "luxurious lifestyles that are incongruous with Peskov's civil servant salary" ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/3/12/new-us-sanctions-on-russian-billionaire-putin-spokesmans-family)).

The luxury angle had surfaced years earlier. On August 1, 2015, Peskov married his third wife, former Olympic champion Tatiana Navka, at an extravagant Sochi ceremony ([Global Voices](https://globalvoices.org/2015/08/02/russias-mustachioed-corruption/)). Days later, anti-corruption activist Alexei Navalny published evidence that Peskov was wearing a Richard Mille RM 52-01 watch worth at least $620,000 — roughly four of his annual declared salaries — a claim Peskov could not explain away on a civil servant's income ([Global Voices](https://globalvoices.org/2015/08/02/russias-mustachioed-corruption/)). Navalny's report noted that Peskov had replied that Navka had paid for the watch, a detail the income declarations could not support ([Global Voices](https://globalvoices.org/2015/08/02/russias-mustachioed-corruption/)).

An older quote still shadows him. In May 2012, after Moscow protests turned violent, Peskov said that protesters who hurt riot police should have "their livers smeared on the asphalt" — a line that caused an outcry among opposition activists ([Global Voices](https://globalvoices.org/2015/08/02/russias-mustachioed-corruption/)). It remains the sharpest illustration of how the translator-turned-spokesman handles dissent: not with diplomatic phrasing, but with threats.
