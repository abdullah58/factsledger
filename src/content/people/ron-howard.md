# Ron Howard

## Why Hillbilly Elegy was personal

Ronald William Howard was born March 1, 1954, in Duncan, Oklahoma
([Fandango](https://www.fandango.com/people/ron-howard-304060/biography)).
When he read J.D. Vance's memoir *Hillbilly Elegy*, what drew him in was not
just the story but the familiarity of it: Howard's own family heritage is
small-town, rural Oklahoma, and — exactly as Vance did — he addressed his own
grandmother as "Mamaw"
([Dove.org](https://dove.org/ron-howard-delivers-the-hillbilly-elegy/)).
He had been looking for a contemporary family story set in rural America
"for a long time," and meeting Vance and his family "deepened" that promise
([postPerspective](https://postperspective.com/director-ron-howard-talks-hillbilly-elegy-and-remote-post/)).

That personal alignment shaped the adaptation's boundaries. Howard turned away
from the socio-political overview parts of the book and from the "dumb tropes"
of hillbilly caricature, choosing instead to make it a family story
([Dove.org](https://dove.org/ron-howard-delivers-the-hillbilly-elegy/)).
He interviewed country-western singers and writers from Appalachia to get the
texture of the place, but found Vance's own stories and home movies the most
effective material. The result, he said, was "my first real family story
based on real events," centered on a rescue story rather than on villains —
and on the women in Vance's life: Mamaw (Glenn Close), his mother Bev
(Amy Adams), his sister Lindsey (Haley Bennett), and his girlfriend Usha
(Freida Pinto)
([Dove.org](https://dove.org/ron-howard-delivers-the-hillbilly-elegy/)).
[Glenn Close](/people/glenn-close) and [Amy Adams](/people/amy-adams) anchor
the cast of the Netflix film, with Gabriel Basso as [J.D. Vance](/people/jd-vance).

The family's one great anxiety about the project was getting Mamaw right
"with all her inconsistencies, warts and all," Howard said; Close built the
performance from conversations with the family and a few home movies, most of
which ended up in the title sequence, and the family was "emotionally
shattered" by what she did
([postPerspective](https://postperspective.com/director-ron-howard-talks-hillbilly-elegy-and-remote-post/)).

## Shooting a family saga through a shutdown

The shoot was, in Howard's words, "very hot and very fast": much of it on
location in Georgia and Ohio, with interiors shot at Screen Gems in Atlanta
([postPerspective](https://postperspective.com/director-ron-howard-talks-hillbilly-elegy-and-remote-post/)).
He worked with cinematographer Maryse Alberti for a "very naturalistic look"
and an unselfconscious approach, and editor James Wilcox — who had cut
Howard's *Genius* for television and stepped in after Howard's longtime
editors Dan Hanley and Mike Hill retired.

As editing progressed, Howard returned to Atlanta for four or five more days
of shooting to add and refine scenes, supported by Netflix. Production wrapped
in mid-March 2020 — "the whole film industry basically shut down due to
COVID," Howard recalled, "and by some miracle, no one on the set got it."
Everything after that was remote: post ran out of New York with Harbor Post,
the mix and color sessions were socially distanced, and even the score — his
ninth collaboration with Hans Zimmer, this time joined by David Fleming —
was done remotely after Zimmer got COVID. Howard asked the composers
specifically to avoid "the obvious regional sounds you might expect from
the title," saying he didn't want it to sound "anything like *Deliverance*."
The film's steel mill was also a fabrication: the crew shot in a factory and
Crafty Apes digitally designed the entire mill, extended the town, and handled
period fixes and cleanup
([postPerspective](https://postperspective.com/director-ron-howard-talks-hillbilly-elegy-and-remote-post/)).

The film drew criticism for how it handled the book's more political material.
Howard's answer was consistent with how he had framed the project from the
start: the memoir's more geopolitical aspects were "not what I felt a film
adaptation could really service and address." What interested him was
"focusing on the family and staying true and authentic about this family,
their world and their journey, and then allowing that to be a window into
some of these issues, instead of dealing with them in a more academic way."
The film was released globally on Netflix on November 24, 2020
([Dove.org](https://dove.org/ron-howard-delivers-the-hillbilly-elegy/);
[postPerspective](https://postperspective.com/director-ron-howard-talks-hillbilly-elegy-and-remote-post/)).

## From Opie to the director's chair

Few careers in American film bridge as much terrain as Howard's. He first
came to prominence as a child actor in the 1960s, playing Opie Taylor for
eight seasons on CBS's *The Andy Griffith Show* (1960–68). Unlike most child
actors, his career continued into his twenties: he starred in George Lucas's
Oscar-nominated high school drama *American Graffiti* (1973) and then became
Richie Cunningham, the affable center of ABC's *Happy Days* (1974–80),
leaving the show in 1980 to move behind the camera
([Fandango](https://www.fandango.com/people/ron-howard-304060/biography)).

He had already made his directorial debut with the low-budget comedy
*Grand Theft Auto* in 1977, plus a few TV movies. His first major theatrical
film was *Night Shift* (1982), which received mostly positive reviews and,
by Fandango's account, gave him the confidence to keep directing. Over the
next two decades came *Splash* (1984), *Cocoon* (1985), *Parenthood* (1989),
*Backdraft* (1991), and *Apollo 13* (1995), which earned two Academy Awards.
In 2002 he won his first two Academy Awards — Best Director and Best Picture
— for the 2001 biographical drama *A Beautiful Mind*, and later earned Oscar
nominations for Best Picture and Best Director for *Frost/Nixon* (2008)
([Fandango](https://www.fandango.com/people/ron-howard-304060/biography)).

Howard told Dove.org that this drift toward real-life material was not an
accident of scheduling: since 2000 he has made more "true life" stories
than fictional ones — *A Beautiful Mind*, *Cinderella Man*, *Rush*, *In the
Heart of the Sea* — outweighing the fictional and the fantastic. His first
film, made in high school, was actually a documentary about the Depression,
but he said he was "afraid" to go back to that kind of filmmaking until
*Apollo 13* taught him that documentaries and nonfiction films were more like
the fiction films than he had guessed
([Dove.org](https://dove.org/ron-howard-delivers-the-hillbilly-elegy/)).

## Producer and company man

Alongside directing, Howard is a noted television producer — most visibly
as the producer and narrator of the Emmy-winning comedy *Arrested
Development* (Fox/Netflix, 2003 onward)
([Fandango](https://www.fandango.com/people/ron-howard-304060/biography)).
He co-founded Imagine Entertainment with producer Brian Grazer in 1986, and
the two serve as the company's chairmen
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/imagines-michael-rosenberg-promoted-co-chairman-brian-grazer-ron-howard/)).
Imagine has been behind some of the most successful American films of the
past three decades, including *A Beautiful Mind*, *Frost/Nixon*, *8 Mile*,
and *J. Edgar*, plus the television shows *Parenthood*, *24*, and
*Arrested Development*
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/imagines-michael-rosenberg-promoted-co-chairman-brian-grazer-ron-howard/)).
Howard also turned to documentary directing late in his career, including
*The Beatles: Eight Days a Week* and the 2019 Luciano Pavarotti biography
*Pavarotti*
([Fandango](https://www.fandango.com/people/ron-howard-304060/biography)).
