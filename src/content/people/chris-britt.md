## Overview

Chris Britt is the co-founder and chief executive officer of [Chime](/organizations/chime/), the American financial technology company offering fee-free mobile banking. He founded Chime in 2012 with [Ryan King](/people/ryan-king/) after senior product roles at Green Dot and Visa, built the company around winning customers' direct-deposit primary accounts, and led it through near-collapse in 2016 to a [Nasdaq](/organizations/nasdaq/) listing in June 2025 ([Tulane University](https://freemannews.tulane.edu/print/pdf/node/3662); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/12/chime-almost-died-in-2016-turned-down-by-100-vcs-today-it-ipod-at-14-5b/)).

Britt grew up in a working-class apartment in Mount Vernon, New York, with his mother and two siblings, and has described a downstairs neighbour, Joe Sonneborn, a Second World War veteran, as the man who looked after him after school and helped pay his school and university fees ([Fintech Bits](https://www.fintechbits.net/chris-britt-chime-founder-profile)). He graduated from Tulane University in 1995 with a history degree and a minor in economics, and in 2024 Tulane named him its Distinguished Entrepreneur of the Year ([Tulane University](https://freemannews.tulane.edu/print/pdf/node/3662)). That background — a childhood in a household where money was tight — is the through-line of his career: every company he built afterwards was aimed at people traditional banks charged the most and served the least.

## The career before Chime

Britt began as a management strategy consultant with Accenture, then worked in business development at the ad-tech startup Flycast Communications and CMGI, and as a vice president and general manager at ComScore, before a senior product role at Visa ([Tulane University](https://freemannews.tulane.edu/print/pdf/node/3662)). In 2007 he joined Green Dot, the prepaid debit card company, and was its chief product officer when it went public in 2010 ([Fintech Bits](https://www.fintechbits.net/chris-britt-chime-founder-profile)).

Green Dot is where he found the idea behind Chime. Customers whose wages were paid straight onto its cards used them for nearly everything — "The people on direct deposit were clearly the whales of the business," he told Forbes ([Fintech Bits](https://www.fintechbits.net/chris-britt-chime-founder-profile)). He concluded there was a much larger opportunity than serving only the unbanked: mainstream Americans who had accounts at traditional banks but were unhappy with them, and a company that could profit from interchange on everyday spending instead of from fees ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-talks/chris-britt-f/transcript.pdf)). In his telling, the market was roughly 150 million Americans living paycheck to paycheck, while the top four banks earned $9 billion in deposit-account fees in the year before the pandemic alone ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-talks/chris-britt-f/transcript.pdf)).

## Founding Chime and the direct-deposit bet

Britt founded Chime in 2012 with Ryan King, a software engineer who had spent a decade at Plaxo, to build a consumer app that would use technology to lower the cost of delivering banking and "fundamentally profit with our members rather than from them" ([Tulane University](https://freemannews.tulane.edu/print/pdf/node/3662)). The name came from a branding agency shortlist arranged through investor Kirsten Green — although King had independently suggested "Chime" too, and joked that Britt just did not want the technical co-founder naming the company ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-talks/chris-britt-f/transcript.pdf)).

The strategy was deliberately contrarian: where other fintechs started with a lightweight product and hoped to earn their way into becoming a checking account, Chime went for the primary account from day one, designing benefits that unlocked with direct deposit — early pay, free overdraft, and later a credit-builder card ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-talks/chris-britt-f/transcript.pdf)). Early investors thought the founders were "nuts" for trying to get people to hand over their paychecks first ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-talks/chris-britt-f/transcript.pdf)).

The first version missed. Chime launched its debit card in April 2014 with shopping rewards and coupons as the hook, and told investors it would have 150,000 cardholders by the end of 2015 — but new cardholders were not setting up direct deposit, and by the end of 2015 the company had about 7,000 active users ([Fintech Bits](https://www.fintechbits.net/chris-britt-chime-founder-profile)). The full primary-account feature set only arrived in early 2016 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/05/31/no-fees-mobile-banking-service-chime-raises-70m-series-c-valuing-its-business-at-500m/)).

## A hundred no's, then the turnaround

In early 2016, trying to raise an extension to the Series A, the founders pitched about 100 investors and got 100 no's; the company was nearly out of money ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/12/chime-almost-died-in-2016-turned-down-by-100-vcs-today-it-ipod-at-14-5b/)). The rescue came from a single investor: [Lauren Kolodny](/people/lauren-kolodny/), then a partner at Aspect Ventures, led a $9 million extension — the only term sheet they had — buying in at 26 cents a share ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/12/chime-almost-died-in-2016-turned-down-by-100-vcs-today-it-ipod-at-14-5b/)). Kolodny was later invited to ring the opening bell at Nasdaq on IPO day ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/12/chime-almost-died-in-2016-turned-down-by-100-vcs-today-it-ipod-at-14-5b/)).

From 2018 the product began to catch on. A $70 million Series C led by Menlo Ventures in May 2018 valued Chime at roughly $500 million, by which point it had passed 1 million accounts and was adding well over 100,000 a month ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/05/31/no-fees-mobile-banking-service-chime-raises-70m-series-c-valuing-its-business-at-500m/)). During the pandemic, Chime gave members early access to government stimulus payments — closer to $7 billion across the stimulus cycles, Britt said — and temporarily raised its free SpotMe overdraft limits from $200 to over $1,000, producing the company's biggest enrollment day ever ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-talks/chris-britt-f/transcript.pdf)). Britt has said almost half of new enrollments each month come from referrals by existing members ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-talks/chris-britt-f/transcript.pdf)).

## IPO and becoming a bank

Chime priced its IPO at $27 per share on June 11, 2025, above its $24–$26 marketed range, raising $864 million; shares began trading on Nasdaq under the ticker CHYM the next day, opened at $42–$43, and closed at $37 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/12/chime-almost-died-in-2016-turned-down-by-100-vcs-today-it-ipod-at-14-5b/)). The company reported $1.3 billion in revenue in 2023 and $1.7 billion in 2024, with losses shrinking from $203 million to $25 million, and turned profitable in the first quarter of 2025 with $13 million of net income on $519 million in revenue ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/12/chime-almost-died-in-2016-turned-down-by-100-vcs-today-it-ipod-at-14-5b/)). Britt told [CNBC](/organizations/cnbc/) at the time that "two-thirds of our customer base use us as their direct deposit account and primary account relationship" ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/how-banking-app-chime-went-from-broke-to-ipo-billions/493263)).

The listing did not end the story Britt started in 2012 — it inverted it. Chime had always insisted it was a financial technology company, not a bank, with partner banks holding deposits ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/how-banking-app-chime-went-from-broke-to-ipo-billions/493263)). On September 8, 2026, it agreed to acquire Stride Bank, its bank partner of more than seven years, for $590 million in cash; on closing, Stride will become Chime Bank, N.A., a wholly owned subsidiary, with the deal expected to close in the first half of 2027 subject to regulatory approval ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/banking/2026/chime-inks-590-million-deal-to-buy-longtime-bank-partner-stride/)). Britt said the acquisition would accelerate Chime's vision "to be the largest provider of primary bank accounts in America" ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/banking/2026/chime-inks-590-million-deal-to-buy-longtime-bank-partner-stride/)). He has also framed the company's regulatory posture as a point of pride, telling [Goldman Sachs](/organizations/goldman-sachs/) that one of Chime's core values is "respect the rules" and that its products are built with banks regulated by the FDIC and OCC ([Goldman Sachs](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pdfs/insights/goldman-sachs-talks/chris-britt-f/transcript.pdf)).

Away from Chime, Britt serves on the board of CoachArt, a non-profit connecting chronically ill children with free lessons in the arts and athletics ([Tulane University](https://freemannews.tulane.edu/print/pdf/node/3662)). At Chime he founded the Chime Scholars Foundation, pledging 1% of Chime Financial equity to fund college and other post-secondary education for aspiring students ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/author/chris-britt/)).
