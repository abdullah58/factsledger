## Overview

Joseph Stalin (1878–1953) was the Soviet dictator who ruled the USSR from the late 1920s until his death, transforming it from an agrarian peasant society into an industrial and military superpower — at a cost historians estimate at 6 to 20 million lives ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). Born Josef Vissarionovich Djugashvili in Gori, Georgia, he took the name Stalin — "man of steel" — in his thirties, rose through the Bolshevik Party as its General Secretary from 1922, and outmaneuvered every rival after Lenin's death ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin)). In [World War II](/events/world-war-ii/) he first stunned the world by signing a pact with [Adolf Hitler](/people/adolf-hitler/), then directed the Soviet war effort that destroyed the German army in the east ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/josef-stalin)).

Britannica's assessment is unsparing: during the quarter-century before his death he "probably exercised greater political power than any other figure in history" — industrializing the USSR, forcibly collectivizing its agriculture, consolidating his position by intensive police terror, helping defeat Germany in 1941–45, and extending Soviet control over a belt of eastern European states ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin)).

## The bureaucrat who outlasted the revolutionaries

Stalin was Georgian, not Russian, and his official biography was long obscured by what Britannica calls a "mendacious Soviet-propagated 'legend'" exaggerating his heroic underground past ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin)). The verifiable outline: born 18 December 1878 (6 December Old Style) — though he later gave 21 December 1879 as his own birth date — the son of a poor cobbler, an alcoholic who beat him, and a devout washerwoman mother who had dreamed he would become a priest ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin); [HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). Childhood smallpox scarred his face; a seminary scholarship in Tiflis ended with his 1899 expulsion after he was caught reading Marx ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin)).

He entered the underground as "Koba," joined Lenin's Bolsheviks, funded the party with bank robberies, and was arrested repeatedly and exiled to Siberia between 1902 and 1913 ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). Lenin co-opted him onto the first Bolshevik Central Committee in 1912; after the 1917 seizure of power Stalin held ministerial posts, but it was the General Secretaryship of the Central Committee — his from 1922 — that became the power base of his dictatorship ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin)). His brilliant rivals, including Leon Trotsky and Grigory Zinovyev, despised the mundane organizational work of the secretariat and assumed the pockmarked Georgian was unintelligent — "a gross error, and one literally fatal in their case" ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin)). Even Lenin's political "testament" calling for Stalin's removal was discounted during Lenin's lifetime ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin)).

## Industrialization by terror

From 1928 Stalin launched the Five-Year Plans to force-march the [Soviet Union](/places/soviet-union/) from peasant society to industrial superpower ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). The centerpiece was forced collectivization: private farms were replaced with state-run collectives, and the kulaks — the more prosperous farmers — were shot or exiled in large numbers ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). The result was famine across the Soviet Union that killed millions, including an estimated four million in the 1932–33 Holodomor in Ukraine ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)).

He ruled by terror "with a totalitarian grip in order to eliminate anyone who might oppose him," expanding the secret police, encouraging citizens to spy on one another, and sending millions to the Gulag ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). From 1934 to 1938 he "cruelly purged the Soviet party, government, armed forces, and intelligentsia," and millions of so-called "enemies of the people" were imprisoned, exiled, or shot ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/josef-stalin)). Around himself he built a cult of personality: cities renamed in his honor, history books rewritten to inflate his revolutionary role, photographs censored to remove purged associates ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)).

## The pact, the invasion, and the alliance

The most disorienting move of Stalin's career came in August 1939. So entrenched was the image of the Soviet Union and Stalin as Nazi Germany's deadly enemy — street battles between Nazis and Communists, the arrest of Communist leaders after the Reichstag fire, Stalin's own works burned in the 1933 Nazi book burnings — that "many outside observers (and many Nazis as well) were shocked, even disoriented, by the short-lived Nazi-Soviet pact of 1939" ([USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/josef-stalin)). Stalin signed the [German–Soviet Nonaggression Pact](/events/german-soviet-nonaggression-pact/) with Hitler, then proceeded to annex parts of Poland and Romania and the Baltic states of Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania, and invaded Finland ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)).

On 22 June 1941 Germany broke the pact and invaded the USSR in [Operation Barbarossa](/events/operation-barbarossa/) ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). Stalin had ignored warnings from the Americans, the British, and his own intelligence agents, and the Soviets were unprepared; as German troops approached Moscow, he remained in the capital and directed a scorched-earth defensive policy ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). The tide turned at [Stalingrad](/events/battle-of-stalingrad/) — the city renamed for him in 1925 — where from August 1942 to February 1943 the Red Army defeated the Germans and began driving them from Soviet territory ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). He then joined the Allied conferences at Tehran (1943) and Yalta (1945), where his "iron will and deft political skills enabled him to play the loyal ally while never abandoning his vision of an expanded postwar Soviet sphere of influence" ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)).

That vision became the postwar order: he established communist governments throughout Eastern Europe, led the Soviets into the nuclear age with the 1949 atomic bomb, and in 1950 gave North Korea's Kim Il Sung permission to invade [South Korea](/places/south-korea/), triggering the [Korean War](/events/korean-war/) ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). He died on 5 March 1953 in Moscow at 74, after suffering a stroke; his embalmed body lay in Lenin's Mausoleum until 1961, when Khrushchev's de-Stalinization moved it to burial near the Kremlin walls — and the city of Stalingrad became Volgograd ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Why did Stalin change his birth date?**

He was born 18 December 1878 (6 December Old Style) in Gori, but later gave 21 December 1879 as his own birth date ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). Britannica notes the 1878 date was confirmed by records in the Communist Party central archives ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Joseph-Stalin)).

**How many people died under Stalin?**

Estimates vary too widely for a single figure: HISTORY.com cites a range of 6 to 20 million, through executions or indirectly through his policies ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)). About four million are believed to have starved in the 1932–33 Holodomor in Ukraine alone, and millions more were imprisoned, exiled, or shot as "enemies of the people" in the purges ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin); [USHMM](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/josef-stalin)).

**Was Stalin surprised by the German invasion in 1941?**

Yes, operationally. He had ignored warnings from the Americans, the British, and his own intelligence agents about a potential invasion, and the Soviets were not prepared for war when Germany broke the 1939 pact on 22 June 1941 ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)).

**What happened to Stalin's body after his death?**

He was embalmed and preserved in Lenin's Mausoleum in Moscow's Red Square until 1961, when it was removed and buried near the Kremlin walls as part of the de-Stalinization process initiated by his successor Nikita Khrushchev ([HISTORY.com](https://www.history.com/topics/european-history/joseph-stalin)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not give a single death toll for Stalin's rule — it reports the attributed 6–20 million range and the ~4 million Holodomor estimate, because the sources disagree. It does not assert that the young Stalin worked as a tsarist police informant; Britannica calls that "unproved speculation." It does not independently verify claims that the 1932–33 famine was intentionally worsened to target Ukraine; it reports the famine's scale as the sources state it. It does not cover the medical or political details of Stalin's death beyond the reported stroke. And it treats the WWII narrative at the level the sources support — the pact, Barbarossa, Stalingrad, Tehran, and Yalta — with fuller battle detail left to the dedicated profiles.
