## Overview

Maxwell Hutchinson is an English architect, broadcaster and former president of [the Royal Institute of British Architects](/organizations/royal-institute-of-british-architects/) (RIBA), who founded the humanitarian architecture charity [Article 25](/organizations/article-25/) after surviving the 2004 tsunami in Sri Lanka, and who presented architecture on television and radio as social history ([Fletcher Associates](http://fletcherassociates.net/Fletcher_Associates_2023/Fletcher_Associates___Agent___Maxwell_Hutchinson___Architect___Broadcaster___Biog.html)). He served as RIBA president from 1989 to 1991, ran a central London practice in the [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/) whose better-known buildings include Skylines on the Isle of Dogs and Aztec Row in Islington, and became one of Britain's most familiar broadcasters on architecture, presenting television series that treated buildings as social history rather than as scenery ([Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/); [Building](https://www.building.co.uk/focus/maxwell-hutchinson/1030083.article)).

Hutchinson's career is unusual in that it runs on three parallel tracks that rarely meet in one person: professional leadership, a working architectural practice, and mass-media presenting. Building magazine, profiling him when he had 25 years of broadcasting behind him, called him "the architectural profession's man on the box" ([Building](https://www.building.co.uk/focus/maxwell-hutchinson/1030083.article)). At the time of that profile he still ran a four-person practice in Clerkenwell, in London's then most fashionable architectural quarter, even while presenting prime-time television ([Building](https://www.building.co.uk/focus/maxwell-hutchinson/1030083.article)). That combination — a practising architect who could also hold a television audience — is the thread that connects his RIBA presidency, his broadcasting, and his charity work: in each case he was translating architecture for people outside the profession.

## Architecture and the RIBA presidency

Hutchinson reached the peak of his profession in 1989, when he became president of RIBA, a post he held until 1991 ([Building](https://www.building.co.uk/focus/maxwell-hutchinson/1030083.article); [Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/)). Article 25's board page confirms the same dates and the same role, listing him as its Founder and President ([Article 25](https://www.article-25.org/our-team)). The presidency matters because it placed a broadcaster-architect, rather than a purely commercial practitioner, at the head of the profession's institute at a moment when architecture's public image was increasingly made on television.

His built work, as recorded by his agency biography and repeated on Article 25's board page, is concentrated in London: Skylines on the Isle of Dogs, the JS Pathology headquarters at Camden Lock, Aztec Row in Islington, and Pink Floyd's recording studio in Britannia Row ([Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/); [Article 25](https://www.article-25.org/our-team)). Beyond individual buildings, he spent ten years as chairman of the Permarock Group of Building Product Manufacturing Companies, of which he was a founder, and was also chairman of Holland Hannen and Cubitts ([Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/); [Fletcher Associates](http://fletcherassociates.net/Fletcher_Associates_2023/Fletcher_Associates___Agent___Maxwell_Hutchinson___Architect___Broadcaster___Biog.html)). He was a visiting professor at the University of Westminster and previously at the Universities of Nottingham and Queen's Belfast, a teaching record that again shows the same pattern: practice, industry and education held together ([Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/); [Article 25](https://www.article-25.org/our-team)). The stat that best captures his dual career is a simple one: a four-person Clerkenwell practice and a prime-time television audience, pursued at the same time ([Building](https://www.building.co.uk/focus/maxwell-hutchinson/1030083.article)).

## Broadcasting architecture as social history

Hutchinson's most distinctive broadcasting achievement was No 57, The History of a House, a Channel 4 series in which he was both front man and originator: he helped to develop the idea and wrote an 80,000-word book to accompany the series ([Building](https://www.building.co.uk/focus/maxwell-hutchinson/1030083.article)). The series traced the social history of a Georgian house in Bristol over 200 years, and its first programme attracted 2.1 million viewers, about 10% of the national audience at the peak viewing time of 8 to 9pm ([Building](https://www.building.co.uk/focus/maxwell-hutchinson/1030083.article)). His agency biography gives the series as six hour-long programmes for Channel 4, with the accompanying book titled No. 57, The History of a House ([Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/)). The format is revealing: Hutchinson did not present architecture as a parade of styles, but as the story of the people who lived in a building — a method he repeated across his career.

Before No 57, he wrote and presented three sets of 15 half-hour programmes for the Discovery Channel — Prefabs and Palaces, Mod Cons and Pure Inventions — each focusing on a pair of buildings, from Blenheim Palace to a wartime prefab home ([Building](https://www.building.co.uk/focus/maxwell-hutchinson/1030083.article)). His wider television credits include Demolition Detectives for Channel 4, The Good, The Bad and The Listed for Carlton Television, Hidden Heritage for Anglia Television, Restoration Nation for the BBC, Inside Out for BBC London, How to Rescue a House and Visionaries: Wren Versus Foster for BBC Two, and The One Show and Songs of Praise for BBC One ([Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/)). On radio he was a regular contributor to BBC Radio 4's You and Yours, BBC Radio 2's Jeremy Vine Show and Radio 5 Live's Late Night Live, presented Landmarks and Lost London on BBC London 94.9FM, and fronted The Max Files on BBC London News for over five years ([Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/)). He was runner-up for the BT Regional News Presenter of the Year Award in 2000, was nominated for two Royal Television Society Awards, and won a Sony Award for Best Specialist Contributor for his contribution to BBC Radio London 94.9FM ([Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/); [Fletcher Associates](http://fletcherassociates.net/Fletcher_Associates_2023/Fletcher_Associates___Agent___Maxwell_Hutchinson___Architect___Broadcaster___Biog.html)).

His writing extended beyond broadcasting scripts. His book The Prince of Wales Right or Wrong – An Architect Replies was a best-seller published by Faber & Faber ([Gordon Poole Agency](https://www.gordonpoole.com/talent/maxwell-hutchinson/); [Fletcher Associates](http://fletcherassociates.net/Fletcher_Associates_2023/Fletcher_Associates___Agent___Maxwell_Hutchinson___Architect___Broadcaster___Biog.html)). Later, alongside broadcasting, he composed three stage musicals and a requiem mass, wrote the fictional novel Stones of Time, and chaired three series of lectures at the Royal Academy under the title The Architects Who Made London with Maxwell Hutchinson ([Fletcher Associates](http://fletcherassociates.net/Fletcher_Associates_2023/Fletcher_Associates___Agent___Maxwell_Hutchinson___Architect___Broadcaster___Biog.html)).

## Article 25 and ordained ministry

The charity work began, on Fletcher Associates' account, when Hutchinson survived the tsunami in Sri Lanka in 2004 and set up Article 25, then called Architects for Aid ([Fletcher Associates](http://fletcherassociates.net/Fletcher_Associates_2023/Fletcher_Associates___Agent___Maxwell_Hutchinson___Architect___Broadcaster___Biog.html)). By November 2007 the Architects' Journal could already report that Architects for Aid, "a charity founded in London by Max Hutchinson, is now sending architect volunteers to work on projects around the world" ([Architects' Journal](https://www.architectsjournal.co.uk/archive/architects-for-aid-diary)). Article 25's own board page now lists him as Founder and President, and describes him as a practising architect and regular broadcaster who has created and presented series for Discovery TV, BBC Two, Channel Four and Carlton Television ([Article 25](https://www.article-25.org/our-team)). The charity's later history — including the 2015 fraud that nearly closed it and its 2024 RIBA International Award — belongs to the organisation's own profile; Hutchinson's part, as documented here, is that of founder, not of day-to-day manager in those later years.

A second late-career turn is documented by Fletcher Associates: in 2014 Hutchinson was ordained as a deacon at St Paul's Cathedral by the Bishop of London, Richard Chartres ([Fletcher Associates](http://fletcherassociates.net/Fletcher_Associates_2023/Fletcher_Associates___Agent___Maxwell_Hutchinson___Architect___Broadcaster___Biog.html)). That ordination, coming a decade after the tsunami and the charity's founding, completes a portrait of an architect whose public work repeatedly moved between buildings, broadcasting and service — explaining buildings to television audiences, then building for communities with the least access to architecture, then serving a church community.
