## Overview

Ethel Percy Andrus (1884–1967) was an American educator who founded the American Association of Retired Persons — [AARP](/organizations/aarp/) — in 1958. Before that, she was the first woman to lead a secondary school in California, spending twenty-eight years as principal of Abraham Lincoln High School in Los Angeles ([USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology](https://gero.usc.edu/about/the-school/history/)), and she built AARP's institutional predecessor, the National Retired Teachers Association, from a 1947 idea into the first group health insurance plan for retirees over 65 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)).

That sequence — a long school career first, an organization second — is the part standard profiles skip. Most reference entries begin at 1958 and treat Andrus as a retiree who had an idea. The sourced record shows something more deliberate: decades of running a large urban high school, a research-driven discovery that retired teachers were living on pensions as small as her own $60 a month, a nine-year campaign to persuade insurers to cover people over 65, and only then a national organization ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)). She was 73 when AARP was founded, and the organization's "creative energy is ageless" philosophy was a description of her own life, not a slogan she invented for members.

## Twenty-eight years at Lincoln High

Andrus was born in San Francisco on September 21, 1884, the younger of two daughters of George Wallace Andrus and Lucretia Frances Duke, and grew up in Chicago after the family moved there for her father's legal education at the University of Chicago ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)). She graduated from Austin High School, taught English and German at the Lewis Institute (later the Illinois Institute of Technology), and volunteered at the Jane Addams-founded Hull House and Chicago Commons settlement houses ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)). In 1910 she returned to California, teaching at Santa Paula High School, then Manual Arts High School and Abraham Lincoln High School in Los Angeles — where her pupils included actors Robert Preston and Robert Young, and General James Doolittle ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)).

She became principal of Lincoln High in 1916 (USC's date) or 1917 (the encyclopedia's date) — the first woman in California history to hold such a post — and stayed twenty-eight years ([USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology](https://gero.usc.edu/about/the-school/history/); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)). Her administration at a school serving a diverse East Los Angeles community emphasized student self-respect and cultural pride; she founded the Opportunity School for Adults, an evening program for immigrant parents that expanded into a full-time evening school, and the school's juvenile-crime reductions earned special citations from the East Los Angeles juvenile court in 1940, with the National Education Association featuring Lincoln in its textbook *Learning Ways of Democracy* ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)). She earned her M.A. in 1928 and Ph.D. in 1930 from the University of Southern California while running the school, then retired from teaching in 1944 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)).

## From the NRTA to AARP: the insurance blueprint

Retirement was the pivot, not the end. As welfare director of the southern section of the California Retired Teachers Association, Andrus examined pensions and benefits for retired teachers nationwide and concluded a national organization was needed; in 1947 she founded the National Retired Teachers Association and became its president ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus); USC confirms the 1947 founding "to provide low-cost insurance programs for former educators" ([USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology](https://gero.usc.edu/about/the-school/history/))). Her great practical victory came in 1956, when she persuaded Continental Casualty to underwrite the first group health and accident insurance plan for retired persons over age 65 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)). Insurers had treated the over-65 as uninsurable; the NRTA plan proved otherwise, and requests from non-teacher retirees for comparable benefits followed.

In response, she established the American Association of Retired Persons in 1958 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)); the fact-checked financial encyclopedia confirms AARP was "founded in 1958 by retired educator Dr. Ethel Percy Andrus" ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/aarp.asp)), and USC's history credits her and insurance broker Leonard Davis jointly ([USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology](https://gero.usc.edu/about/the-school/history/)). She ran both organizations from Ojai, California — where she had established the Grey Gables retirement home for teachers in 1954 — and as AARP president she built out second-career programs, health insurance access, and travel programs while editing four Association journals, including [Modern Maturity](/creative-works/modern-maturity/), the monthly magazine of AARP ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus); [USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology](https://gero.usc.edu/about/the-school/history/)).

## Mail-order drugs, Congress, and a posthumous campus

Andrus's most concrete post-founding fight was over drug prices. In 1959 she created a nonprofit mail-order drug buying service for AARP and NRTA members, selling prescription medicines at least 25 percent below retail through pharmacist-staffed centers in California and Washington, D.C., delivered directly to members' doors ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)). Announcing the program, she said research showed "Americans over 65 years of age spend approximately ten percent of their average annual income for drugs and medications" — a stat that turned a pricing complaint into a policy argument, and an early citation-magnet pairing of data and implication: seniors were being priced out of medicine they needed to live, so she built the discount channel herself ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)).

That same year she took the argument to Congress twice. In July 1959 she opposed Representative A. J. Forand's health care bill, which would have funded coverage through an added payroll tax; she counter-proposed deducting voluntary premium costs from Social Security benefits, administered by a private board of trustees, objecting that the Forand bill "denied freedom of choice" ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)). In December 1959 she was back, protesting Parke, Davis & Company of Detroit for cutting off sales to the distributor supplying discount drugs to AARP and NRTA retirees ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)). Her governing philosophy — "creative energy is ageless," opposition to mandatory retirement laws, second careers after leaving a job — was lived before it was preached: in a 1954 *Time* interview she said of retirement, "when you leave a job, they often just give you a gold watch and all you can do is look at it and count the hours until you die." In 1963 she founded the Institute of Life Long Learning for retiree classes and seminars, served on the national advisory committee for the 1961 [White House Conference on Aging](/organizations/us-executive-branch/), and was named National Teacher of the Year in 1954 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)).

She died on July 13, 1967. The next year the AARP Andrus Foundation was established to fund aging research, and in 1973 the University of California, AARP, and the NRTA jointly established the Ethel Percy Andrus Gerontology Center — the first major research institution devoted solely to the study of aging — with the Leonard Davis School of Gerontology founded in the center's building two years later ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus); [USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology](https://gero.usc.edu/about/the-school/history/)). She was inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame in 1993 ([National Women's Hall of Fame](https://www.womenofthehall.org/women-of-the-hall/page/11/?view=list&sort=born&order=asc&sortby=inducted&sortdir=desc)), and the Extra Mile National Monument in Washington, D.C. lists her as an honoree — "Founder, AARP" ([Points of Light](https://www.pointsoflight.org/the-extra-mile-points-of-light-volunteer-pathway/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Who was Ethel Percy Andrus?** She was an American educator (1884–1967), California's first woman high school principal, who spent 28 years leading Abraham Lincoln High School in Los Angeles and then founded the National Retired Teachers Association in 1947 and AARP in 1958 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus); [USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology](https://gero.usc.edu/about/the-school/history/)).

**Did she found AARP alone?** She established it in 1958 after requests from non-teacher retirees for NRTA-style benefits; USC's institutional history credits her and insurance broker Leonard Davis jointly with the founding, and Davis later led the campaign that built her memorial gerontology center ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus); [USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology](https://gero.usc.edu/about/the-school/history/)).

**When was she inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame?** In 1993, per the Hall's own inductee listing. Some encyclopedia entries say 1998, but the awarding body's own record is the authoritative source for its honors ([National Women's Hall of Fame](https://www.womenofthehall.org/women-of-the-hall/page/11/?view=list&sort=born&order=asc&sortby=inducted&sortdir=desc)).

**Why did a school principal become an aging advocate?** Retiring in 1944 on a $60-a-month state pension made her study what retired teachers actually lived on; her welfare-director research at the California Retired Teachers Association convinced her that meager pensions and no health insurance were national problems requiring a national organization ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/ethel-andrus)).

**What is named after her?** The Ethel Percy Andrus Gerontology Center at the University of Southern California, dedicated in 1973 as the first major research institution devoted solely to aging, plus a medallion on the Extra Mile National Monument in Washington, D.C. ([USC Leonard Davis School of Gerontology](https://gero.usc.edu/about/the-school/history/); [Points of Light](https://www.pointsoflight.org/the-extra-mile-points-of-light-volunteer-pathway/)).
