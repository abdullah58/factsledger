## Overview

Stefan Batory is a Polish serial entrepreneur who co-founded [Booksy](/organizations/booksy/), the beauty and wellness appointment-booking platform, in 2014 and leads it as chief executive ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/27/booksy-raises-70m-war-chest-to-acquire-salon-appointment-apps-expand-internationally/)). He is a founder who learned the same lesson twice: his first venture, the software house [EO Networks](/organizations/eo-networks/), taught him that selling hours does not scale, and his second, [iTaxi](/organizations/itaxi/) — [Poland](/places/poland/)'s first taxi-hailing app — taught him that a marketplace can be strangled by whoever controls its supply side ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/10/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-2/); [Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). Booksy was designed around both lessons: global from day zero, aimed at a fragmented market with no gatekeeper in the middle. By January 2021 it had raised a $70 million Series C led by [Cat Rock Capital](/organizations/cat-rock-capital/), taking total funding to $119 million, and by 2023 it was profitable, with the US making up almost half its business ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/27/booksy-raises-70m-war-chest-to-acquire-salon-appointment-apps-expand-internationally/); [Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)).

Batory grew up in Poland in the late 1970s, when the country was still under communist rule, and as a child he loved mathematics — puzzles and problem-solving, at a time when even maths books were hard to come by ([StartupFundraising](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/stefan-batory)). That love of maths took him to university, where, while still studying, he and seven friends started a company in January 2000 — EO Networks, a software development house ([StartupFundraising](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/stefan-batory)). He never went to business school: "I learned it on the go," he said, calling the experience his own MBA ([StartupFundraising](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/stefan-batory)).

## EO Networks: the service-business trap

EO Networks began badly. The dot-com bust hit shortly after the January 2000 founding, the three co-founders meant to run the business side left, and Batory and his four remaining developer friends had to choose between folding or learning to run a company ([StartupFundraising](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/stefan-batory)). They voted for him to lead it, and for the next two years he lived, in his own telling, on a potato diet while they figured out marketing, sales and bookkeeping ([StartupFundraising](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/stefan-batory)). Once they got a handle on it, the business doubled or tripled its revenues for twelve consecutive years, becoming one of the 50 fastest-growing companies in Central and Eastern Europe and one of the 500 fastest-growing in Europe, the Middle East and Africa, before Batory took it public on the local stock exchange around 2011 or 2012 ([StartupFundraising](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/stefan-batory); [Growth Business](https://growthbusiness.co.uk/booksy-raises-3-4m-globalise-mobile-first-bookings-platform-15582/)).

The success contained the lesson that drove everything after it. After twelve years and more than 200 engineers, Batory was tired of a model in which doubling revenue meant doubling headcount: "To double the revenue, I had to double the headcount. When we got to over 200 engineers, I didn't feel like I wanted to hire another 200 to double our revenue the next year and then 400 to double it the following year" ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/10/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-2/)). He started looking for a business model that would let the top line grow without the team growing at the same rate — a search that led him, at the end of 2011, to launch the first taxi-hailing app in Poland ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/10/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-2/)).

## iTaxi: winning the product, losing the supply

iTaxi launched in February 2012 as a subsidiary of the software house, using only licensed drivers — all of whom worked for traditional taxi dispatching companies ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/10/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-2/)). Consumers loved the idea, but the dispatchers threatened drivers who joined, and the classic chicken-and-egg problem followed: drivers would not join without clients, and clients left without drivers ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/10/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-2/)). His partner, who ran the software house with him, wanted to kill the business a few months after launch; Batory instead bought it back, paying with his shares in the software business, and detached a classified-media division with it ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/10/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-2/)).

iTaxi survived and became the number-one taxi-hailing app in Poland, according to Growth Business ([Growth Business](https://growthbusiness.co.uk/booksy-raises-3-4m-globalise-mobile-first-bookings-platform-15582/)). But Batory learned its strategic limit the hard way: a locally successful app had little chance once Uber and the startup that became FreeNow entered the Polish market ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). When it came to Booksy, he and co-founder Konrad Howard decided not to repeat the mistake: "On Day Zero, we already knew that we wanted to make a global company. We wanted to be an Uber, not an iTaxi" ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). Fittingly, Booksy's first client, in 2014, was in the US rather than Poland ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)).

## Booksy: a physiotherapist, a marathon, and a pivot

The Booksy idea came from Batory's running. Training for the Marathon des Sables — a 250-kilometre multi-day ultramarathon across the Sahara, carrying his own food and supplies — left him with aching legs and a physiotherapist he could never reach: workouts ended at 9 or 10 p.m., the therapist was asleep, and daytime calls went unanswered because his hands were busy ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/11/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-3/); [ITKeyMedia](https://itkey.media/i-see-more-opportunities-in-the-crisis-than-threats-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy/)). Days of phone tag followed, and Batory realised the problem was general: service professionals who work with their hands cannot take calls while working, and after work they want to go home, not spend hours on the phone — yet their businesses depend on bookings ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/11/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-3/)).

He had a small division of the software house, and Konrad Howard — a friend he had worked with for a couple of years — joined him to manage it while Batory still focused on the taxi business ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/11/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-3/)). Together they visited hundreds of salons in Poland and the US over about three months, then started developing the platform in 2014 ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/12/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-4/)). The launch, at the end of 2014, ran through a partnership with the classified-media site TheFlyer.com, which acquired salons while Booksy handled the consumer side — but the per-booking-fee model failed, because 80% of appointments came from salons' existing clients, who did not want to pay a fee on them ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/12/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-4/)). The fix came from a barber in Los Angeles who asked to use Booksy just for her existing, fully-booked clientele and took 50 bookings in a day or two; within weeks Booksy pivoted to a transparent monthly subscription, the "merchant-first" model that differentiated it from lead-generation marketplaces such as [Treatwell](/organizations/treatwell/) ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/12/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-4/); [tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2017/03/03/booksy-series-a/)). The addressable market was large: Batory put the global beauty services industry at more than $150 billion with 4 to 6 million outlets, and over 90% of appointments still processed by phone and email ([Growth Business](https://growthbusiness.co.uk/booksy-raises-3-4m-globalise-mobile-first-bookings-platform-15582/)).

## The American bet — and the turn to profitability

Booksy's early growth in Poland and the US ran in tandem, but the US was always the priority: "The US has always been in first place for us and for the investors," Batory told Sifted ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). Before the seed round he gambled his meagre cash on marketing in the US through an Instagram account, barbershopconnect: "It was a gamble, we knew that if this succeeds, we'll raise a round, if it doesn't then we'll have to close the business. And it worked" ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). The funding followed: a $4.2 million Series A in March 2017 led by [OpenOcean](/organizations/openocean/) with German investor [Kai Hansen](/people/kai-hansen/) participating, a $13.2 million Series B in July 2018 when Booksy was seeing 2.5 million bookings a month, and the $70 million Series C in January 2021 ([tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2017/03/03/booksy-series-a/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/07/06/booksy-the-worldwide-booking-system-raises-13-2-million/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/27/booksy-raises-70m-war-chest-to-acquire-salon-appointment-apps-expand-internationally/)).

It was not a smooth ascent. Batory moved to California and relocated headquarters stateside, but progress for years was, in his words, "hard, bad and ineffective" ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). American investors liked the product but were unsure about founders who were not Stanford graduates, knew nobody and had never worked at Google: "It was a bigger risk to back a founder [like me]," he said ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). The US market was also structurally different — more fragmented, with many more independent contractors, and with ethnicity shaping which services people used, which "influences a go-to-market market, and it also influences unit economics" ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). Covid then devastated the beauty industry: Batory laid off 70% of staff worldwide and pivoted temporarily into new services such as online appointments, while also opening a new niche in bank-branch booking with BNP Paribas and Alior Bank ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc); [ITKeyMedia](https://itkey.media/i-see-more-opportunities-in-the-crisis-than-threats-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy/)).

The recovery came through the direct-sales model that had worked in Poland: after the Series C, Booksy relaunched it in a dozen US cities including New York, Washington, Boston and Las Vegas ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). But when the 2022 fundraising environment turned, Batory chose profitability over another raise, laying off nearly 40% of staff, postponing projects and cancelling the expensive field-sales model altogether ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). A year later he called it a good decision: "It turned out the flywheel was strong enough that we're still growing in the US" ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)). By then the company employed around 700 people — about 100 in each of its five local markets (US, Poland, the UK, France and Spain) and around 200 in the global team, mostly in Warsaw — and Inovia Capital, the backer of Lightspeed and Hopper, took a stake in November ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)).

## Running as method

Batory's running is not a hobby sidebar; it is the through-line of his public story. He took up running to lose weight after the IPO years, ran the Marathon des Sables three times — first to finish, then aiming for the top 100, then the top 50, which he achieved — and draws explicit parallels between desert racing and entrepreneurship: start before you feel ready, do not quit on the last mile, and focus on the next rock rather than the 50 kilometres still to go ([StartupFundraising](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/stefan-batory); [Refresh Miami](https://refreshmiami.com/news/from-the-sahara-to-south-beach-booksys-founder-turned-endurance-into-a-billion-booking-business/)). "If you can't run any more, then jog. If you can't jog, then walk. Just keep on moving," he said of both ([StartupFundraising](https://startupfundraising.com/library/articles/stefan-batory)).

The running also reshaped his life. He moved from Poland to Palo Alto in 2018, working brutal hours — often starting at 4 a.m. — to bridge California and European teams; then came Covid, followed by an autoimmune condition that forced him to rethink how he lived ([Refresh Miami](https://refreshmiami.com/news/from-the-sahara-to-south-beach-booksys-founder-turned-endurance-into-a-billion-booking-business/)). He came to Miami for a short stint in the sun, felt better, and stayed — a move he calls serendipity and "one of the best things that happened to me in my professional life," for the talent and networking he found there ([Refresh Miami](https://refreshmiami.com/news/from-the-sahara-to-south-beach-booksys-founder-turned-endurance-into-a-billion-booking-business/)). Booksy, by 2026, employed more than 1,000 people and powered hundreds of millions of appointments a year, and Batory's next ambition is AI matching: upload a hairstyle photo and your own image, and the platform assesses whether the look suits your face shape and hair texture and which nearby stylist can best deliver it ([Refresh Miami](https://refreshmiami.com/news/from-the-sahara-to-south-beach-booksys-founder-turned-endurance-into-a-billion-booking-business/)). His own summary of the plan is a marathon metaphor: "We're running a marathon and we're on the fifth kilometre. We don't want to lose the leadership position. We want to win this race" ([Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Who is Stefan Batory?**
The Polish co-founder and CEO of Booksy, founded in 2014 with Konrad Howard; before that he founded EO Networks, a software house he took public, and iTaxi, Poland's first taxi-hailing app ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/27/booksy-raises-70m-war-chest-to-acquire-salon-appointment-apps-expand-internationally/); [Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/10/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-2/)).

**How did Booksy start?**
From Batory's frustration booking physiotherapy appointments after late-night marathon training — phone tag stretching over days — and three months visiting hundreds of salons in Poland and the US with Howard before building the platform in 2014 ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/11/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-3/)).

**Why did Booksy switch to subscriptions?**
Because 80% of appointments came from salons' existing clients, who did not want to pay a per-booking fee on them; a Los Angeles barber using Booksy only for existing clients showed the subscription model worked, and Booksy pivoted within weeks of launch ([Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/12/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-4/)).

**How big is Booksy now?**
It raised $70 million in January 2021 (total $119 million then; $162 million per Dealroom later), was profitable by 2023 with the US at almost half its business, and employed more than 1,000 people by 2026 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/27/booksy-raises-70m-war-chest-to-acquire-salon-appointment-apps-expand-internationally/); [Sifted](https://sifted.eu/articles/how-booksy-broke-into-america?ref=newsletter.kaya.vc); [Refresh Miami](https://refreshmiami.com/news/from-the-sahara-to-south-beach-booksys-founder-turned-endurance-into-a-billion-booking-business/)).

**What is the Marathon des Sables connection?**
Batory ran the 250-kilometre Sahara ultramarathon three times, and the training injuries that left him unable to reach his physiotherapist gave him the Booksy idea ([ITKeyMedia](https://itkey.media/i-see-more-opportunities-in-the-crisis-than-threats-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy/); [Sramana Mitra](https://www.sramanamitra.com/2020/03/11/bootstrapping-with-services-from-poland-to-a-us-saas-company-stefan-batory-ceo-of-booksy-part-3/)).
