## Overview

Marsha Miro is a Detroit art writer, architectural historian, and filmmaker whose 21-year run as art critic of the [Detroit Free Press](/organizations/detroit-free-press/) (1974–1995) made her the critical voice of the Cass Corridor art movement, and who went on to found and direct the Museum of Contemporary Art Detroit (MOCAD) ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf); [Christie's](https://press.christies.com/release-masterpieces-of-art-deco-the-marsha-miro-collection/)). No SERP result tells her full arc in one place: born in Detroit, educated at the University of Michigan and the London School of Economics in the late 1960s, she worked as a gallery assistant in the Fisher Building and taught art history at Oakland Community College before the Free Press hired her and agreed to teach her journalism, since no journalist they could find knew enough about art ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)). Alongside criticism and museum-building, she became a noted collector of French Art Deco furniture, with her collection headlining a 2014 Christie's sale ([Christie's](https://press.christies.com/release-masterpieces-of-art-deco-the-marsha-miro-collection/)).

## Education and early career

Miro was born in Detroit, at Hutzel Hospital, and grew up in the city, attending James Vernor Elementary School and Mumford High School before going to the University of Michigan, where she earned a bachelor's degree with a major in political science and a minor in art history ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)). For her junior year she went to London and the London School of Economics, which had no separate junior-year program then, so she joined the regular school and was swept into student protests and the city itself — she recalls The Beatles and Mick Jagger playing in the cafeteria — and spent her time in the museums, deciding she was "much more interested in the arts than I was in the political science" ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)).

Back in Detroit she worked on her Master's in art history at Wayne State University, where she remembered teachers such as Art Schier, Bernard Goldman, and Wayne Andrews as being "as good as art historians teaching anywhere in the country," while working as assistant to the director at the Gertrude Kasle Gallery in the Fisher Building, where she met leading artists including abstract expressionists ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)). After graduating she taught art history at Oakland Community College for a couple of years until a recession cost her the job — as the youngest hire she was laid off first — and with few art jobs available, she applied for the art critic opening at the Detroit Free Press ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)).

## The Free Press years, 1974–1995

Miro got the job: the paper hired her on art-historical training and promised to teach her journalism, and she stayed as the Free Press's art critic from 1974 until 1995, leaving during the big newspaper strike ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)). At the time, the Free Press circulated over a million copies, and she saw the paper as a major forum that needed a consistent critical voice — the Detroit News already had Joy Hakanson Colby, "who was wonderful," but Miro wanted a second, different voice for the city ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)).

Her weekly rhythm was a story in the Sunday paper, a review in the Friday paper, and the art calendar with listings, plus art news as it broke ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)). That 21-year stretch exactly covered the Cass Corridor explosion — little of it started in 1971–72 when curator Sam Wagstaff was at the Detroit Institute of Arts, but the movement "really started growing" as she began writing, and she wrote about its artists at least once or twice a month ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)). Her reviews with national resonance traveled on the Knight Ridder News Wire and were printed across the country when a show had more than local importance; readership surveys found her column was read by 25 percent of readers — surpassed only by the sports pages, and ahead of the front pages — which won her more space, including more front pages of the lifestyle section ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)).

She was friends with the artists, visited their studios, went to openings and lectures together, and bought their work from the beginning, believing it important that they have enough money to live — while trying to keep the lines straight, writing positively when she believed in the work and gently when she thought an artist was not doing what they should ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)). She considered painters Gordon Newton and Michael Luchs the two most important Cass Corridor artists: Newton, who rebuilt the city over and over in his work and embedded early conservation themes about the Great Lakes and resource extraction; Luchs, the rural Ohio-born artist whose animal imagery tracked the city's decay back into nature ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)). She remembered three Detroit Institute of Arts moments above all: Michael Heizer's early-1970s earthwork on the DIA's front lawn at Sam Wagstaff's invitation ("one of the most important pieces in the early 1970s, period"), the "Kick Out the Jams" Cass Corridor show, and Jan van der Marck's "Interventions" ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)).

## Founding MOCAD

In 1995, Miro and gallery owner Susanne Hilberry decided Detroit needed a contemporary art museum, and MOCAD began ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf); [Mirror News](https://mirrornews.hfcc.edu/node/5513)). Their first path was a proposal to the Detroit Institute of Arts to integrate a modern-art wing into the DIA's collection; after about five years of study the DIA declined to fold modern works into its art-historical mission ([Model D](https://modeldmedia.com/mocad67/)). DIA board chairman Richard Manoogian, however, stayed enthusiastic about the concept and committed Manoogian Foundation funds toward purchasing a Woodward Avenue building, and Miro and Hilberry built an independent board and fundraising operation, with Miro serving as volunteer acting director ([Model D](https://modeldmedia.com/mocad67/)).

In January 2004, Miro and an advisory group including Masco Foundation and Manoogian Foundation directors Lillian Bauder, Sharon Rothwell, and Melonie Colaianne selected the site, and the team renovated a former car dealership on Woodward Avenue for the museum ([Mirror News](https://mirrornews.hfcc.edu/node/5513)). MOCAD held its grand opening on October 28, 2006 — "we needed a place that anyone would feel comfortable walking into," Miro said, adding that "art is critical for everybody, not just the artists, the people who can afford to buy it, or those initiated in the intellectual ramifications of it" ([Mirror News](https://mirrornews.hfcc.edu/node/5513)). On May 11, 2013, MOCAD was gifted artist Mike Kelley's Mobile Homestead, a life-size replica of his childhood home, which became a permanent installation ([Mirror News](https://mirrornews.hfcc.edu/node/5513)). Miro described her trajectory as founding director of the museum, then stepping aside when the board hired director Luis Croquer — she was a writer, not trained in curatorial work — and becoming president of the board, while returning to her Cranbrook book ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)); Christie's 2014 still listed her as founding director and board president ([Christie's](https://press.christies.com/release-masterpieces-of-art-deco-the-marsha-miro-collection/)).

## Cranbrook, filmmaking, and writing

When she left the Free Press, Miro took a job as architectural historian at the Cranbrook Educational Community in Bloomfield Hills, documenting the new architecture going up there; she made a film about Cranbrook's architecture, accumulated oral histories and interviews, and was halfway through a book about Cranbrook's architecture before pausing it to work on MOCAD, stopping at Cranbrook in 2004 ([Wayne State University Library](https://library.wayne.edu/resources/digital/audio/Miro_Interview.pdf)). Christie's confirms the Cranbrook role and describes her as an art writer whose criticism, collecting, and institutional work were of a piece — "the informed intelligence and the rigor that has marked her approach to life" ([Christie's](https://press.christies.com/release-masterpieces-of-art-deco-the-marsha-miro-collection/)).

## Art Deco collecting

Miro was drawn to Art Deco in the early 1980s, at a fortuitous moment when great pieces were coming to market for the first time from estates and clients' heirs, and she built a collection of exemplary furniture and objects displayed alongside 20th-century paintings and sculpture in her home ([Christie's](https://press.christies.com/release-masterpieces-of-art-deco-the-marsha-miro-collection/)). On December 9, 2014, Christie's New York presented "Masterpieces of Art Deco: The Marsha Miro Collection" as part of its 20/21 Design sale, with pieces by Edgar Brandt, Eugène Printz, Ernest Boiceau, Pierre Chareau, Jean Dunand, Pierre Legrain, and Emile-Jacques Ruhlmann ([Christie's](https://press.christies.com/release-masterpieces-of-art-deco-the-marsha-miro-collection/)). Headline lots included Ivan da Silva-Bruhns's circa-1930 carpet made for the Maharaja of Indore's palace (estimated $300,000–500,000), Paul Iribe's circa-1913 cabinet-on-stand ($120,000–180,000), and three Eugène Printz pieces — a unique asymmetrical sideboard ($300,000–500,000), a unique dining table ($120,000–180,000), and a set of six dining chairs ($40,000–60,000) ([Christie's](https://press.christies.com/release-masterpieces-of-art-deco-the-marsha-miro-collection/)).
