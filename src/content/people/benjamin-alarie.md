## Overview

Benjamin Alarie is a Canadian legal scholar and entrepreneur: professor of law and Osler Chair in Business Law at the [University of Toronto](/universities/university-of-toronto/), and co-founder and CEO of [Blue J](/organizations/blue-j/), the AI tax-research company. In 2016 he coined the term "legal singularity" for a future in which artificial intelligence makes law predictable and complete, a thesis he developed in two books and embodied in Blue J's products.

Alarie has held the Osler Chair since 2016 and has been a full professor since 2018 ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/people/benjamin-alarie)). His research sits at the intersection of tax law, artificial intelligence, legal theory and judicial decision-making, and he is the author, co-author or editor of nine books and collections and nearly 70 articles ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/people/benjamin-alarie)). His work, including the co-authored treatise *Canadian Income Tax Law*, has been cited by the [Supreme Court of Canada](/organizations/supreme-court-of-canada/) ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/people/benjamin-alarie)). He previously served as Associate Dean of the First Year Program ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/people/benjamin-alarie); [Law.com](https://www.law.com/legaltechnews/2026/11/10/tax-law-platform-blue-j-raises-176m-series-e-round/)).

His books trace a single arc from scholarship to company to institutional redesign:

| Book | Year | Co-author | Publisher |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Commitment and Cooperation on High Courts* | 2017 | Andrew J. Green | Oxford University Press |
| *Canadian Income Tax Law* (6th edition) | 2018 | Co-author, several editions | LexisNexis |
| *The Legal Singularity* | 2023 | Abdi Aidid | University of Toronto Press |
| *Superjustice* | 2026 | Samuel I. Becher | Oxford University Press |

The through-line is prediction: first studying how judges actually decide, then arguing that machine prediction could make law complete, then building a company that sells prediction to tax professionals — and now asking how legal institutions should be redesigned once prediction is cheap.

## Education, clerkship and early academic career

Alarie earned an M.A. in economics from the University of Toronto in 2002 ([University of Toronto Alumni](https://alumni.utoronto.ca/news/globe-and-mails-2022-changemakers-list-shines-light-impressive-u-t-alumni)), and holds an M.A. and J.D. from Toronto and an LL.M. from [Yale Law School](/schools/yale-law-school/) ([Toronto Computer Lawyers Group](https://www.tclg.org/events/disruptivetechnologies/)). Before his academic appointment he was a law clerk to Madam Justice Louise Arbour at the Supreme Court of Canada in 2003–2004 ([Toronto Computer Lawyers Group](https://www.tclg.org/events/disruptivetechnologies/)), and he joined the University of Toronto's Faculty of Law as a full-time professor in 2004 ([Legal Talk Network](https://legaltalknetwork.com/guests/benjamin-alarie/)).

He teaches and researches in taxation law and judicial decision-making ([Legal Talk Network](https://legaltalknetwork.com/guests/benjamin-alarie/)), and in 2009 the law school's graduating class awarded him the Alan Mewett QC Prize for excellence in teaching ([Legal Talk Network](https://legaltalknetwork.com/guests/benjamin-alarie/)). His research has been funded by the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council, the Canadian Foundation for Innovation and the Ontario Ministry of Research and Innovation ([Legal Talk Network](https://legaltalknetwork.com/guests/benjamin-alarie/)). He is also an affiliated faculty member at the Vector Institute for Artificial Intelligence ([MaRS](https://impactai.marsdd.com/speaker/benjamin-alarie/)), and his work has appeared in journals including the *Canadian Tax Journal*, the *British Tax Review* and *[Tax Notes](/organizations/tax-notes/)* ([AICPA](https://agenda.aicpastore.com/speakers/63971-benjamin-alarie)).

## Taxation and judicial decision-making research

Alarie's empirical work asks whether judges decide tax cases on the law or on something else. With Andrew J. Green, he studied about 3,400 decisions of the Tax Court of Canada from 2000 to 2006, together with related appeals to the Federal Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court of Canada ([EconPapers](http://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ctf:journl:v:62:y:2014:i:4:p:985-1027)). The study, "Policy Preferences and Expertise in Canadian Tax Adjudication," published in the *Canadian Tax Journal* in 2014, reached three conclusions: judges' policy preferences matter, but not that much; resources matter — a lot; and the Federal Court of Appeal's affirmance patterns are difficult to explain, possibly reflecting a desire to avoid the apprehension of bias ([EconPapers](http://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ctf:journl:v:62:y:2014:i:4:p:985-1027)).

He extended the question comparatively in *Commitment and Cooperation on High Courts* (Oxford University Press, 2017, with Andrew J. Green), which asks four institutional questions — who decides, how an appeal reaches the court, who is before the court, and how the court decides — of the supreme courts of the United States, the United Kingdom, [Canada](/places/canada/) and India and the High Court of Australia ([University of Toronto Law Journal](https://www.utpjournals.press/doi/abs/10.3138/utlj.2018-0060)). A review essay in the *University of Toronto Law Journal* praised the book for taking institutions seriously through cross-national assessment, calling it "bound to take its place among the classics in the ever-growing field of judicial behaviour" ([University of Toronto Law Journal](https://www.utpjournals.press/doi/abs/10.3138/utlj.2018-0060)). Alongside this research he co-authored several editions of *Canadian Income Tax Law*, a leading text, including the 6th edition published by LexisNexis in 2018 ([Legal Talk Network](https://legaltalknetwork.com/guests/benjamin-alarie/)).

## The legal singularity: from a 2016 article to two books

In 2016 Alarie published a short article in the *University of Toronto Law Journal*, "The Path of the Law: Towards Legal Singularity," arguing that legal prediction was about to become far more achievable than lawyers assumed. Building on Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.'s 1897 prediction that "the man of the future is the man of statistics," he proposed the term "legal singularity" for the culmination of cheap data and machine learning in law ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2767835)). He predicted three gradual transitions: from standards adjudicated after the fact to query-able systems of rules knowable in advance; to superior and increasingly complete specifications of tax law; and finally to a complete specification of law in positive and normative equilibrium, a form of reflective equilibrium in John Rawls's sense ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2767835)).

He developed the thesis with Abdi Aidid in *The Legal Singularity: How Artificial Intelligence Can Make Law Radically Better* (University of Toronto Press, 2023), which argues that AI-enabled prediction can make law more predictable, coherent and fair, and devotes a chapter to answering the thesis's critics ([The Legal Singularity](https://legalsingularity.com)). The book won the 2024 Association of American Publishers PROSE Award in Legal Studies and Criminology and was shortlisted for the 2023 Donner Prize, which honours the best public policy book by a Canadian author ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/celebrates/abdi-aidid-and-benjamin-alarie-recognized-association-american-publishers); [University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/book-awards)). With Samuel I. Becher he then extended the argument from prediction to institutional design in *Superjustice: Law in the Age of Artificial Intelligence* (Oxford University Press, hardcover 30 July 2026), which asks how entire legal systems should be redesigned once the constraints of scarcity, delay and inconsistency can be relaxed by computation ([The Legal Singularity](https://legalsingularity.com)).

## Blue J: from prediction to generative AI

Alarie co-founded Blue J in 2015 with fellow University of Toronto law professors [Anthony Niblett](/people/anthony-niblett/) and [Albert Yoon](/people/albert-yoon/), and recruited Brett Janssen — a childhood friend of Niblett's — as chief technology officer ([Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/resources/legal-technology/blue-j-legal-co-founder-benjamin-alarie-on-how-ai-is-powering-a-new-generation-of-legal-tech/374021)). The original product, Tax Foresight, collected and analysed the facts and findings from thousands of cases to predict what a court would decide in new situations, and in 2016 the company signed an exclusive distribution agreement with [Thomson Reuters](/organizations/thomson-reuters/) ([University of Toronto](https://2016.research.utoronto.ca/innovation.html)). Alarie described the early system as "islands of predictability" — dozens of tax predictors and an employment-law module predicting reasonable notice on dismissal — separated by a lot of ocean ([Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/resources/legal-technology/blue-j-legal-co-founder-benjamin-alarie-on-how-ai-is-powering-a-new-generation-of-legal-tech/374021)).

The generative-AI turn closed that ocean. Blue J rebuilt its platform around large language models paired with its predictive models and primary materials, launching the Ask Blue J chat product, and by December 2024 it could produce answers within 10 seconds at 95-per-cent-plus accuracy, with technical back-and-forth conversation on complex tax matters ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/u-t-spinout-led-benjamin-alarie-raises-167-million-demand-its-chatgpt-chatbot-tax-pros-takes)). Alarie's account of the shift is that tax research is moving from manual triangulation across keyword and Boolean search to automated synthesis: users upload client documents, the system synthesises multi-million-page corpora of case law and guidance, and every answer carries traceable citations so it can be verified against the statutory text ([Accountancy Age](https://accountancyage.com/2026/03/30/benjamin-alarie-blue-j-tax-research-genai-evolution/)). The same tools let advisers simulate the strongest argument a tax authority might make and find the precise "flip point" where changing one fact turns a compliant position non-compliant ([Accountancy Age](https://accountancyage.com/2026/03/30/benjamin-alarie-blue-j-tax-research-genai-evolution/)).

Blue J's customers include the Big Four accounting firms in Canada and the federal government — the [Canada Revenue Agency](/organizations/canada-revenue-agency/) and the Department of Justice use its tax platforms, and many departments use its labour-and-employment product — while KPMG in the UK opened the route into the British market ([Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/resources/legal-technology/blue-j-legal-co-founder-benjamin-alarie-on-how-ai-is-powering-a-new-generation-of-legal-tech/374021)). Its funding rounds track the product's arc:

| Round | Announced | Amount | Lead / note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Series B | August 2021 | US$9 million | Announced August 2021; four rounds raised in total by February 2023 |
| Series D | August 2025 | US$122 million (C$167 million) | Oak HC/FT and Sapphire Ventures |
| Series E | 10 November 2026 | US$176 million | JMI Equity; valuation above US$700 million |

At the Series E, more than 6,500 organisations and over 45,000 tax professionals used Blue J, which had answered more than 14 million tax questions across Canadian, US and UK tax law, and annual recurring revenue had grown 165% in the 15 months since the Series D ([Blue J](https://www.bluej.com/blog/blue-j-announces-series-e-funding)). The new capital is earmarked for Workflows, agentic AI that carries tax work from the first question to a finished deliverable — reviewing returns, preparing IRS notice responses, analysing agreements and drafting client communications — and for expansion in the UK and internationally ([Blue J](https://www.bluej.com/blog/blue-j-announces-series-e-funding)). "An answer is rarely the end of a tax professional's job," Alarie said at the announcement. "It's the beginning of the work a client needs done." ([Blue J](https://www.bluej.com/blog/blue-j-announces-series-e-funding))

## Recognition

In 2022 the [Globe and Mail](/organizations/globe-and-mail/) named Alarie one of its Top 50 Changemakers, emerging leaders reinventing how Canada does business ([University of Toronto Alumni](https://alumni.utoronto.ca/news/globe-and-mails-2022-changemakers-list-shines-light-impressive-u-t-alumni)). The same year he became the subject of *A.I. Taxman*, an educational documentary from UDocs, produced with Hot Docs, that premiered at Toronto's Isabel Bader Theatre on 29 September, with a screening followed by a keynote from [Marshall Rothstein](/people/marshall-rothstein/), a retired justice of the Supreme Court of Canada ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/ai-taxman-documentary-focuses-u-t-law-prof-who-launched-legal-tech-startup)). The film's premise is his own summary of Blue J's purpose: to use machine learning to give taxpayers certainty they are not overpaying, and to give the tax authority clear boundaries on what is owed ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/ai-taxman-documentary-focuses-u-t-law-prof-who-launched-legal-tech-startup)). **What is the legal singularity?** The legal singularity is Benjamin Alarie's term, coined in a 2016 *University of Toronto Law Journal* article, for a future in which artificial intelligence makes law functionally complete — predictable, coherent and knowable in advance rather than argued after the fact ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2767835)). He developed the idea in *The Legal Singularity* (2023) and *Superjustice* (2026) ([The Legal Singularity](https://legalsingularity.com)).

**What is Blue J?** Blue J is an AI tax research and advisory platform co-founded by Alarie in 2015, which gives tax professionals technical analysis grounded in authoritative sources ([Blue J](https://www.bluej.com/blog/blue-j-announces-series-e-funding)). More than 6,500 organisations use it, and it has answered over 14 million tax questions across Canadian, US and UK tax law ([Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/cl-plus/deals/4411)).

**What books has Benjamin Alarie written?** His books include *Commitment and Cooperation on High Courts* (2017, with Andrew J. Green), several editions of *Canadian Income Tax Law*, *The Legal Singularity* (2023, with Abdi Aidid) and *Superjustice* (2026, with Samuel I. Becher) ([University of Toronto Law Journal](https://www.utpjournals.press/doi/abs/10.3138/utlj.2018-0060); [The Legal Singularity](https://legalsingularity.com)).

**What is Benjamin Alarie's role at the University of Toronto?** He is Professor of Law and Osler Chair in Business Law at the Faculty of Law, a chair he has held since 2016, and has been a full professor since 2018 ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/people/benjamin-alarie)). He joined the faculty in 2004 and previously served as Associate Dean of the First Year Program ([Legal Talk Network](https://legaltalknetwork.com/guests/benjamin-alarie/)).
