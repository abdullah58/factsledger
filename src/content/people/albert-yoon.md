## Overview

Albert Hyun Yoon is a professor of law at the [University of Toronto](/universities/university-of-toronto/) Faculty of Law, where he holds the Michael J. Trebilcock Chair in Law and Economics. He is an empirical legal scholar who studies the legal profession, judicial behaviour, and the effects of technology on law — and a co-founder of [Blue J](/organizations/blue-j/), the [Toronto](/places/toronto/) legal-technology company that uses artificial intelligence to answer tax, labour and employment law questions. In 2022 he was named a Pierre Elliott Trudeau Foundation Fellow, one of five scholars chosen for the foundation's 2022–2025 Scientific Cycle on "Global Economies" ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/celebrates/albert-yoon-named-2022-pierre-elliott-trudeau-foundation-fellow)).

Yoon took his bachelor's degree at Yale, then earned both his law degree and his doctorate in political science at [Stanford University](/universities/stanford-university/), where he was senior articles editor of the *Stanford Law Review* ([Pierre Elliott Trudeau Foundation](https://www.trudeaufoundation.ca/directory/albert-yoon/)). After clerking on the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, he taught at Northwestern University from 2001 to 2008, then joined Toronto, where he has been a professor since 2011 ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/role-position-person/full-time-faculty)). What sets his scholarship apart is method: he treats the legal system as a source of data — randomly assigned cases, bar-exam scores, law-school application choices, experiments on practising lawyers — and uses it to test claims the profession usually takes on faith.

## Education and early career

Yoon received his B.A. from Yale College in 1991, in English and Political Science, graduating cum laude ([Albert Hyun Yoon CV](https://www.russellsage.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/ahy_cv_feb2023.pdf)). He then spent 1992–99 at Stanford, taking his J.D. from Stanford Law School in 1998 and his Ph.D. in Political Science in 1999, advised by John Ferejohn ([Albert Hyun Yoon CV](https://www.russellsage.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/ahy_cv_feb2023.pdf)). Before academia he worked as a consultant in the Criminal Justice Division of the RAND Corporation (1996–98) ([Albert Hyun Yoon CV](https://www.russellsage.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/ahy_cv_feb2023.pdf)).

After graduating he clerked for Judge R. Guy Cole, Jr., of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit in 1998–99, then spent 1999 to 2001 as a Robert Wood Johnson Scholar in Health Policy Research at UC Berkeley ([Pierre Elliott Trudeau Foundation](https://www.trudeaufoundation.ca/directory/albert-yoon/)). He joined Northwestern University in 2001, rising from assistant professor to full professor by 2005 and holding the Benjamin Mazur Research Professorship in 2003 ([Albert Hyun Yoon CV](https://www.russellsage.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/ahy_cv_feb2023.pdf)). During those years he was a Law and Public Affairs Fellow at [Princeton University](/universities/princeton-university/) (2003–04) and later a visiting scholar at the Russell Sage Foundation (2008–09) ([Pierre Elliott Trudeau Foundation](https://www.trudeaufoundation.ca/directory/albert-yoon/)). He joined the University of Toronto Faculty of Law in 2008 as an associate professor and became a full professor in 2011; he also holds a courtesy appointment in political science and is a faculty affiliate of the Vector Institute ([Albert Hyun Yoon CV](https://www.russellsage.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/ahy_cv_feb2023.pdf)).

## Does the lawyer matter? Measuring attorney ability

Yoon's best-known study asks a question the legal profession rarely answers with evidence: how much difference does the individual lawyer make? In "The Luck of the Draw: Using Random Case Assignment to Investigate Attorney Ability" (2007, with David S. Abrams), published in the *University of Chicago Law Review*, the two exploited a natural experiment — the Clark County Public Defender's Office in Las Vegas assigns incoming felony cases randomly among its attorneys, removing the usual problem that better lawyers get better cases ([University of Chicago Law Review](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/74_4_Abrams_Yoon.pdf)). The headline result: a veteran public defender with ten years of experience reduced the average length of incarceration by 17 percent relative to a defender in her first year ([University of Chicago Law Review](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/74_4_Abrams_Yoon.pdf)).

The study also produced findings that cut against conventional wisdom. There was no statistical difference in outcomes based on which law school the attorney had attended, or on gender — but the attorney's race correlated with sentence length, with Hispanic attorneys obtaining sentences up to 26 percent shorter on average than those obtained by Black or white attorneys ([University of Chicago Law Review](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/74_4_Abrams_Yoon.pdf)). The authors found evidence that at least part of the sentencing gap ran through different plea-bargaining behaviour ([University of Chicago Law Review](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/74_4_Abrams_Yoon.pdf)). In 2007 Yoon shared the Ronald H. Coase Prize with Abrams and was elected a member of the American Law Institute ([Pierre Elliott Trudeau Foundation](https://www.trudeaufoundation.ca/directory/albert-yoon/)).

## Rethinking law-school rankings

In 2024 Yoon returned to how law schools are judged, with a paper that arrived at a charged moment. In the fall of 2022, several of the most selective U.S. law schools formally withdrew from the *U.S. News & World Report* rankings, exposing longstanding criticism of their opaque methodology ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/empleg/v21y2024i2p279-336.html)). In "Rankings without U.S. News: A Revealed Preference Approach to Evaluating Law Schools", published in the *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies* with Jesse Rothstein, Yoon built an alternative from behaviour rather than reputation: using individual-level data from the Law School Admission Council, they analysed the universe of applicants to U.S. law schools from 1988 through 2017 and ranked schools solely by where applicants chose to matriculate given their offers of admission ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/empleg/v21y2024i2p279-336.html)).

The approach needs no survey data from schools and imposes no arbitrary weights on factors such as LSAT scores or spending, which the authors argue minimises the scope for manipulation ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/empleg/v21y2024i2p279-336.html)). Its verdict was twofold: matriculants reveal a strong preference for a handful of the most selective schools, but outside that top tier they do not appear to draw meaningful distinctions between schools ranked adjacently or even near each other ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/empleg/v21y2024i2p279-336.html)). Much of the ranking ladder below the elite, in other words, is noise.

## Legal ethics, tested experimentally

Yoon's recent scholarship has turned from measuring lawyers to testing how they think about their own ethics. In "In the Eye of the Beholder: How Lawyers Perceive Legal Ethical Problems" (2025, *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies*, solo-authored), he ran an experiment on licensed lawyers in Ontario, randomly assigning fact scenarios in which respondents stood to benefit or lose from ethically questionable conduct ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/empleg/v22y2025i3p345-360.html)). A large majority in each group found that the conduct violated the rules of professional conduct — but respondents were less likely to reach that conclusion when they themselves benefited ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/empleg/v22y2025i3p345-360.html)).

The second finding is the more unsettling one. When asked how most other lawyers would respond, a smaller percentage of both groups thought their peers would find a violation — and the gap between first-person and peer perceptions grew larger precisely when respondents agreed most strongly that a violation had occurred ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/empleg/v22y2025i3p345-360.html)). Lawyers, in other words, are confident their own commitment to the rules exceeds that of their fellow lawyers — and Yoon argues that where actions count as much as beliefs, this may affect lawyers' own fidelity to the rules ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/empleg/v22y2025i3p345-360.html)).

He pursued the same theme at scale in "Determinants of Attorney Misconduct: A Study of California Lawyers", presented at UC Irvine Law in January 2025 and at the University of Hong Kong in April 2025 ([UC Irvine Law](https://www.law.uci.edu/centers/cerlp/activities/)). Using data from the State Bar of California, the study examines the universe of lawyers admitted in the state from 1990 to 2023, with anonymised individual-level data on education, bar performance, demographics and discipline history ([University of Hong Kong Faculty of Law](https://www.law.hku.hk/events/determinants-of-attorney-misconduct-a-study-of-california-lawyers/)). Lawyers with the highest rates of investigation and discipline came disproportionately from graduates of less selective law schools and those receiving low passing scores on the state bar exam; gender and ethnicity were also strong predictors ([University of Hong Kong Faculty of Law](https://www.law.hku.hk/events/determinants-of-attorney-misconduct-a-study-of-california-lawyers/)). But Yoon's argument is that these factors alone provide an incomplete and likely misleading narrative — the regulatory framework is largely reactive, and regulators could instead proactively identify lawyers at high risk of discipline and provide training and resources ([University of Hong Kong Faculty of Law](https://www.law.hku.hk/events/determinants-of-attorney-misconduct-a-study-of-california-lawyers/)).

## Law, technology, and Blue J

Yoon's third strand of work asks what machine learning does to legal practice itself. With [Benjamin Alarie](/people/benjamin-alarie/) and [Anthony Niblett](/people/anthony-niblett/) he published "Using Machine Learning to Predict Outcomes in Tax Law" in 2016 and "Technology and the Future of the Legal Profession" in 2018, and the three were at work on "Regulation by Machine" for the *Journal of Machine Learning Research* ([Albert Hyun Yoon CV](https://www.russellsage.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/ahy_cv_feb2023.pdf)). The scholarship and the company grew from the same idea: if patterns in past decisions can be measured, they can be used to predict future ones.

That idea became Blue J. Alarie founded the company in 2015 alongside fellow Faculty of Law members Yoon and Niblett, plus former IBM enterprise software developer Brett Janssen ([University of Toronto News](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/u-t-startup-blue-j-legal-raises-us7-million-plans-cross-border-expansion)). Blue J began with an AI-powered prediction engine for tax-law cases and expanded into labour and employment law, business diagramming and tax research, launching a generative-AI tax research solution in 2023 ([Blue J](https://www.bluej.com/about-us)). Yoon remains a co-founder and now serves as Senior Academic Advisor, supporting academic adoption of Blue J by tax faculty across North America and the United Kingdom and helping guide the company's research and product strategy ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/role-position-person/full-time-faculty)). In November 2026 the company raised a US$176 million Series E round led by JMI Equity ([Blue J](https://www.bluej.com/about-us)). One detail does not line up: Yoon's own CV dates his Blue J role from 2016, a year after the founding date given by the company and by the University of Toronto — see "What this profile does not claim" below.

## Honours and later career

Yoon was named a Pierre Elliott Trudeau Foundation Fellow in 2022, one of five scholars chosen for the foundation's 2022–2025 Scientific Cycle, "Global Economies" ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/celebrates/albert-yoon-named-2022-pierre-elliott-trudeau-foundation-fellow)). The same year he became a Fellow of the Society for Empirical Legal Studies ([Albert Hyun Yoon CV](https://www.russellsage.org/sites/default/files/2024-03/ahy_cv_feb2023.pdf)). In 2023 he was the George Francis Brownell Visiting Professor of Law at [Harvard Law School](/universities/harvard-university/) ([University of Toronto Faculty of Law](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/role-position-person/full-time-faculty)). His work has appeared in the *Chicago*, *Stanford* and *Virginia* law reviews and has been cited in the *New York Times*, the *Wall Street Journal* and the judiciary ([Pierre Elliott Trudeau Foundation](https://www.trudeaufoundation.ca/directory/albert-yoon/)).
