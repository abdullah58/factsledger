## The clerk who became a judge, and the judge he learned from

Before [Amir Ali](/people/amir-ali/) became the Washington federal judge whose 2026 rulings made headlines — including the September decision blocking Homeland Security grant conditions — he spent 2012 to 2013 clerking on the Supreme Court of Canada for Justice Marshall Rothstein ([White House archives](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2024/01/10/president-biden-names-forty-fourth-round-of-judicial-nominees/)). That clerkship is a small footnote in Rothstein's 60-year career — as the University of Winnipeg put it, a career that "left an indelible mark on Canadian law" ([University of Winnipeg](https://news.uwinnipeg.ca/elders-community-advocate-former-supreme-court-justice-to-receive-honorary-doctorates/)). But it is one of the few places where two of this site's profiles genuinely intersect: a Canadian Supreme Court justice and the American judge he trained.

## Winnipeg lawyer

Marshall Rothstein was born on December 25, 1940, in Winnipeg, Manitoba, where he went to school before the University of Manitoba, earning a B.Com. in 1962 and an LL.B. in 1966 ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://www.scc-csc.ca/about-apropos/judges-juges/list-liste/marshall-rothstein/)). Called to the Manitoba Bar in 1966, he started at Thorvaldson, Eggertson, Saunders and Mauro, then moved in 1969 to Aikins, MacAulay & Thorvaldson, where he was a partner from 1972 to 1992 ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://www.scc-csc.ca/about-apropos/judges-juges/list-liste/marshall-rothstein/)). He became a leading counsel in transportation and competition law — "a leading expert in regulatory and competition law," as Winnipeg later summarized his counsel career in air and rail transportation cases ([University of Winnipeg](https://news.uwinnipeg.ca/elders-community-advocate-former-supreme-court-justice-to-receive-honorary-doctorates/)) — and was appointed Queen's Counsel in 1979 ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://www.scc-csc.ca/about-apropos/judges-juges/list-liste/marshall-rothstein/)).

Alongside practice, Rothstein spent much of his working life on human rights and in the classroom. He served as an adjudicator under Manitoba's Human Rights Act from 1978 to 1983 and as a member of the Canadian Human Rights Tribunal from 1986 to 1992; he taught transportation law in the University of Manitoba's Faculty of Law from 1970 to 1983 and again from 1988 to 1992, contract law in the Extension Department, and the Bar Admission Course for the Law Society of Manitoba ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://www.scc-csc.ca/about-apropos/judges-juges/list-liste/marshall-rothstein/)).

## Up the bench

Rothstein was appointed to the Trial Division of the Federal Court of Canada on June 24, 1992, serving also ex officio on the Appeal Division, the Court Martial Appeal Court of Canada, and the Competition Tribunal, and was elevated to the Federal Court of Appeal on January 21, 1999 ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://www.scc-csc.ca/about-apropos/judges-juges/list-liste/marshall-rothstein/)). Then, in 2006, came the appointment that made him a permanent part of Canadian legal history.

Newly elected Prime Minister Stephen Harper named Rothstein to the Supreme Court of Canada, and — in what the Toronto Star's Tonda MacCharles later called an event where Rothstein "made Canadian legal history as the first ever to face a public grilling by parliamentary committee" — the nominee appeared before an all-party parliamentary committee in a televised hearing ([Administrative Law Matters](https://www.administrativelawmatters.com/media/marshall-rothstein-muses-on-nine-years-in-canadas-supreme-court/)). It was a precedent-setting break with Canada's closed appointment tradition, and it terrified him: rookie prime minister Harper's advice was "Just don't screw up," and Rothstein later admitted, "It was very scary" ([Administrative Law Matters](https://www.administrativelawmatters.com/media/marshall-rothstein-muses-on-nine-years-in-canadas-supreme-court/)). He was, in the end, a safe pick — an administrative and transportation law expert who had been shortlisted by a committee before the election, after a search conducted under the previous Liberal government ([Administrative Law Matters](https://www.administrativelawmatters.com/media/marshall-rothstein-muses-on-nine-years-in-canadas-supreme-court/)).

He served on the country's highest court from March 1, 2006 until August 31, 2015 ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://www.scc-csc.ca/about-apropos/judges-juges/list-liste/marshall-rothstein/)). Among his notable work there was the majority judgment in the 2012 broadcasting regulatory reference — *In Reference re Broadcasting Regulatory Policy CRTC 2010-167 and Broadcasting Order CRTC 2010-168*, 2012 SCC 68 — which legal scholars still use as a teaching text on the boundaries of the broadcast regulator's authority ([UBC Allard School of Law](https://commlaw.allard.ubc.ca/2019/02/04/special-guest-on-tuesday-february-5-2019-the-honourable-marshall-rothstein-c-c-q-c/)). His clerks in those years included the future U.S. federal judge: Amir Ali clerked for him from 2012 to 2013 ([White House archives](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2024/01/10/president-biden-names-forty-fourth-round-of-judicial-nominees/)).

## The Order of Canada and what came after

On June 30, 2017, Governor General David Johnston announced that Rothstein had been appointed a Companion of the Order of Canada — one of only three Companions in a list of 99 new appointments — for his "eminent service as a jurist, notably on the Supreme Court of Canada, and for his dedication to legal education" ([Osler](https://www.osler.com/en/about-us/media-centre/osler-s-marshall-rothstein-appointed-companion-of/)). After retirement he joined Osler Hoskin & Harcourt's partnership, based in the firm's Vancouver office, advising on complex commercial disputes across tax, competition, intellectual property, transportation, and energy ([Osler](https://www.osler.com/en/about-us/media-centre/osler-s-marshall-rothstein-appointed-companion-of/)). The University of Winnipeg — his own school — later bestowed an Honorary Doctor of Laws on him at its 128th Convocation, for "outstanding contributions to the development of Canadian jurisprudence," noting that he is one of only two people born in Manitoba to ever serve on the Supreme Court ([University of Winnipeg](https://news.uwinnipeg.ca/elders-community-advocate-former-supreme-court-justice-to-receive-honorary-doctorates/)). Today he continues as a partner at Osler and as an arbitrator and mediator ([University of Winnipeg](https://news.uwinnipeg.ca/elders-community-advocate-former-supreme-court-justice-to-receive-honorary-doctorates/)).

He is married to Sheila Dorfman, a Montreal native; they have four children — Ronald, Douglas, Tracey, and Robert — and six grandchildren ([Supreme Court of Canada](https://www.scc-csc.ca/about-apropos/judges-juges/list-liste/marshall-rothstein/)).
