## Overview

The most revealing archive document on Vladimir Putin the intelligence officer is not Russian at all. In December 2018, researchers working through the last unprocessed share of the Dresden Stasi archives found an East German secret-police identity pass issued to Putin during his KGB posting in the city (1985–1990). The Stasi Records Agency (BStU) stressed that "current research gives no indication" he ever worked for the East German secret police: under a 1978 KGB–Stasi cooperation protocol, KGB liaison officers were issued such passes to enter Stasi offices, and Putin — one of about 30 KGB liaison officers in East Germany — used his to discuss operational matters with his Stasi colleagues, the historian Douglas Selvage writes after reviewing the archival record ([Wilson Center](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/vladimir-putins-stasi-id-press-sensation-and-its-historical-reality)). The record corrects the spy-story glamour: he was a mid-level KGB officer in East Germany's third city, discussing potential recruitment targets among the local population, foreign students and visitors to Dresden, and meeting agents not in Stasi buildings but in KGB "conspiratorial apartments" ([Wilson Center](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/blog-post/vladimir-putins-stasi-id-press-sensation-and-its-historical-reality)).

That man has led Russia as president or prime minister since 1999 — president from 2000 to 2008 and again since 2012 ([Russian Government](http://archive.premier.gov.ru/eng/premier/biography.html); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)). A former KGB foreign-intelligence officer, he ordered the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 and won a fifth presidential term in March 2024 with 87.28% of the vote — an official result Western governments called neither free nor fair ([Reuters](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/putin-wins-russian-presidential-election-with-8797-of-the-vote-first-official-results-show)).

## From the KGB to the Kremlin

Putin was born to a factory-worker family and grew up in a communal apartment shared by three families ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)). He graduated from the Law Department of Leningrad State University in 1975 and immediately joined the KGB's First Chief Directorate for foreign intelligence ([Russian Government](http://archive.premier.gov.ru/eng/premier/biography.html); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)). He served 15 years as a KGB foreign-intelligence officer, including six years in Dresden, East Germany (1985–1990), and retired in 1990 with the rank of lieutenant colonel ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin)).

Archival detail on the Dresden years has come from Germany, not Russia. During the posting both of his daughters were born, and he was promoted to lieutenant colonel there ([RNZ/BBC](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/world/378123/putin-s-stasi-spy-id-pass-found-in-germany)). In 1989 the GDR awarded him a bronze medal "for faithful service to the National People's Army," according to the Kremlin website ([RNZ/BBC](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/world/378123/putin-s-stasi-spy-id-pass-found-in-germany)). Putin himself has said he personally calmed a crowd that surrounded the KGB building in Dresden in December 1989, telling them it was Soviet territory, and in a June 2017 Russian state-television interview he described his KGB work as "illegal intelligence-gathering" ([RNZ/BBC](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/world/378123/putin-s-stasi-spy-id-pass-found-in-germany)).

His university tutor was Anatoly Sobchak, later the first democratically elected mayor of St. Petersburg; Putin became Sobchak's adviser, then first deputy mayor of the city by 1994 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin)). He moved to Moscow in 1996 into the presidential administration, and Yeltsin made him FSB director in July 1998 — later Secretary of the Security Council ([Russian Government](http://archive.premier.gov.ru/eng/premier/biography.html); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin)). Yeltsin appointed him prime minister on 9 August 1999, and his popularity rose on the back of the military campaign against Chechen separatists ([Russian Government](http://archive.premier.gov.ru/eng/premier/biography.html); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)). Yeltsin resigned on 31 December 1999 and Putin became acting president the same day, granting Yeltsin immunity from prosecution ([Russian Government](http://archive.premier.gov.ru/eng/premier/biography.html); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)).

## A quarter-century at the top

Putin was elected president on 26 March 2000 with about 53% of the vote and inaugurated on 7 May, then re-elected on 14 March 2004 ([Russian Government](http://archive.premier.gov.ru/eng/premier/biography.html); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)). Early in his presidency he divided Russia's 89 regions into seven federal districts, curbed the regional governors' power, and moved against oligarchs and independent media ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin)). Term limits forced him out in 2008; Dmitry Medvedev became president and Putin served as prime minister from 2008 to 2012, widely seen as retaining real power ([Russian Government](http://archive.premier.gov.ru/eng/premier/biography.html); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin)).

He returned to the presidency with the 4 March 2012 election, winning just under 65% amid fraud complaints and street protests ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin)). In January 2020 he proposed constitutional changes; the referendum held 25 June–1 July returned 77.9% yes on 65% turnout, resetting his term count to zero and opening the path to rule until 2036 — critics alleged fraud ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2020/7/2/russians-clear-way-for-putin-to-extend-his-rule-until-2036)). He signed the amendments into law on 5 April 2021 ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)). He then won a fifth term in March 2024 with 87.28% on a claimed 77.5% turnout; the US, UK, Germany and EU called the vote neither free nor fair, and Reuters noted the result positioned him as Russia's longest-serving leader since Stalin ([Reuters](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/putin-wins-russian-presidential-election-with-8797-of-the-vote-first-official-results-show)).

## War in Ukraine and confrontation with the West

After Ukraine's pro-Russian president fled in 2014, Putin sent troops into Crimea in March 2014 and annexed it; separatist uprisings followed in Donetsk and Luhansk ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vladimir-Putin)). On 24 February 2022 he ordered a full-scale invasion of Ukraine, calling it a "special military operation" ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)). On 30 September 2022 he announced the annexation of Luhansk, Donetsk, Kherson and Zaporizhzhia ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)). The International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Putin on 17 March 2023 over the alleged deportation of Ukrainian children to Russia ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)). The war drew extensive Western sanctions, and by 2024–2026 it had become Europe's largest land war since 1945 with no settlement ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts); [Associated Press](https://www.military.com/daily-news/2025/08/15/trump-and-putin-meet-alaska-high-stakes-summit-russia-ukraine-war.html)).

## The 2025–2026 diplomacy track

Putin met Donald Trump in Anchorage, Alaska, on 15 August 2025 for about two and a half hours; Putin claimed an "understanding" was reached while Trump said no deal was done — "there's no deal until there's a deal" — and the two took no press questions ([Associated Press](https://www.military.com/daily-news/2025/08/15/trump-and-putin-meet-alaska-high-stakes-summit-russia-ukraine-war.html)). On 3 September 2025 he appeared publicly alongside China's Xi Jinping and North Korea's Kim Jong Un at a Beijing military parade ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/europe/vladimir-putin-fast-facts)).

He received US special envoy Steve Witkoff and [Jared Kushner](/people/jared-kushner/) at the Kremlin — press reporting dates the meeting to 5 September 2026, with [Yuri Ushakov](/people/yuri-ushakov/) and Kirill Dmitriev present — and the Kremlin's own record quotes Putin saying he had ordered a halt to strikes on Kyiv from midnight 5 September, citing reductions in Ukrainian strikes, thanking the envoys for helping secure the release of former US Marine Robert Gilman, and expressing trust in the mediation channel ([Kremlin](https://en.kremlin.ru/events/president/news/80699)). The New York Times (via NV.ua) reported that Kushner challenged Putin's claim that Russia could take all of Donbas within months — "With all due respect, you told us the same thing a year ago" — and that Putin demanded Ukraine withdraw from Donbas territory it still holds; the envoys travelled to Kyiv the next day, and Trump and Putin spoke on 8 September ([NV.ua](https://english.nv.ua/russian-war/kushner-challenged-putin-s-claim-russia-could-seize-donbas-within-months-nyt-50642492.html)). No agreement was announced; both the Kremlin record and the press reporting describe discussion only.

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not claim the Stasi ID pass meant Putin worked for the East German secret police; the Stasi Records Agency says current research gives no indication he did — the pass gave KGB liaison officers access to Stasi offices.
- It does not claim a net worth figure for Putin; published estimates vary wildly and none rest on auditable evidence that could be verified.
- It does not claim the September 2026 Moscow talks produced any agreement or ceasefire; both the Kremlin record and press reporting describe discussion only, with no announced outcome.
- It does not claim the exact duration of the September 2026 Kremlin meeting; the Kremlin's primary transcript does not state one.
- It does not claim the 2020 referendum or any Russian election result as independently verified; the figures given are official results, and their fairness is contested by the US, the EU, and Russian opposition figures.
- It does not claim details about Putin's current private or family life beyond the two daughters named in official biographies; anything further is unverified rumour.
- It does not pin down the precise legal date of the 2013 divorce; CNN's own pages list both 2013 and 2014, so the profile says the divorce was announced in 2013.
