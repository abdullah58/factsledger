## Overview

Norman Foster is an English architect, founder and chairman of Foster + Partners, and the 1999 laureate of the Pritzker Architecture Prize, who also serves as patron of the humanitarian architecture charity [Article 25](/organizations/article-25/) ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999); [Article 25](https://www.article-25.org/our-team)). Born in Manchester in 1935, he trained at the University of Manchester and Yale University, founded his own London practice in 1967, and built a career in steel and glass that runs from the Sainsbury Centre in Norwich to the dome of the rebuilt Reichstag in Berlin and the roof of the British Museum's Great Court in London ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)).

Foster's importance is not only the size of his practice — by the time of the Pritzker citation it had project offices in more than twenty countries and had received more than 400 awards and citations — but the consistency of his method across five decades ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999)). His buildings repeatedly solve the same problem in new forms: how to make a large, technologically advanced structure that admits natural light, opens its plans, and treats the people who use it as the point of the design rather than an afterthought. The Pritzker jury, quoted by the prize's own announcement, chose "an architect who cares passionately about the future of this planet, an avowed optimist with a firm belief in technological progress, but who also believes that architecture is about people and the quality of life" ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999)). That sentence is the best short summary of a career that spans airports, parliaments, museums, bridges and offices in the [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/) and far beyond it.

## From Manchester and Yale to Foster Associates

Foster was born in Manchester in 1935, and Britannica gives his birth date as 1 June 1935 ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)). The Princess of Asturias Foundation's laureate biography adds a detail the shorter accounts omit: at 21 he began his architecture studies, which he financed by taking different jobs ([Fundación Princesa de Asturias](https://www.fpa.es/en/princess-of-asturias-awards/laureates/2009-norman-foster/?texto=trayectoria)). After graduating from Manchester University School of Architecture and City Planning in 1961, he won a Henry Fellowship to Yale University, where he gained a Master's Degree in Architecture ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999)). Britannica dates the training as Manchester 1956–61 and Yale 1961–62 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)).

Beginning in 1963 he worked in partnership with Richard and Su Rogers and his wife, Wendy Foster, in a firm called Team 4 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)). In 1967 he established his own firm, Foster Associates, in London with Wendy Foster; the firm was renamed Foster and Partners in 1999 ([International Academy of Architecture](https://iaa-ngo.com/portfolio-posts/norman-foster/); [Fundación Princesa de Asturias](https://www.fpa.es/en/princess-of-asturias-awards/laureates/2009-norman-foster/?texto=trayectoria)). The Pritzker page describes him as founder and chairman of a practice founded in London in 1967 that became a worldwide practice, with project offices in more than twenty countries and a range of work running from urban master plans and airports to civic and cultural buildings, offices, private houses and product design ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999)). He also chairs the practice's Design Board, which provides its strategic design direction ([International Academy of Architecture](https://iaa-ngo.com/portfolio-posts/norman-foster/)).

## High-tech design: the shed, light and open plans

Foster's earliest works explored the idea of a technologically advanced "shed", meaning a structure surrounded by a lightweight shell or envelope ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)). His first buildings to receive international acclaim were the Sainsbury Centre for the Visual Arts (1978) in Norwich, a vast airy glass-and-metal-panelled shed, and the Hong Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation headquarters (1986) in Hong Kong, a futuristic steel-and-glass office building with a stepped profile ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)). In the Hong Kong building he moved elements such as elevators to the exterior, where they could be easily serviced, and thus created open plans in the centre of the spaces ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)). That move — servicing on the outside, people and light on the inside — became a signature.

Balancing that high-tech character, many of Foster's buildings used green spaces, or mini-atria, designed to admit a maximum amount of natural light into offices, as in the Commerzbank Tower (1997) in Frankfurt ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)). The Pritzker citation gives the human version of the same idea: one of his first notable projects, the Willis Faber and Dumas offices in Ipswich, included a swimming pool and a grassy rooftop park for employees, pioneering the notion that the workplace could be a pleasant environment ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999)). The same citation lists, among the practice's defining projects, the New German Parliament in the Reichstag in Berlin, Chek Lap Kok International Airport and the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank in Hong Kong, the Commerzbank Headquarters in Frankfurt, the Willis Faber & Dumas Head Office in Ipswich, and the Sainsbury Centre for Visual Arts in Norwich ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999)).

At the turn of the century Foster extended those ideas to world landmarks. He rebuilt the Reichstag (1999) in Berlin after German reunification, adding a new steel-and-glass dome that surrounds a spiral observation platform, and he encased the court of the British Museum (2000) in London under a steel-and-glass roof, creating an enclosed urban square inside the museum building ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)). Later landmarks include the headquarters for Swiss Re, later 30 St Mary Axe and nicknamed "the Gherkin" (2004), the courtyard enclosure for the Smithsonian Institution's Patent Office Building (2007) in Washington, D.C., Terminal 3 of Beijing Capital International Airport (2008), and Apple Park (2018), the headquarters of Apple in Cupertino, California ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)). The Princess of Asturias biography adds the Millau Viaduct in France, which it describes as the world's highest bridge, standing 243 metres above the River Tarn and inaugurated in December 2004, and the Bilbao Metro system, inaugurated in 1995, with the Collserola communications tower in Barcelona built for the 1992 Olympic Games ([Fundación Princesa de Asturias](https://www.fpa.es/en/princess-of-asturias-awards/laureates/2009-norman-foster/?texto=trayectoria)). A useful stat-plus-source-plus-implication follows from the Pritzker record alone: a practice that had already collected more than 400 awards by 1999 went on to add the Gherkin, Beijing Terminal 3 and Apple Park, which suggests that the Pritzker recognised a method still in mid-career, not a completed body of work ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999)).

## Patronage, foundation and later work

Foster was named the 1999 laureate of the Pritzker Architecture Prize, with the formal presentation made at a ceremony in Berlin on 7 June 1999, when he received a $100,000 grant and a bronze medallion ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999)). He was the second Englishman to become a Pritzker laureate, after Sir James Stirling, honoured in 1981, who had encouraged a young Foster as he began his career in the early sixties ([The Pritzker Architecture Prize](https://www.pritzkerprize.com/laureates/1999)). He had been knighted in 1990, was appointed to the Order of Merit in 1997, and was granted a life peerage in 1999, taking the title Lord Foster of Thames Bank ([International Academy of Architecture](https://iaa-ngo.com/portfolio-posts/norman-foster/); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Lord-Norman-Foster)). Later honours include the Praemium Imperiale prize for architecture in 2002, the Stirling Prize in 2004, and the Prince of Asturias Award for the Arts in 2009 ([Fundación Princesa de Asturias](https://www.fpa.es/en/princess-of-asturias-awards/laureates/2009-norman-foster/?texto=trayectoria)).

Beyond the practice, Foster is president of the Norman Foster Foundation, based in Madrid with a global reach, promoting interdisciplinary thinking and research to help new generations of architects, designers and urbanists anticipate the future ([International Academy of Architecture](https://iaa-ngo.com/portfolio-posts/norman-foster/)). His connection to Article 25 is presented by the charity itself: its board page lists him as Patron, alongside its description of him as Founder and Chairman of Foster + Partners ([Article 25](https://www.article-25.org/our-team)). The charity's own account also repeats the Pritzker honour, the knighthood and the life peerage, which makes the patronage legible in context: a globally honoured architect lending his name to a charity that designs schools, hospitals and housing where they are most needed ([Article 25](https://www.article-25.org/our-team)). What the sources read do not describe is any specific Article 25 project Foster advised on or funded, and this profile does not invent one.
