## Overview

Bill Shine is the television executive who ran Fox News's programming arm for most of the network's first two decades, became its co-president in the wake of the Roger Ailes scandal, and then served as President Donald Trump's White House communications chief from July 2018 to March 2019. That is where most reference accounts of Shine end. What they miss is his quiet return to cable news: in June 2020, Nexstar hired him as a consultant to its startup news channel NewsNation, and the staff did not learn of his involvement until an industry blog surfaced it in February 2021 - setting off a staff exodus and a public reckoning over the channel's "unbiased" branding ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/former-fox-news-co-president-white-house-comms-director-bill-shine-joins-newsnation-as-consultant/); [Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/nexstar-medias-newsnation-sees-3-top-editorial-execs-resign-including-vp-of-news/)).

Shine spent 21 years at Fox News, from the network's October 1996 launch to his forced exit on May 1, 2017. He arrived as a producer for Hannity & Colmes and climbed to co-president, running all programming and news functions while co-president Jack Abernethy handled the business side ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/bill-shine-named-assistant-to-president-trump-and-deputy-chief-of-staff/); [Cablefax](https://www.cablefax.com/campaigns/william-shine)). He was never personally accused of harassment, but he was named in lawsuits as someone who knew about misconduct and failed to act ([NPR](https://www.wcbe.org/2018-07-05/white-house-taps-former-fox-news-executive-for-senior-communications-job)). In July 2018 Trump brought him into the White House as deputy chief of staff for communications; eight months later he was gone, moved to a senior advisory role on the 2020 re-election campaign ([NPR](https://www.wcbe.org/2019-03-08/bill-shine-resigns-as-white-house-communications-director)).

## The NewsNation chapter no reference profile covers

NewsNation - the Chicago-based cable news operation Nexstar built on WGN America after its $4.1 billion acquisition of Tribune - hired Shine in June 2020, before the channel's official September 2020 launch, to help oversee talk shows across the network ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/morning-media-newsfeed/former-fox-news-co-president-white-house-comms-director-bill-shine-joins-newsnation-as-consultant-2/)). The hiring stayed under the radar until the industry blog FTVLive reported it on a Wednesday morning in February 2021 ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/former-fox-news-co-president-white-house-comms-director-bill-shine-joins-newsnation-as-consultant/)).

What followed is documented by Adweek, relaying the New York Times' reporting: staffers raised concerns about editorial direction and Shine's involvement, and several top editors quit in response ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/nexstar-medias-newsnation-sees-3-top-editorial-execs-resign-including-vp-of-news/)). News director Sandy Pudar and managing editor Richard Maginn stepped down, and days later VP of news Jennifer Lyons - who had joined from Chicago's WGN-TV to spearhead the NewsNation launch - resigned, as first reported by Chicago media columnist Robert Feder. In a statement, Nexstar said Lyons had "decided to leave her position," thanked her for her efforts, and put executive vice president Blake Russell in interim charge of the channel ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/nexstar-medias-newsnation-sees-3-top-editorial-execs-resign-including-vp-of-news/)).

The reaction from the top showed how much was at stake. At a staff meeting in Chicago, Nexstar CEO Perry Sook sought to reassure employees of his commitment to the channel's vision: "Despite reports to the contrary that you may read, we're committed to the vision of unbiased reporting," he said, according to a recording obtained by the Times. "But obviously along the way there will be growing pains" ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/nexstar-medias-newsnation-sees-3-top-editorial-execs-resign-including-vp-of-news/)).

The anxiety inside the newsroom was not hard to understand. NewsNation had pitched itself as an "unbiased alternative" to the other cable news channels, and Mediaite reported that insiders had reservations that Shine - the defining programming mind of Fox News's prime-time era - signaled a sharp move to the right ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/former-fox-news-co-president-white-house-comms-director-bill-shine-joins-newsnation-as-consultant/)). Adweek noted at the time that it was unknown how significant Shine's role would be, and that despite the infrastructure - 115 local newsrooms across 197 stations, 5,400 journalists, more than 75 million homes - the ratings had not arrived ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/nexstar-medias-newsnation-sees-3-top-editorial-execs-resign-including-vp-of-news/)).

## Twenty years at Fox News

Shine's Fox News run lasted 21 years, from the network's inception in October 1996 to his departure in May 2017 ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/bill-shine-has-resigned-from-the-trump-administration/)). He started as producer of Hannity & Colmes and worked his way up through executive producer, vice president of production, and executive vice president of programming before being named co-president in the summer of 2016, the month after Roger Ailes resigned - a tenure at the top of only about seven months ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/bill-shine-named-assistant-to-president-trump-and-deputy-chief-of-staff/)).

The division of labor at the top was clean. Shine managed all programming and news functions - production, technical operations, and talent management - while co-president Jack Abernethy oversaw finance, ad sales, and HR ([Cablefax](https://www.cablefax.com/campaigns/william-shine)). Cablefax, profiling Shine as a 2017 industry honoree, credited the transition period with the channel "not skipping a beat": Fox News held the #1 cable news spot for 182 consecutive months across total day and prime time, and Fox Business News topped CNBC in business-day viewers in back-to-back quarters ([Cablefax](https://www.cablefax.com/campaigns/william-shine)). The same profile recorded the small personal detail that Shine's stated education was SUNY Oswego and that, when asked how he celebrates his birthday, he replied: "My birthday is the 4th of July so there's usually fireworks involved" ([Cablefax](https://www.cablefax.com/campaigns/william-shine)).

The Ailes scandal ended his run. The [Associated Press](https://factsledger.com/organizations/associated-press/) later described Shine as "Ailes' operations man and enforcer, the one who tried to put his boss' directives into action," and noted he was named in lawsuits as someone who tried to keep a lid on allegations of bad workplace behavior instead of trying to root it out ([Associated Press](https://www.anews.com.tr/world/2019/03/08/bill-shine-resigns-from-white-house-communications-post)). Shine denied wrongdoing. His exit carried an $8.4 million severance, according to his financial disclosure form, with the severance agreement expiring May 1, 2019 ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/bill-shine-has-resigned-from-the-trump-administration/)).

His Fox exit had an unusual public defender. When reports surfaced that Shine was about to be fired, longtime friend Sean Hannity - whose top producer Shine had been for years - tweeted: "Somebody HIGH UP AND INSIDE FNC is trying to get an innocent person fired. i pray this is NOT true because if it is, that's the total end of the FNC as we know it" ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/bill-shine-named-assistant-to-president-trump-and-deputy-chief-of-staff/)). Fourteen months later, Hannity's was the No. 1 show on cable news in total viewers and the 25-54 demographic ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/bill-shine-named-assistant-to-president-trump-and-deputy-chief-of-staff/)).

## Eight months in the White House

On July 5, 2018, the White House announced that Shine would serve as Assistant to the President and Deputy Chief of Staff for Communications, filling a communications-director vacancy that had stood since [Hope Hicks](https://factsledger.com/people/hope-hicks/) left in March. He was the fifth person to take on the communications-chief role under Trump, following Hicks, Anthony Scaramucci (who lasted 11 days), Mike Dubke, and Sean Spicer ([NPR](https://www.wcbe.org/2018-07-05/white-house-taps-former-fox-news-executive-for-senior-communications-job); [Associated Press](https://www.anews.com.tr/world/2019/03/08/bill-shine-resigns-from-white-house-communications-post)). The controversy followed him in: NPR noted the pick invited controversy "given the cloud surrounding his exit from Fox News," and that Shine was known to be close to Hannity, by then both Fox's biggest star and a Trump booster ([NPR](https://www.wcbe.org/2018-07-05/white-house-taps-former-fox-news-executive-for-senior-communications-job)).

Shine's White House stint produced one of the defining press-freedom episodes of 2018. On July 25, during an Oval Office photo-op, CNN reporter Kaitlan Collins - serving as the pool reporter for all five television networks - asked four questions: "Did Michael Cohen betray you, Mr. President?" "Mr. President, are you worried about what Michael Cohen is about to say to the prosecutors?" "Are you worried about what is on the other tapes, Mr. President?" and "Why is Vladimir Putin not accepting your invitation, Mr. President?" Trump ignored all four ([NPR](https://www.wcbe.org/2018-07-26/cnn-reporter-white-house-called-my-questions-inappropriate-banned-me-from-event?_amp=true)).

Shortly afterward, Collins said she was called into Shine's office, where Shine and press secretary Sarah Sanders told her she was disinvited from that afternoon's open press event in the Rose Garden because they thought her questions were "inappropriate" ([NPR](https://www.wcbe.org/2018-07-26/cnn-reporter-white-house-called-my-questions-inappropriate-banned-me-from-event?_amp=true)). Sanders later said in a statement that Collins had shouted questions and refused to leave the Oval Office despite repeated requests - but made clear any other CNN journalist could attend. White House Correspondents' Association president Olivier Knox condemned the decision as "wholly inappropriate, wrong-headed, and weak," saying "it cannot stand." Even Fox News weighed in: Fox News president Jay Wallace issued a statement saying the network stood "in strong solidarity with CNN for the right to full access for our journalists as part of a free and unfettered press" ([NPR](https://www.wcbe.org/2018-07-26/cnn-reporter-white-house-called-my-questions-inappropriate-banned-me-from-event?_amp=true)).

Behind the scenes, Shine's standing was eroding. The New York Times reported in January 2019 that Shine had come to see his role as confined to "stagecraft" and that Trump had "openly expressed skepticism about what he has done" ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/tvnewser/bill-shine-has-resigned-from-the-trump-administration/)). On March 8, 2019, the White House announced his resignation; he had offered it Thursday night, and Trump accepted. Shine moved to a senior adviser role on the 2020 re-election campaign. Trump said in the statement: "Bill Shine has done an outstanding job working for me and the Administration. We will miss him in the White House, but look forward to working together on the 2020 Presidential Campaign, where he will be totally involved." Shine called the stint "the most rewarding experience of my entire life" ([NPR](https://www.wcbe.org/2019-03-08/bill-shine-resigns-as-white-house-communications-director)).

The Associated Press, drawing on two people close to the president, reported that Trump had grown frustrated that Shine had not done more to improve his press coverage - and that Shine, like others before him, had struggled with a president who preferred to run his own communications strategy via tweet. The announcement surprised much of the West Wing; one sign of the drift was that Shine had not joined Trump on the high-stakes Vietnam summit with Kim Jong Un. Two days before the resignation, the Democratic National Committee had announced it would bar Fox News from hosting one of its sanctioned 2020 primary debates, citing a New Yorker article on the network's closeness to the White House ([Associated Press](https://www.anews.com.tr/world/2019/03/08/bill-shine-resigns-from-white-house-communications-post)).

## What this profile does not claim

Shine's birth year and early upbringing - widely given elsewhere as July 4, 1963, in Farmingville, New York, raised on Long Island - could not be confirmed in any source read in full for this profile; the July 4 date itself comes from Shine's own answer in a Cablefax Q&A. This profile does not assert the roughly $15 million Fox exit-compensation figure found in some accounts; it states only the $8.4 million severance recorded in his financial disclosure. Fully-read sources disagree by a month on when Shine was named co-president (August versus September 2016), so this profile does not pin it tighter than the summer of 2016. It does not claim Shine is still involved with NewsNation or describe any role he has held since 2021, because no source read in full covers that period. And it does not assign motives to his NewsNation hiring or to the editors' resignations beyond what the reporting documents.
