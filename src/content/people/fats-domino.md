## Overview

Antoine Dominique "Fats" Domino Jr. was a New Orleans pianist, singer and songwriter whose
boogie-woogie piano and warm, relaxed voice made him one of the founding figures of rock 'n' roll
([City of New Orleans](https://cityofno.granicus.com/MetaViewer.php?view_id=7&clip_id=3877&meta_id=544249)).
Born in New Orleans on February 26, 1928, the child of Haitian Creole plantation workers and the
grandson of a slave, he worked days at a mattress factory while playing piano at night, and became
one of the best-selling Black artists of the 1950s — a reported 59 R&B and 63 pop chart entries
between 1950 and 1963, outselling Little Richard, Chuck Berry and Buddy Holly combined
([NPR](https://www.kwit.org/2017-10-25/fats-domino-founding-father-of-rock-n-roll-dies-at-89)).
His easygoing two-fisted piano triplets became a building block of New Orleans R&B, and his
biggest hit, "Blueberry Hill," remains the record most associated with him
([NPR](https://www.kwit.org/2017-10-25/fats-domino-founding-father-of-rock-n-roll-dies-at-89)).

## The architect of the New Orleans sound

Domino's career started almost by accident: in the summer of 1947, bandleader Billy Diamond heard
him play at a backyard barbecue and invited him to join his band, the Solid Senders
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/fats-domino-rock-pioneer-blueberry-hill-singer-dies-89/)).
Diamond is said to have nicknamed him "Fats" after the pianists Fats Waller and Fats Pichon, and
Imperial Records signed him right off the bandstand
([NPR](https://www.kwit.org/2017-10-25/fats-domino-founding-father-of-rock-n-roll-dies-at-89)).
His first release for Imperial, "The Fat Man" (1949), began a partnership with producer and
arranger Dave Bartholomew that produced the body of work — "Ain't That a Shame," "I'm Walkin',"
"Blue Monday," "Walking to New Orleans" — that carried New Orleans to the vanguard of popular
music, recorded with session men like saxophonists Herbert Hardesty and Alvin "Red" Tyler and
drummer Earl Palmer
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/fats-domino-rock-pioneer-blueberry-hill-singer-dies-89/)).

Domino toured this music through Jim Crow America. His audiences were segregated, sometimes with
only a rope dividing Black and white fans, and the concerts could be violent — bottle-throwing, tear
gas, stabbings and arrests. Driving the band's bus in South Carolina, Hardesty once got directions
from the Ku Klux Klan by the light of a burning cross; yet by 1960, Domino's audience was
overwhelmingly white
([NPR](https://www.kwit.org/2017-10-25/fats-domino-founding-father-of-rock-n-roll-dies-at-89)).
He also played the venues his own community built: a surviving 1960 poster advertises a Fats
Domino appearance at [Lincoln Beach](https://factsledger.com/places/lincoln-beach-amusement-park),
the Black amusement park of segregated New Orleans
([Old New Orleans](http://old-new-orleans.com/NO_LincolnBeach.html)).

## Honors, Katrina, and the Lower Ninth Ward

Domino was among the first musicians inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1986, received
the [Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award](https://factsledger.com/organizations/grammy-awards) in
1987, and was awarded the National Medal of Arts by President Bill Clinton in 1998
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/fats-domino-rock-pioneer-blueberry-hill-singer-dies-89/)).
After a final three-week European tour in 1995, he stopped leaving New Orleans
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/fats-domino-rock-pioneer-blueberry-hill-singer-dies-89/)).

In 2005 he refused to evacuate ahead of Hurricane Katrina, telling his agent Al Embry he planned to
stay; his agent and his niece reported him missing on September 1, until his daughter Karen Domino
White recognized him in a Times-Picayune photograph of a rescue boat
([Pollstar](https://news.pollstar.com/2005/09/02/fats-domino-rescued-2/)).
He had ridden out the storm with his wife Rosemary and family on the second story of his flooded
Lower 9th Ward home, and was taken by the New Orleans Harbor Police to a Baton Rouge shelter
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/fats-domino-rock-pioneer-blueberry-hill-singer-dies-89/)).

Domino spent his final years in Harvey, Louisiana, and died there of natural causes on October 24,
2017, at age 89
([Associated Press](https://archive.blkalerts.com/2022/10/14/renowned-pianist-fats-domino-has-street-renamed-in-his-honor/)).
He never left his neighborhood: in 2021 the New Orleans City Council voted to rename the street he
lived on — Caffin Avenue, named for a former slaveowner — to Fats Domino Avenue, effective January
1, 2022, and a second-line led by the Stooges Brass Band celebrated the renaming in October 2022
([City of New Orleans](https://cityofno.granicus.com/MetaViewer.php?view_id=7&clip_id=3877&meta_id=544249);
[Associated Press](https://archive.blkalerts.com/2022/10/14/renowned-pianist-fats-domino-has-street-renamed-in-his-honor/)).

## What this profile does not claim

No claim is made about Domino's exact total record sales, because the sources disagree: NPR reported
more than 65 million records, while the Associated Press reported more than 110 million in both 2005
and 2022 — this profile states both figures with attribution. The sources also date "The Fat Man"
to 1949 (TheWrap, NPR) or 1950 (an AP-sourced 2005 piece); the profile follows the 1949 release
dating. This profile makes no claim about Domino's private life beyond what the obituaries document
(his wife Rosemary and eight children), and it does not repeat the widely circulated but here
unverified anecdotes (such as the Elvis "real king of rock 'n' roll" remark) without a
first-hand source. Nothing here concerns any other person or place sharing the name.
