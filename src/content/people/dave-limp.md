Dave Limp is the chief executive of [Blue Origin](/organizations/blue-origin/), the Kent, Washington-based rocket company founded by [Jeff Bezos](/people/jeff-bezos/) — a post he took on December 4, 2023, after nearly fourteen years running Amazon's devices and services division. On Tuesday, September 29, 2026, Limp was listed among the technology executives expected at Washington's Andrew W. Mellon Auditorium for the unveiling of America.gov, the White House's new AI-powered portal for federal services — a notable public appearance for an executive who built his reputation in the living room rather than in Washington. ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/news/white-house/4746534/watch-live-trump-launch-new-federal-government-ai-tool/); [GovCon Wire](https://www.govconwire.com/articles/amazon-exec-dave-limp-appointed-blue-origin-ceo))

America.gov was presented as a single AI-driven entry point to the federal government, replacing the need to navigate information scattered across individual agency websites. The unveiling event was set for 10 a.m. at the Mellon Auditorium, and the expected tech contingent placed Limp alongside Tesla CEO Elon Musk, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, and [NASA](/organizations/nasa/) Administrator Jared Isaacman. ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/news/white-house/4746534/watch-live-trump-launch-new-federal-government-ai-tool/)) For a rocket executive, a seat at the government's digital-modernization launch is unusual company — and it reflects how central Blue Origin has become to the administration's space ambitions.

## The Amazon years: Alexa, Echo, and fourteen years of gadgets

Limp joined [Amazon](/organizations/amazon/) in 2010 as senior vice president of Devices & Services, overseeing the company's Echo hardware line and its Alexa voice-assistant business, among other initiatives. He was a member of Amazon's senior leadership team — the internal "s-team" — and, before Amazon, had worked at Apple and Palm. ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2023/amazon-devices-chief-dave-limp-to-depart/))

On August 14, 2023, Amazon CEO [Andy Jassy](/people/andy-jassy/) announced to employees that Limp would step down later that year. Jassy's memo said that "after 13.5 years with Amazon and countless contributions to the company, Dave Limp has decided to retire from Amazon," crediting him with making Amazon "one of the world's leading innovators in building devices and underlying services that customers love. From Kindle readers to Fire TV to Alexa and Echo." Limp, then 56, published his own note alongside it. ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2023/amazon-devices-chief-dave-limp-to-depart/))

His memo is worth reading because it is unusually candid about what was not behind the decision: "It's not because of our team, this team is SO good and some of you I have worked with for decades. You're all masters of your craft," he wrote. "It's not because I am less bullish about the devices and services business... On top of all of that, we are living in the golden age of computer science." His stated reason was simpler: "I have been doing a version of this job (building and shipping consumer electronics) on and off for 30+ years. I love it, but I also want look into the future through a different lens. I am not sure what that future is right now, with the notable exception that it won't be in the consumer electronics space." ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2023/amazon-devices-chief-dave-limp-to-depart/))

The context around the exit was complicated: Amazon had conducted layoffs in its Devices & Services team the prior year as part of company-wide cuts, amid reports that the division was losing as much as $10 billion a year — even as the company disclosed that more than 500 million Alexa-enabled devices had been sold since the original Echo speaker debuted in late 2014. ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2023/amazon-devices-chief-dave-limp-to-depart/))

## Taking over Blue Origin

The "different lens" turned out to be rockets. In September 2023, CNBC obtained internal notes to Blue Origin staff written by Jeff Bezos and outgoing CEO Bob Smith: Limp would officially join the space company as CEO on December 4, succeeding Smith, who would stay through January 2 to manage the transition. In the note, Bezos called Limp "a proven innovator with a customer-first mindset and extensive experience leading and scaling large, complex organizations." Beyond the Amazon résumé, Limp's career included four years as a venture partner at Azure Capital Partners and senior leadership roles at Palm, Liberate Technologies, and Apple — and, at Amazon, leadership of the Alexa, Ring, and Echo businesses plus the Project Kuiper satellite-internet constellation and the Zoox autonomous-vehicle program. ([GovCon Wire](https://www.govconwire.com/articles/amazon-exec-dave-limp-appointed-blue-origin-ceo))

## The 2025 reset: first flight, then layoffs

Limp's first full year at Blue Origin paired a milestone with a gutting. In January 2025, the company's heavy-lift New Glenn rocket reached orbit on its maiden flight — although the booster stage was lost on its way back to Earth. Weeks later, in February, Limp announced the company would cut about 10% of its workforce, roughly 1,400 of its nearly 14,000 employees, with positions in engineering, R&D, and program and project management eliminated and layers of management thinned. ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/blue-origin-to-lay-off-10-of-workforce-read-memo-from-ceo-to-employees/))

His email to staff framed the cuts as the price of focus: "Our primary focus in 2025 and beyond is to scale our manufacturing output and launch cadence with speed, decisiveness, and efficiency for our customers," he wrote. "We grew and hired incredibly fast in the last few years, and with that growth came more bureaucracy and less focus than we needed." The growth had been dramatic — from fewer than 1,000 employees as recently as 2018, to 6,000 by 2022, 11,000 in 2023, and about 14,000 at the time of the cuts. At an all-hands meeting the same day, he told staff, "We just came to the painful conclusion that we aren't set up for the kind of success that we really wanted to have," adding that the company needed a culture that is "quick, nimble, decisive, and very focused on our customers," according to Reuters reporting quoted by GeekWire. ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/blue-origin-to-lay-off-10-of-workforce-read-memo-from-ceo-to-employees/))

The flight ledger kept moving: a second New Glenn launch in November 2025 put a pair of Mars-bound spacecraft into space and achieved Blue Origin's first booster landing on a drone ship. In April 2026 the company reflew that booster — but the upper stage failed and the customer payload, an AST SpaceMobile satellite, was lost. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/02/blue-origin-plans-to-launch-new-glenn-again-this-year-after-explosion/))

## 2026: explosion, rebuild, and the end of Bezos-only funding

On May 28, 2026, a New Glenn rocket exploded during a prelaunch engine test at Launch Complex 36 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, producing a huge fireball and widespread damage to the pad — the company's only one then capable of supporting New Glenn. Investigators blamed an anomaly at the main oxygen valve on one of the rocket's BE-4 engines. ([Florida Today](https://www.floridatoday.com/story/tech/science/space/2026/09/28/blue-origin-ceo-dave-limp-sees-path-to-return-to-launch-in-2026-after-new-glenn-rocket-explosion/91985125007/))

Limp's response was an aggressive return-to-flight timetable. In early June he said more of the pad's infrastructure was in better shape than expected, that another previously flown New Glenn booster and three upper stages at the complex "also look good," and declared, "We will fly again before the end of this year." He ruled out skipping straight to the larger New Glenn variant but said the company would change how it transports and stands up its rockets, retiring the old "transporter-erector" approach. Many in the industry had assumed a return would slip into 2027 at the earliest. ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/02/blue-origin-plans-to-launch-new-glenn-again-this-year-after-explosion/))

Speaking at Blue Origin's Rocket Park manufacturing campus on Merritt Island on September 28, 2026, he doubled down: "Space is hard. And our job is to make it look easy," he said, adding that hundreds of people were at the pad rebuilding it and that "we still think we have a path to launch this year." He put the company's Florida footprint at millions of square feet of facilities, $3 billion in capital spent to date, and more than 5,000 workers on the job. ([Florida Today](https://www.floridatoday.com/story/tech/science/space/2026/09/28/blue-origin-ceo-dave-limp-sees-path-to-return-to-launch-in-2026-after-new-glenn-rocket-explosion/91985125007/))

Parallel to the rebuild, Limp oversaw a historic shift in how Blue Origin is funded. Announced in July 2026, the company raised outside capital for the first time — $10 billion in total, with $8 billion from outside investors and $2 billion from Bezos himself, valuing the business at about $140 billion and bringing Bezos's cumulative investment to roughly $30 billion since he founded the company in 2000, according to Wall Street Journal reporting. As Limp put it when the round was announced: "We are raising outside money for the first time. Jeff has funded it up till now." ([Behind the Black](https://behindtheblack.com/behind-the-black/points-of-information/blue-origin-successfully-raises-10-billion-in-its-first-outside-investment-round/); [Free Malaysia Today](https://www.freemalaysiatoday.com/category/business/2026/09/30/bezos-no-longer-sole-backer-as-blue-origin-seeks-external-investors))
