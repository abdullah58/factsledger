## Overview

In June 2026, Christiane Amanpour walked onto a Beirut stage to receive the American University of Beirut's highest honor — an honorary Doctor of Humane Letters, conferred at the university's 157th commencement alongside a Palestinian architect and a Harvard physician-scientist ([AUB](https://aub.edu/communications/media/Documents/May-26/AUB_Awards_Honorary_Doctorate_2026-EN.pdf)). The honor landed in the middle of a career that began, improbably, as a desk assistant's job at a fledgling cable network in 1983 and grew into four decades of war reporting, world-leader interviews, and press-freedom advocacy ([AUB](https://aub.edu/communications/media/Documents/May-26/AUB_Awards_Honorary_Doctorate_2026-EN.pdf)). And it landed alongside a wry coda from three years earlier: according to CNN's retired president Tom Johnson, Amanpour has interviewed "almost every world leader" — and in recent years, the one she has never been able to get is [Vladimir Putin](/people/vladimir-putin/). A Russian friend of Johnson's offered an explanation: "I simply think he may be afraid of her" ([Georgia Tech](https://iac.gatech.edu/news/item/667508/christiane-amanpour-receives-ivan-allen-prize)).

## The one interview that never happened

Amanpour's pursuit of Putin became public in February 2024, when [Tucker Carlson](/people/tucker-carlson/) announced he would interview the Russian president and claimed that "not a single Western journalist has bothered to interview" him since the invasion of Ukraine ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/tucker-carlson-slammed-journalists-putin-interview/)). Amanpour answered on X: "Does Tucker really think we journalists haven't been trying to interview President Putin every day since his full scale invasion of Ukraine? It's absurd — we'll continue to ask for an interview, just as we have for years now" ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/tucker-carlson-slammed-journalists-putin-interview/)). She was not alone in the rebuttal — the BBC's Moscow correspondent Steve Rosenberg said the BBC had lodged several [Kremlin](/organizations/kremlin/) requests in 18 months and gotten "always a 'no'" — and even the Kremlin's spokesman contradicted Carlson, saying "many" outlets had requested interviews ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/tucker-carlson-slammed-journalists-putin-interview/)).

The backstory had surfaced a year earlier at Georgia Tech, where Amanpour received the 2023 Ivan Allen Jr. Prize for Social Courage — an award carrying a $100,000 stipend that honors the Atlanta mayor who helped integrate the city, and whose past recipients include John Lewis and Jimmy Carter ([Georgia Tech](https://iac.gatech.edu/news/item/667508/christiane-amanpour-receives-ivan-allen-prize)). Johnson's tribute noted that some leaders found her interviews so tough that President Bill Clinton once complained to CNN about one ([Georgia Tech](https://iac.gatech.edu/news/item/667508/christiane-amanpour-receives-ivan-allen-prize)). Georgia Tech's president, Ángel Cabrera, quoted her credo back at her: "When you're neutral, you become an accessory" ([Georgia Tech](https://iac.gatech.edu/news/item/667508/christiane-amanpour-receives-ivan-allen-prize)).

## From Tehran to the CNN foreign desk

Amanpour was born in London on January 12, 1958, to an Iranian father who was an airline executive and an English mother; the family moved to Tehran shortly after her birth and lived a privileged, politically connected life there ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)). At 11 she was sent back to England for school — first the Holy Cross Convent in Buckinghamshire, then New Hall, the oldest Roman Catholic girls' school in the country ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)). In January 1979, the Islamic revolution toppled the shah and forced many of his followers, the Amanpours among them, out of the country; her father lost everything he had built in Iran. Amanpour later credited this firsthand upheaval with her desire to become a journalist ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)).

She moved to the United States and graduated in journalism from the University of Rhode Island in 1983 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)). After a stint at an NBC affiliate in Providence, she was hired that September by the three-year-old CNN as an assistant on the international news desk ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)). By 1986 she was a producer-correspondent in CNN's New York bureau, and in 1989 she won a posting to Frankfurt, West Germany — arriving just as pro-democracy movements swept Eastern Europe and becoming CNN's reporter on the fall of communism ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)).

## The Gulf War, Bosnia, and 60 Minutes

Amanpour became a familiar face during the Persian Gulf War of 1990–91, covering the conflict from Iraq's invasion of Kuwait through the U.S.-led coalition victory, then reporting on the Kurdish uprising in northern Iraq ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)). In 1992 she went to Bosnia and Herzegovina to cover the war she called "my generation's war." Her reporting from besieged Sarajevo was credited with bringing the conflict's savagery to the world's attention — though some critics accused her of editorializing, saying she was clearly biased against the Serbs ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)).

While remaining CNN's chief international correspondent, she began contributing to [CBS's 60 Minutes](/organizations/cbs-60-minutes/) in 1996 — a dual-network arrangement that continued until 2005 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)). For CNN she produced long-form documentaries that went deeper than nightly news allowed: *Where Have All the Parents Gone?* (2006), on Kenyan children orphaned by AIDS; *In the Footsteps of bin Laden* (2006); *The War Within* (2007), on Islamic radicalization in Britain; and the six-hour *God's Warriors* (2007), on religious fundamentalism across Judaism, Christianity, and Islam ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)). From 2009 to 2010 she hosted CNN's interview series *Amanpour* ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)).

## The ABC detour and the PBS chapter

In 2010 Amanpour left CNN to join ABC News and host its political affairs program *This Week* — but stepped down in December 2011, returning to CNN under a special arrangement while continuing as ABC's global affairs anchor ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)). *Amanpour* returned in 2012 on CNN International, and in 2017 it began airing on PBS as an interim replacement for the *Charlie Rose* show, which had been canceled amid sexual-misconduct allegations against Rose; in 2018 the slot became the newly created *Amanpour & Company*, an hour-long interview program featuring Amanpour and other correspondents ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)).

## UNESCO and press freedom

In April 2015, UNESCO named Amanpour its Goodwill Ambassador for Freedom of Expression and Journalist Safety at a ceremony at the organization's Paris headquarters ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/christiane-amanpour-named-unesco-goodwill-ambassador-freedom-expression-and-journalist-safety)). Director-General Irina Bokova said the honor recognized her efforts to promote "free, independent and pluralistic media, having the right to work free from the threat of violence" ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/christiane-amanpour-named-unesco-goodwill-ambassador-freedom-expression-and-journalist-safety)). In her acceptance speech, Amanpour called journalists "pillars of reform, freedom and democracy" and made an impassioned plea for the release of all imprisoned journalists, condemning the rising number of attacks and assassinations of media professionals ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/christiane-amanpour-named-unesco-goodwill-ambassador-freedom-expression-and-journalist-safety)). At the time she had already won eleven News and Documentary Emmys, four Peabody Awards, two George Polk Awards and three duPont-Columbia Awards; she also sat on the boards of the Committee to Protect Journalists and the International Women's Media Foundation ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/christiane-amanpour-named-unesco-goodwill-ambassador-freedom-expression-and-journalist-safety)). The CBE followed in 2007 — she was named a Commander of the Order of the British Empire for services to journalism — and the Edward R. Murrow Award came in 2002 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christiane-Amanpour)).

## The interview that walked away

In September 2022, a long-planned interview with Iranian President Ebrahim Raisi — set for the sidelines of the UN General Assembly — collapsed after Raisi's aide imposed a last-minute condition: Amanpour had to wear a headscarf ([NPR](https://www.mtpr.org/2022-09-22/cnn-says-irans-president-tried-to-make-a-hijab-a-condition-for-amanpour-interview)). Her team had waited 40 minutes for Raisi when the aide told her the headscarf was a "matter of respect" because of the holy months of Muharram and Safar ([NPR](https://www.mtpr.org/2022-09-22/cnn-says-irans-president-tried-to-make-a-hijab-a-condition-for-amanpour-interview)). "I politely declined. We are in New York, where there is no law or tradition regarding headscarves," she said, pointing out that no previous Iranian president had required it for interviews outside Iran — she had interviewed Hassan Rouhani in the U.S. several times without one ([NPR](https://www.mtpr.org/2022-09-22/cnn-says-irans-president-tried-to-make-a-hijab-a-condition-for-amanpour-interview)). She called the condition "unprecedented and unexpected" ([NPR](https://www.mtpr.org/2022-09-22/cnn-says-irans-president-tried-to-make-a-hijab-a-condition-for-amanpour-interview)). The planned topics — the nationwide demonstrations and women burning their hijabs to protest the death in police custody of Mahsa Amini — never got asked; Iran's state news agency blamed Amanpour for "refusing protocol," which it said was "being determined by the guest" ([NPR](https://www.mtpr.org/2022-09-22/cnn-says-irans-president-tried-to-make-a-hijab-a-condition-for-amanpour-interview)).

## The cancer announcement

On June 14, 2021, Amanpour opened her program with personal news: she had been diagnosed with ovarian cancer after a four-week absence ([Beacon Journal](https://www.beaconjournal.com/story/news/local/2021/06/15/christiane-amanpour-reveals-she-has-ovarian-cancer/7698884002/)). "Like millions of women around the world, I've been diagnosed with ovarian cancer," she said from London. "I've had successful major surgery to remove it, and I'm now undergoing several months of chemotherapy for the very best possible long-term prognosis, and I'm confident" ([Beacon Journal](https://www.beaconjournal.com/story/news/local/2021/06/15/christiane-amanpour-reveals-she-has-ovarian-cancer/7698884002/)). She framed the announcement as a call for early diagnosis — urging women to educate themselves about the disease, get regular screenings and scans, listen to their bodies, and make sure "legitimate medical concerns are not dismissed or diminished" ([Beacon Journal](https://www.beaconjournal.com/story/news/local/2021/06/15/christiane-amanpour-reveals-she-has-ovarian-cancer/7698884002/)). During treatment she anchored Monday through Wednesday ([Beacon Journal](https://www.beaconjournal.com/story/news/local/2021/06/15/christiane-amanpour-reveals-she-has-ovarian-cancer/7698884002/)).
