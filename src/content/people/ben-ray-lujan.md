## Overview

Ben Ray Luján is the Democratic junior United States senator from [New Mexico](/places/new-mexico/), in office since January 2021 after twelve years in the U.S. House of Representatives, where he rose to Assistant Speaker and chaired the campaign committee that retook the House in 2018 ([Associated Press](https://eu.ndinsider.com/story/news/2020/11/03/democrats-see-victory-lujan-keeps-new-mexico-senate-seat/6157018002/); [U.S. House of Representatives](https://history.house.gov/People/Detail/17317)). In the Senate he is best known for two threads that run through his record: connecting rural, Tribal and underserved New Mexico communities to federal investment, and leading the Senate effort to give the Greater Chaco region permanent protection from oil, gas and mineral development ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/nm-delegation-reintroduce-legislation-to-permanently-protect-chaco-canyon)). His career is unusual in how directly it maps onto his home state: he grew up in Nambé, a small farming community north of Santa Fe bordered by the Nambé and Pojoaque Pueblos, and his father spent nearly four decades in the New Mexico Legislature ([Senator Ben Ray Luján's office](https://www.lujan.senate.gov/about/); [Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecordnm.com/articles/news/new-mexico-election-2026-u-s-senator/)).

## Early life, education and state service

Luján was born in Santa Fe on June 7, 1972, graduated from Pojoaque Valley High School in 1990, attended the University of New Mexico from 1990 to 1995, and earned a Bachelor of Business Administration from New Mexico Highlands University in 2007 ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://history.house.gov/People/Detail/17317)). Before entering Congress he worked in New Mexico state government as deputy state treasurer and as director of administrative services and chief financial officer for the state Cultural Affairs Department ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://history.house.gov/People/Detail/17317)). His Senate biography notes that he is one of only two Head Start graduates ever to serve in the Senate, and the first Hispanic to represent New Mexico in the chamber in more than 40 years ([Senator Ben Ray Luján's office](https://www.lujan.senate.gov/about/)).

In 2004 he won election to the New Mexico Public Regulation Commission, the body overseeing the state's utilities, and chaired it from 2005 to 2007, serving on the commission until 2008 ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://history.house.gov/People/Detail/17317); [Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecordnm.com/articles/news/new-mexico-election-2026-u-s-senator/)). His father, Ben Ray Luján Sr., served in the state Legislature for nearly 40 years, including more than a decade as House speaker, a family record that made the younger Luján a familiar name in New Mexico politics long before he reached Washington ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecordnm.com/articles/news/new-mexico-election-2026-u-s-senator/)).

## House career: the 2018 majority and Assistant Speaker

Luján won a seat in the U.S. House of Representatives in 2008 and represented New Mexico's 3rd congressional district from January 2009 to January 2021 ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecordnm.com/articles/news/new-mexico-election-2026-u-s-senator/); [U.S. House of Representatives](https://history.house.gov/People/Detail/17317)). He chaired the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee in 2018, the cycle in which Democrats retook the House, and the final tally gave Democrats a net gain of 40 seats, for a 235–200 House majority in the 116th Congress ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecordnm.com/articles/news/new-mexico-election-2026-u-s-senator/); [Eno Center for Transportation](https://enotrans.org/article/final-democratic-gains-in-u-s-house-40-seats/)). In that Congress he served as Assistant Speaker, the No. 4 position in House Democratic leadership ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://history.house.gov/People/Detail/17317); [Associated Press](https://eu.ndinsider.com/story/news/2020/11/03/democrats-see-victory-lujan-keeps-new-mexico-senate-seat/6157018002/)).

In 2020 he ran for the Senate seat being vacated by retiring Senator Tom Udall, won the Democratic primary uncontested, and defeated Republican former television meteorologist Mark Ronchetti and Libertarian scientist Bob Walsh in the general election ([Associated Press](https://eu.ndinsider.com/story/news/2020/11/03/democrats-see-victory-lujan-keeps-new-mexico-senate-seat/6157018002/)). On the campaign trail he emphasised consumer health protections under the Affordable Care Act and new investment in clean-energy infrastructure ([Associated Press](https://eu.ndinsider.com/story/news/2020/11/03/democrats-see-victory-lujan-keeps-new-mexico-senate-seat/6157018002/)).

## Senate career, stroke and return

Luján took office in the [U.S. Senate](/organizations/us-senate/) in January 2021 and sits on the committees for Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry; Budget; Commerce, Science, and Transportation; Finance; and Indian Affairs ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecordnm.com/articles/news/new-mexico-election-2026-u-s-senator/)). His Senate office states that he helped pass the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law that created the Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment (BEAD) program, and that New Mexico's $382 million broadband plan subsequently received federal approval, aimed at reaching full connectivity across the state's rural, Tribal and underserved communities ([Senator Ben Ray Luján's office](https://www.lujan.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/lujan-applauds-federal-approval-of-new-mexicos-382-million-broadband-plan/)).

In late January 2022, while visiting New Mexico, Luján suffered a stroke in the cerebellum that affected his balance, checking himself into CHRISTUS St. Vincent Regional Medical Center in Santa Fe before transfer to the University of New Mexico Hospital in Albuquerque, where he underwent decompressive surgery to ease swelling ([The Well News](https://www.thewellnews.com/us-senate/lujan-greeted-with-standing-ovation-on-senate-return/)). He returned to Senate work in March 2022 to a bipartisan standing ovation at a Commerce Committee hearing, telling colleagues, "It's an absolute honor to be back," and saying he expected to fully participate in Senate business going forward ([The Well News](https://www.thewellnews.com/us-senate/lujan-greeted-with-standing-ovation-on-senate-return/)). Citing that experience, he later helped secure $3 million for a new MRI scanner at the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center to support closer study of brain chemistry ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecordnm.com/articles/news/new-mexico-election-2026-u-s-senator/)).

In June 2026 he won the Democratic Senate primary with 84 percent of the vote against 16 percent for his opponent, and he faces Republican write-in candidate Larry Marker in the November 3, 2026 general election ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecordnm.com/articles/news/new-mexico-election-2026-u-s-senator/)). His Senate biography also records a June 2026 James Beard Impact Award for his work to restore the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) ([Senator Ben Ray Luján's office](https://www.lujan.senate.gov/about/)).

## The Chaco campaign: from temporary withdrawal to permanent protection

The defining legislative thread of Luján's Senate tenure is the campaign to protect [Chaco Canyon](/places/chaco-canyon/) and the Greater Chaco region, the landscape in northwestern New Mexico that holds living sacred sites for many Pueblos and Tribes and was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/nm-delegation-reintroduce-legislation-to-permanently-protect-chaco-canyon)). In April 2025, Luján led the Senate side of the New Mexico delegation's reintroduction of the Chaco Cultural Heritage Area Protection Act, with Representative Teresa [Leger Fernández](/people/teresa-leger-fernandez/) leading it in the House ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/breaking-nm-delegation-condemns-trump-administrations-move-to-begin-reversing-protections-for-chaco-canyon)). The bill would prevent future leasing and development of oil, gas and minerals on non-Indian federal lands within a 10-mile buffer zone around Chaco Culture National Historical Park, creating a Chaco Protection Zone ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/nm-delegation-reintroduce-legislation-to-permanently-protect-chaco-canyon)).

The point of the legislation is permanence: in 2023 the Biden administration began a 20-year administrative withdrawal of non-Indian federal lands in the same buffer zone, a step the delegation's release describes as successful but reversible, whereas the bill would withdraw those lands from new mineral development in perpetuity ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/nm-delegation-reintroduce-legislation-to-permanently-protect-chaco-canyon)). Independent local reporting confirms the same logic, noting that the bill seeks to enshrine the 2023 withdrawal in law after earlier introductions never reached the president's desk ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news/lawmakers-reintroduce-bill-permanently-protect-chaco-canyon-oil-gas-and-mining)). The urgency sharpened when the Trump administration began the process of revoking Public Land Order No. 7923, the order behind the withdrawal, prompting the delegation to condemn the move and pledge to pass the Act into law ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/breaking-nm-delegation-condemns-trump-administrations-move-to-begin-reversing-protections-for-chaco-canyon)).

The campaign's institutional anchor is the [All Pueblo Council of Governors](/organizations/all-pueblo-council-of-governors/), whose chairman, James R. Mountain, is quoted in the reintroduction release thanking Luján by name and crediting "countless meetings, cultural resource studies, and tireless advocacy" for carrying the effort forward ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/nm-delegation-reintroduce-legislation-to-permanently-protect-chaco-canyon)). Luján's own statement at reintroduction called the Greater Chaco Region "one of the world's greatest treasures that must be protected for our future generations" and described permanent protection as a longstanding priority for Pueblo and Tribal communities, environmental advocates and the New Mexico delegation ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/nm-delegation-reintroduce-legislation-to-permanently-protect-chaco-canyon)). The bill is also designed to leave Indian, private and state lands untouched: it strengthens protections for infrastructure and development on those lands, including Navajo allotments, and a 2022 federal assessment found only 10 Navajo allotments would be highly impacted by the withdrawal ([Senator Martin Heinrich's office](https://www.heinrich.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/nm-delegation-reintroduce-legislation-to-permanently-protect-chaco-canyon)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim that the Chaco Cultural Heritage Area Protection Act has been enacted: it has been introduced in previous sessions and reintroduced in April 2025, and its fate remains legislative, not settled ([Cibola Citizen](https://www.cibolacitizen.com/news/lawmakers-reintroduce-bill-permanently-protect-chaco-canyon-oil-gas-and-mining)). It does not predict the outcome of the November 3, 2026 general election, which had not occurred at drafting time ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecordnm.com/articles/news/new-mexico-election-2026-u-s-senator/)). It does not resolve the differing committee lists across Senate biography vintages, the differing Public Regulation Commission date summaries, or the differing All Pueblo Council of Governors leadership titles in 2026 reporting; each is flagged in the open items above rather than silently settled ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://history.house.gov/People/Detail/17317)). It makes no claim about Luján's private or family life beyond his father's public legislative career and his Nambé upbringing, and it does not present the first-party award and broadband statements as independently verified achievements ([Senator Ben Ray Luján's office](https://www.lujan.senate.gov/about/)).
