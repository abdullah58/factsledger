## Overview

Michael Truell is the co-founder and chief executive of [Anysphere](/organizations/anysphere/), the San Francisco company behind Cursor, the AI code editor that grew from a March 2023 launch to a $29.3 billion valuation by November 2025 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/10/11/anysphere-raises-8m-from-openai-to-build-an-ai-powered-ide/); [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/biggest-funding-rounds-ai-defense-tech-anysphere/)). As a schoolboy at Horace Mann in the Bronx, he co-created Halite, an online programming competition that won a national high-school computing prize ([Association for Computing Machinery](https://www.acm.org/media-center/2018/march/cutler-bell-prize-2017)).

Truell grew up in New York City and attended Horace Mann, a private prep school in the Bronx ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). He then left the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](/universities/massachusetts-institute-of-technology/) in 2022 with three classmates to found Anysphere, after two earlier product ideas failed ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). He enrolled at MIT, finished his first year, and at 18 took a summer internship at Google, where he worked on language models for feed ranking ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). That internship changed his trajectory: there he met Ali Partovi, an early investor in Facebook and Airbnb who was recruiting for his Neo Scholars program, an accelerator for young tech talent ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). Truell completed a written coding test Partovi gave him in record time, became one of just 30 Neo Scholars selected that year, and Partovi went on to become one of Cursor's first investors ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)).

## Halite: a competition built by a schoolboy

Long before Cursor, Truell had already learned how to build tools that other programmers want to use. With Horace Mann classmate Benjamin Spector, he created Halite, an online programming competition ([Association for Computing Machinery](https://www.acm.org/media-center/2018/march/cutler-bell-prize-2017)). ACM's prize citation describes it as, by its second iteration, "one of the largest limited-time programming competitions with more than 5,500 users over the course of the two competition runs" ([Association for Computing Machinery](https://www.acm.org/media-center/2018/march/cutler-bell-prize-2017)). The design goal, as ACM records it, was an open-source platform and game where "anyone could easily program a bot, but would also have the depth to support and interest experienced programmers" ([Association for Computing Machinery](https://www.acm.org/media-center/2018/march/cutler-bell-prize-2017)). Players could write code in any language, test and visualise their bots locally, and, once uploaded, watch them play against other bots in real time with performance feedback in real time ([Association for Computing Machinery](https://www.acm.org/media-center/2018/march/cutler-bell-prize-2017)).

Spector and Truell did not just run the competition; they wrote up how it worked. Their paper, "The Design and Implementation of Modern Online Programming Competitions", submitted in October 2017, presents a framework for building such competitions, including principles for designing the multiplayer game and a practical framework for constructing the competition environment, using the 2016-17 Halite challenge as its example ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.07738)). In March 2018, ACM and the Computer Science Teachers Association announced Spector and Truell as winners of the Cutler-Bell Prize in High School Computing, a prize for graduating high-school seniors whose submitted projects engage modern technology and computer science, with each winner receiving a $10,000 cash prize ([Association for Computing Machinery](https://www.acm.org/media-center/2018/march/cutler-bell-prize-2017)). ACM noted that Halite had let thousands of users, mostly university and high-school students, some of whom had never programmed before, learn skills ranging from programming languages to machine learning ([Association for Computing Machinery](https://www.acm.org/media-center/2018/march/cutler-bell-prize-2017)). The through-line to Cursor is plain: a teenager who built a platform where beginners and experts could both program, and who documented the design, later built an editor meant to make professional programming itself faster and more accessible.

## From two failed ideas to Cursor

At MIT, Truell met [Sualeh Asif](/people/sualeh-asif/), [Arvid Lunnemark](/people/arvid-lunnemark/) and [Aman Sanger](/people/aman-sanger/), and the four became close friends, sharing the goal of creating an integrated development environment that could speed up common programming and software-building tasks, like debugging ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/10/11/anysphere-raises-8m-from-openai-to-build-an-ai-powered-ide/)). In 2022 the four left school to start Anysphere, the company behind Cursor ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). Their first two ideas failed: a coding assistant for mechanical engineers, and a message-encryption project ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). They then pivoted to AI coding, a space they had initially avoided because, as Truell told Y Combinator's AI Startup School, they thought it was too competitive ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)).

Cursor launched in March 2023 ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). It is a fork of VS Code, Microsoft's open-source code editor, packing AI-powered tools designed to help developers write code and ask questions about it ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/10/11/anysphere-raises-8m-from-openai-to-build-an-ai-powered-ide/)). In October 2023 Anysphere announced an $8 million seed round led by OpenAI's Startup Fund, with participation from former GitHub chief executive Nat Friedman and Dropbox co-founder Arash Ferdowsi, bringing its total raised to $11 million, with annual recurring revenue already over $1 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/10/11/anysphere-raises-8m-from-openai-to-build-an-ai-powered-ide/)). Truell told TechCrunch at the time that the company's mission was "to make programming an order of magnitude faster, more fun and creative" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/10/11/anysphere-raises-8m-from-openai-to-build-an-ai-powered-ide/)).

Growth then accelerated sharply. Cursor reached $100 million in annualized revenue in 12 months, and its valuation went from $2.5 billion in January 2025 to $29.3 billion by November 2025 after raising $2.3 billion in Series D funding ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). Crunchbase News confirms the Series D: $2.3 billion backed by Accel, Thrive Capital, Andreessen Horowitz, DST Global, Coatue, Nvidia and Google, setting a $29.3 billion post-money valuation, more than three times what the company had secured just six months earlier ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/biggest-funding-rounds-ai-defense-tech-anysphere/)). Forbes estimates that Truell holds roughly a 4.5% stake in Anysphere, putting his net worth at approximately $1.3 billion ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). That is a stat-plus-source-plus-implication worth stating plainly: a founder who started by giving away a programming game to thousands of students now holds a stake worth over a billion dollars in a company built on the same instinct, that programming tools should be open to newcomers and powerful for experts.

In April 2026 the story took another turn. On April 21, [SpaceX](/organizations/spacex/) announced on X that it was working together with Cursor to develop what it called "the world's best coding and knowledge work AI" ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). The deal had two parts: SpaceX pays Cursor $10 billion for their work together, or it acquires the company for $60 billion later in 2026 ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). Truell responded on X that he was "excited to partner with the SpaceX team to scale up Composer", the name of Cursor's AI model ([Moneywise](https://moneywise.com/news/top-stories/spacex-cursor-deal-michael-truell-mit-dropout-ceo)). The acquisition, confirmed in a filing with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, was expected to close by the third quarter of 2026, with each of Cursor's four co-founders projected to be worth roughly $2.7 billion once the transaction completes ([TechJuice](https://www.techjuice.pk/sualeh-asif-aman-sanger-cursor-billionaires-spacex/)).
