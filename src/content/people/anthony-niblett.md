## Overview

Anthony Niblett is a professor at the [University of Toronto](/universities/university-of-toronto/)'s Faculty of Law and, since 1 January 2026, the inaugural Donald Harrison Chair in AI and Technology. A Harvard-trained economist turned law professor, he co-founded the legal-AI company [Blue J](/organizations/blue-j/) in 2015 and is best known for scholarship arguing that predictive technology will replace rules and standards with personalised "micro-directives" — machine-generated instructions telling each person exactly how to comply with the law in their own circumstances.

Niblett joined the Faculty of Law in 2011 and held the Canada Research Chair in Law, Economics & Innovation from 2016, renewed in 2020 ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/u-t-gains-34-new-canada-research-chairs)). His research spans law and economics, contract law, judicial behaviour, artificial intelligence, innovation and competition policy ([University of Toronto](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/donald-harrison-chair-ai-and-technology)).

## Education and academic career

Niblett holds a PhD in economics from [Harvard University](/universities/harvard-university/) and degrees in law and commerce from the University of Melbourne ([University of Toronto](https://futureoflaw.utoronto.ca/node/105)). Before joining the University of Toronto, he was a Bigelow Fellow at the University of Chicago Law School, one of the historic centres of law-and-economics scholarship ([University of Toronto](https://futureoflaw.utoronto.ca/node/105)). He joined the [Toronto](/places/toronto/) faculty in 2011, the same year his early empirical work on the evolution of legal rules was circulating as a National Bureau of Economic Research working paper ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/cf_dev/AbsByAuth.cfm?per_id=1302584)).

He teaches Contracts, Legal Methods, Competition Policy and Economic Analysis of Law, and has taught courses in Australia, the United States and China ([University of Toronto](https://futureoflaw.utoronto.ca/node/105)). His teaching has been recognised twice: the University of Toronto's Early Career Teaching Award in 2016 and the Alan Mewett QC Prize for excellence in teaching, awarded by the JD class of 2017 ([University of Toronto](https://futureoflaw.utoronto.ca/node/105)). He is also a Faculty Affiliate Researcher with the Vector Institute for Artificial Intelligence, and co-director of the Faculty of Law's Centre for Innovation Law & Policy ([University of Toronto](https://futureoflaw.utoronto.ca/node/105); [Canadian Law and Economics Association](https://www.canlecon.org/the-team)).

In December 2025 the law faculty announced he would become the inaugural Donald Harrison Chair in AI and Technology, created by a gift from alumnus Donald Harrison (LLB 1997), a Google executive ([University of Toronto](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/donald-harrison-chair-ai-and-technology)). Dean Jutta Brunnée called him "uniquely equipped to advance our thinking around law and technology and its implications for society as well as the legal profession" ([University of Toronto](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/donald-harrison-chair-ai-and-technology)).

## Scholarship: rules, standards and micro-directives

Niblett's most cited idea, developed with University of Chicago law professor Anthony J. Casey, is the "micro-directive". In "The Death of Rules and Standards" (Indiana Law Journal, 2017), the two argue that lawmakers have always had to choose between rules, which give certainty but fit badly, and standards, which fit well but leave people guessing ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/cf_dev/AbsByAuth.cfm?per_id=1302584)). Predictive technology, they say, dissolves the choice: a machine can translate a legislative goal such as "drive reasonably" into a precise instruction for one driver in one moment — a speed limit tailored to that driver's experience, the weather and the traffic, broadcast into the car in real time ([University of Chicago Law School](https://www.law.uchicago.edu/news/research-matters-anthony-casey-death-rules-and-standards)).

The appeal is that a micro-directive gives the certainty of a rule with the calibration of a standard: the driver knows for sure whether the behaviour is legal, and the instruction is correctly set for the conditions ([University of Chicago Law School](https://www.law.uchicago.edu/news/research-matters-anthony-casey-death-rules-and-standards)). But the authors are candid that the shift would remake legal institutions: lawyers would become more like lobbyists, arguing over algorithms and policy goals; judges would advise on whether an algorithm achieves the right policy instead of making law case by case; and the personal data the system needs raises real questions about privacy and autonomy ([University of Chicago Law School](https://www.law.uchicago.edu/news/research-matters-anthony-casey-death-rules-and-standards)).

Niblett and Casey developed the idea across a run of papers: "Self-Driving Laws" (University of Toronto Law Journal, 2016), "Self-Driving Contracts" (Journal of Corporation Law, 2017), "A Framework for the New Personalization of Law" (University of Chicago Law Review, 2019) and "Problems with Probability" (University of Toronto Law Journal, 2023) ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/cf_dev/AbsByAuth.cfm?per_id=1302584)). With [Benjamin Alarie](/people/benjamin-alarie/) and [Albert Yoon](/people/albert-yoon/), he also presented "Regulation by Machine" at the 2016 Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems, arguing that machine learning could improve the regulation of human activity — and that the algorithms doing so would, in effect, "become the law" ([Slaw](https://www.osgoode.yorku.ca/osgoode_news/machines-regulating-humans-will-algorithms-become-law/)). His most recent work in this line, "AI and the Nature of Disagreement" (with Yoon), appeared in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A in 2024 ([The Royal Society](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsta/issue/382/2270)).

## Judicial behaviour and contracts

A second strand of Niblett's scholarship is empirical: measuring how judges actually behave, rather than how doctrine says they should. In "Friendly Precedent" (with Albert Yoon, William & Mary Law Review, 2016), he built a dataset of 591,936 citations to US Supreme Court decisions by federal Courts of Appeals in 127,668 unanimous decisions from 1971 to 2007, and found that panels gravitate toward "friendly" precedent — decisions delivered by Supreme Court justices with similar political preferences to the panel ([William & Mary Law Review](https://scholarship.law.wm.edu/wmlr/vol57/iss5/5/)). The result matters because it shows judges holding different conceptions of what binding precedent requires, not merely disagreeing about outcomes.

His earlier sole-authored "Tracking Inconsistent Judicial Behavior" (International Review of Law & Economics, 2012) pursued the same question from a different angle, while his contract-law work includes "The Limits of Public Contract Law" (Law and Contemporary Problems, 2022) and "Hostile Takeovers and Overreliance" (Seattle University Law Review, 2015) ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/cf_dev/AbsByAuth.cfm?per_id=1302584)). With Alarie he has also written on explainable AI in tax (2023), addressing the problem that a prediction a lawyer cannot interrogate is of limited use in practice ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/cf_dev/AbsByAuth.cfm?per_id=1302584)).

## Blue J Legal: from a Watson demo to a US$700 million company

Niblett's scholarship and his company grew from the same seed. In 2014 the University of Toronto's computer science department was one of ten institutions worldwide invited to build applications on [IBM](/organizations/ibm/)'s Watson, and Alarie was asked to judge the legal apps students produced ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/law-and-computer-science-collaborate-change-future-legal-research)). The campus contest sparked the idea for a tax-law adviser built on cognitive computing, and Niblett joined Alarie, Yoon and former IBM enterprise software developer Brett Janssen in founding Blue J in 2015 ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/u-t-startup-blue-j-legal-raises-us7-million-plans-cross-border-expansion)). Niblett had known Janssen since childhood, and it was Niblett who brought him in to lead the technology work ([Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/resources/legal-technology/blue-j-legal-co-founder-benjamin-alarie-on-how-ai-is-powering-a-new-generation-of-legal-tech/374021)).

At a 2015 demonstration Niblett summed up the early system with a line that captures the whole project: "Watson is a good student, but we are still teaching it" ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/law-and-computer-science-collaborate-change-future-legal-research)). The product scans thousands of judicial decisions to predict how a court would likely decide a tax or employment question, presenting lawyers with the prediction and the underlying case law ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/u-t-startup-blue-j-legal-raises-us7-million-plans-cross-border-expansion)). At a 2017 Osgoode Hall talk, Alarie said the system had achieved a 90% accuracy rate for fact-based dispute resolution — a company claim from that time, not a current verified figure ([Slaw](https://www.osgoode.yorku.ca/osgoode_news/machines-regulating-humans-will-algorithms-become-law/)).

The financing followed the thesis. Blue J raised US$7 million in November 2018, led by Relay Ventures, to expand into the United States, and a US$9 million Series B in August 2021 ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/u-t-startup-blue-j-legal-raises-us7-million-plans-cross-border-expansion); [Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/resources/legal-technology/blue-j-legal-co-founder-benjamin-alarie-on-how-ai-is-powering-a-new-generation-of-legal-tech/374021)). After relaunching its product in 2023 around large-language-model technology, the company raised US$122 million (C$167 million) in a Series D in August 2025, led by Sapphire Ventures and Oak HC/FT at a valuation above US$300 million ([University of Toronto](https://gro.utoronto.ca/policy-in-the-news/u-of-t-startup-blue-j-legal-raises-167-million-the-globe-and-mail/)). On 10 November 2026 it announced a US$176 million Series E led by JMI Equity, valuing it at more than US$700 million ([Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/cl-plus/deals/4411)). More than 6,500 organisations now use the platform, which has answered over 14 million tax questions across Canadian, US and UK tax law ([Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/cl-plus/deals/4411)). Its clients include the Canada Revenue Agency and the Department of Justice, and the UK is its next market after the US and Canada ([Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/resources/legal-technology/blue-j-legal-co-founder-benjamin-alarie-on-how-ai-is-powering-a-new-generation-of-legal-tech/374021)).

Niblett himself frames the arc in the terms of his scholarship. "We predicted this world where people would be told — instantly — what you're doing is lawful or not," he said when the Harrison chair was announced, adding: "where do you draw the line between instantaneous legal advice from a machine versus an instantaneous legal ruling from the machine?" ([University of Toronto](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/donald-harrison-chair-ai-and-technology)). He insists the human lawyer still has a role, because legal education is about "understanding the process" of reasoning, not "teaching an 'answer'" ([University of Toronto](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/donald-harrison-chair-ai-and-technology)).

## Teaching, service and public engagement

Beyond the classroom, Niblett is academic advisor to the Future of Law Lab, the faculty's hub for law, innovation and technology, which places students in half-summer internships at Blue J ([University of Toronto](https://www.law.utoronto.ca/news/future-law-here)). He is treasurer of the Canadian Law and Economics Association ([Canadian Law and Economics Association](https://www.canlecon.org/the-team)), and has given a TED talk on machine learning in the law ([University of Toronto](https://futureoflaw.utoronto.ca/node/105)). And he has one credential no other law professor can claim: he won Jeopardy! — on Christmas Day 2013, two years after the computer Watson had won the same show ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/law-and-computer-science-collaborate-change-future-legal-research)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Who is Anthony Niblett?** Anthony Niblett is a professor at the University of Toronto's Faculty of Law and, since 1 January 2026, the inaugural Donald Harrison Chair in AI and Technology ([University of Toronto](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/donald-harrison-chair-ai-and-technology)). He is a Harvard-trained economist who researches law and economics, contracts, judicial behaviour and artificial intelligence, and he co-founded the legal-AI company Blue J Legal in 2015 ([University of Toronto](https://futureoflaw.utoronto.ca/node/105)).

**What is a "micro-directive"?** A micro-directive is Niblett's term, developed with Anthony J. Casey, for a personalised, machine-generated instruction that tells a person exactly how to comply with the law in their specific circumstances — for example, a speed limit calibrated to one driver, the weather and the traffic, delivered in real time ([University of Chicago Law School](https://www.law.uchicago.edu/news/research-matters-anthony-casey-death-rules-and-standards)). The idea is set out in "The Death of Rules and Standards" (Indiana Law Journal, 2017) ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/cf_dev/AbsByAuth.cfm?per_id=1302584)).

**What is Blue J Legal?** Blue J Legal is a Toronto legal-technology company co-founded by Niblett in 2015 with Benjamin Alarie, Albert Yoon and Brett Janssen ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/u-t-startup-blue-j-legal-raises-us7-million-plans-cross-border-expansion)). Its AI platform predicts the outcomes of tax and employment-law questions, and in November 2026 it raised US$176 million at a valuation above US$700 million ([Canadian Lawyer](https://www.canadianlawyermag.com/cl-plus/deals/4411)).

**What chair does Anthony Niblett hold?** He held the Canada Research Chair in Law, Economics & Innovation from 2016, renewed in 2020 ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/u-t-gains-34-new-canada-research-chairs)). From 1 January 2026 he is the inaugural Donald Harrison Chair in AI and Technology ([University of Toronto](https://jackmanlaw.utoronto.ca/news/donald-harrison-chair-ai-and-technology)).

**Did Anthony Niblett really win Jeopardy!?** Yes. He won on Christmas Day 2013, as the University of Toronto reported when profiling the Blue J team in 2015 ([University of Toronto](https://www.utoronto.ca/news/law-and-computer-science-collaborate-change-future-legal-research)).
