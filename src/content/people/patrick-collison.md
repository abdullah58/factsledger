## Overview

Patrick Collison is the co-founder and chief executive of [Stripe](/organizations/stripe/), the payments company he started with his younger brother John in 2010, and the co-founder of the Arc Institute, a biomedical research organization pioneering a new model for basic research. In April 2025 he joined [Meta](/organizations/meta/)'s board of directors, and like his brother he was an early investor in the collectibles platform [Alt](/organizations/alt/) ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/04/patrick-collison-and-dina-powell-mccormick-to-join-meta-board-of-directors/); [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/startups/alt-raises-31m-to-buy-and-sell-sports-cards/)).

One idea connects all three acts: infrastructure. At Stripe the missing infrastructure was a simple way to accept payments online; in science, Collison concluded, it was a fast way to fund researchers. The through-line runs from a programming language he built as a teenager to a payments giant valued at $95 billion in 2021 and then to a grant program that moved money in 48 hours ([Retool](https://retool.com/blog/stripe-ceo-ai-agents-and-the-future-of-software); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/03/15/daily-crunch-stripe-valued-at-95b/); [Industrial Equipment News](https://www.ien.com/product-development/news/22867738/quick-grants-from-tech-billionaires-aim-to-speed-up-research)).

## A teenage language and a userless company

Patrick's journey into programming started with a fascination for obscure programming languages: at 16 he created a language called Chroma, a Lisp dialect designed for web applications ([Retool](https://retool.com/blog/stripe-ceo-ai-agents-and-the-future-of-software)). Not long after, he built his first company, Auctomatic, entirely on Smalltalk. The architecture was elegant and the tool was powerful, but there was one problem, as he later put it: "Nobody ever benefited from it because Auctomatic had no users" — it was, he joked, "the world's most elegantly designed userless company" ([Retool](https://retool.com/blog/stripe-ceo-ai-agents-and-the-future-of-software)).

That experience shaped one of Stripe's founding philosophies: prioritize pragmatism over elegance. "Every time there's a super elegant way to do things and a practical, pragmatic way to do things, we're just gonna cut the corner — at least until we validate that there's actual user value here," he said ([Retool](https://retool.com/blog/stripe-ceo-ai-agents-and-the-future-of-software)). Stripe was accordingly built in Ruby, not Lisp or Smalltalk, and initially used MongoDB despite its limitations, because validation trumped architectural purity ([Retool](https://retool.com/blog/stripe-ceo-ai-agents-and-the-future-of-software)). The same philosophy extended beyond code into a broader obsession: understanding what drives human progress and why it might be slowing down, a field he argues has surprisingly little organized study given that economic growth is, in his view, the most important development in human history ([Retool](https://retool.com/blog/stripe-ceo-ai-agents-and-the-future-of-software)).

## Stripe: from developer tool to $95 billion

Stripe began as an answer to the brothers' own frustration: accepting payments online meant awkward integrations and multiple financial intermediaries, while building a website had become easy. The company they started in 2010 grew into a technology company building programmable financial services, with Patrick as chief executive and John as president ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/04/patrick-collison-and-dina-powell-mccormick-to-join-meta-board-of-directors/)). In March 2021 Stripe raised $600 million at a $95 billion valuation, saying it would use the money to expand in Europe and grow its global payments and treasury network ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/03/15/daily-crunch-stripe-valued-at-95b/)). Patrick's investing footprint extended beyond payments: he and John were named among the investors in Alt's $31 million seed and Series A rounds, the Series A led by Alexis Ohanian's Seven Seven Six ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/startups/alt-raises-31m-to-buy-and-sell-sports-cards/)).

## Fast Grants and the Arc Institute: infrastructure for science

In March 2020, as the first pandemic lockdowns took effect, an experiment in science philanthropy was hatched in a five-minute call between Patrick Collison and economist Tyler Cowen ([Industrial Equipment News](https://www.ien.com/product-development/news/22867738/quick-grants-from-tech-billionaires-aim-to-speed-up-research)). Researchers were waiting to hear whether they could redirect federal grants to COVID-19 work, and the pair worried the National Institutes of Health was not moving fast enough — so they launched Fast Grants to get emergency research dollars to virologists, coronavirus experts and other scientists rapidly ([Industrial Equipment News](https://www.ien.com/product-development/news/22867738/quick-grants-from-tech-billionaires-aim-to-speed-up-research)). Collison and his brother John contributed, and together with Cowen they raised more than $50 million from tech figures including Jack Dorsey, Elon Musk, Peter Thiel, Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan, and Eric and Wendy Schmidt ([Industrial Equipment News](https://www.ien.com/product-development/news/22867738/quick-grants-from-tech-billionaires-aim-to-speed-up-research)).

The design was deliberately the opposite of traditional grantmaking: the first round of grants went out in 48 hours, later rounds within two weeks, against the hundreds of days a scientist typically waits to hear from the NIH ([Industrial Equipment News](https://www.ien.com/product-development/news/22867738/quick-grants-from-tech-billionaires-aim-to-speed-up-research)). Grants of $10,000 to $500,000 backed early efforts to sequence new coronavirus variants, clinical trials of repurposed drugs and a simple saliva-based COVID-19 test, and by January 2022 all the money had gone out to more than 260 projects ([Industrial Equipment News](https://www.ien.com/product-development/news/22867738/quick-grants-from-tech-billionaires-aim-to-speed-up-research)). The model drew both applause and scepticism — some scientists questioned whether small-scale funding experiments were money well spent, and others worried about science research being driven by a handful of tech elites — but it also inspired imitators and a permanent institution ([Industrial Equipment News](https://www.ien.com/product-development/news/22867738/quick-grants-from-tech-billionaires-aim-to-speed-up-research)).

That institution was the Arc Institute, which Collison helped launch in 2021 with $650 million pledged by more than a dozen donors, himself included alongside Ethereum creator Vitalik Buterin ([Industrial Equipment News](https://www.ien.com/product-development/news/22867738/quick-grants-from-tech-billionaires-aim-to-speed-up-research)). The Palo Alto-based biomedical research organization gives scientists no-strings-attached funding over eight-year terms to study the causes of complex diseases like cancer, so that if researchers want to change course, their hands are not tied — the Fast Grants lesson, made permanent ([Industrial Equipment News](https://www.ien.com/product-development/news/22867738/quick-grants-from-tech-billionaires-aim-to-speed-up-research)). Meta's announcement of his board seat described the Arc Institute the same way, as a biomedical research institute pioneering a new model for basic research ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/04/patrick-collison-and-dina-powell-mccormick-to-join-meta-board-of-directors/)). Meta's chief executive Mark Zuckerberg said Patrick and Dina Powell McCormick, elected together effective April 15, 2025, brought experience supporting businesses and entrepreneurs that would be valuable to the businesses relying on Meta's services ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/04/patrick-collison-and-dina-powell-mccormick-to-join-meta-board-of-directors/)).
