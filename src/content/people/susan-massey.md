## Overview

Susan Massey is a US tax lawyer who spent 13 years in the [Internal Revenue Service](/organizations/internal-revenue-service/)'s Office of Chief Counsel — the last four as a Branch Chief in its Corporate division — and now leads tax research at [Blue J](/organizations/blue-j/), the Toronto legal-tech company whose generative-AI platform answers tax questions with cited sources. She holds a J.D. and an LL.M. in International & Comparative Law from [Duke University](/universities/duke-university/) School of Law ([AICPA](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/cpe-learning/webcast/tax-practice-quarterly-navigating-innovation-in-tax)).

Massey's job at Blue J is the human half of an AI product. As vice-president of tax research, she leads the team responsible for the content and tax technical review of the platform ([AICPA](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/cpe-learning/webcast/tax-practice-quarterly-navigating-innovation-in-tax)). Her experts constantly test the AI and refine its performance — the quality-control loop that Blue J credits, alongside exclusive content licensing and user feedback, with turning a flawed 2023 launch into a platform that processed more than 3 million tax research queries in 2025 ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/how-ai-tax-startup-blue-j-torched-its-entire-business-model-for-chatgpt-and)). She is also a published writer on the subject: in 2023 she co-authored two [Tax Notes](/organizations/tax-notes/) Federal articles testing whether AI can do tax research reliably, including a demonstration that ChatGPT confidently invents tax law that does not exist ([Tax Notes Federal](https://3838611.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/3838611/tax%20notes%20articles%202023/Rise%20of%20Generative%20AI%20in%20Tax%20Research%20%5BMay%2026%5D.pdf)).

## Thirteen years at the IRS Office of Chief Counsel

Massey practiced for 13 years in the International and Corporate divisions of the IRS Office of Chief Counsel, serving the last four years as a Branch Chief in Corporate ([AICPA](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/cpe-learning/webcast/tax-practice-quarterly-navigating-innovation-in-tax)). That career is the credential her current role rests on: she spent over a decade inside the agency that administers and enforces US federal tax law, finishing in a senior supervisory post in its corporate-tax work, before moving to the private sector to build the tool that now answers the questions she used to decide from the other side.

What is documented is the duration, the two divisions, and the four-year Branch Chief tenure, all from the AICPA's professional bio ([AICPA](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/cpe-learning/webcast/tax-practice-quarterly-navigating-innovation-in-tax)). One widely cited account compresses the record: VentureBeat describes her as having "spent 13 years at the IRS Office of Chief Counsel as Branch Chief for Corporate Tax" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/how-ai-tax-startup-blue-j-torched-its-entire-business-model-for-chatgpt-and)). ## Building Ask Blue J

By February 2023 Massey was a director at Blue J Legal, co-authoring a Tax Notes article that applied the company's machine-learning model to a Tax Court case ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/relief-innocent-spouses-not-so-podlucky/2023/02/24/7fzml)). By May 2023 she was vice-president of legal research, the title under which she co-authored the company's paper on generative AI in tax research ([Tax Notes Federal](https://3838611.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/3838611/tax%20notes%20articles%202023/Rise%20of%20Generative%20AI%20in%20Tax%20Research%20%5BMay%2026%5D.pdf)). Three months later, on 23 August 2023, Blue J unveiled Ask Blue J, its generative-AI research platform for US tax law, with Massey as vice-president of legal research ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20230823880057/en/Introducing-Ask-Blue-J-the-Groundbreaking-Generative-AI-Platform-for-Tax?_gl=1)).

The launch release put her at the centre of the product's credibility claim. "Researching accurate answers to complicated tax questions can be incredibly time-consuming," she said. "Ask Blue J takes a process that can take days and harnesses artificial intelligence to provide research answers to tax professionals with sources to verify the answers in under a minute" ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20230823880057/en/Introducing-Ask-Blue-J-the-Groundbreaking-Generative-AI-Platform-for-Tax?_gl=1)). The release also described how the work was organised: the legal research team, led by Massey, "works hand-in-glove with experienced data scientists and developers to tune the algorithm to the complexities of tax law" ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20230823880057/en/Introducing-Ask-Blue-J-the-Groundbreaking-Generative-AI-Platform-for-Tax?_gl=1)). That pairing — tax lawyers setting the standard, engineers tuning the system to meet it — is the structure her team still works in.

She also stated the platform's central safeguard in her own words at launch: "To ensure our platform minimizes hallucinations, Ask Blue J is trained to admit when it is not able to formulate an answer supported by authoritative sources" ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20230823880057/en/Introducing-Ask-Blue-J-the-Groundbreaking-Generative-AI-Platform-for-Tax?_gl=1)). A tax answer with no source behind it is, in her framing, an answer the system should refuse to give — a position that follows directly from her thirteen years inside the IRS, where an unsupported position does not survive contact with the Tax Court.

## The expert-in-the-loop method

VentureBeat's November 2025 account of Blue J's rebuild identifies deep human expertise as one of the three pillars that made the platform work, alongside exclusive content licensing and a feedback flywheel from user queries. "Blue J employs tax experts led by Susan Massey, who spent 13 years at the IRS Office of Chief Counsel as Branch Chief for Corporate Tax. Her team constantly tests the AI and refines its performance" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/how-ai-tax-startup-blue-j-torched-its-entire-business-model-for-chatgpt-and)). The team is the reason the platform's answers carry citations: experts write the test questions, determine the correct answers, and use the results to judge whether a model is good enough to ship.

The testing method matters because it is grounded in real practice rather than in benchmarks. Blue J's expert team develops what chief executive [Benjamin Alarie](/people/benjamin-alarie/) calls "ecologically valid" test questions — drawn from actual tax professional queries, with correct answers determined by the expert team — and uses them to evaluate models from every major provider ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/how-ai-tax-startup-blue-j-torched-its-entire-business-model-for-chatgpt-and)). The same questions feed the company's early-access partnership with [OpenAI](/organizations/openai/), in which Blue J trades high-quality feedback on forthcoming models for early access to them ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/how-ai-tax-startup-blue-j-torched-its-entire-business-model-for-chatgpt-and)). Massey's team, in other words, does not only check Blue J's own product: its judgements help shape the underlying models as well.

The results VentureBeat reports for the platform her team guards are striking, though they are company-reported rather than independently audited. The August 2023 launch took 90 seconds to respond, had issues in about half its answers, and registered a Net Promoter Score of 20; by 2025 response times were measured in seconds, the dissatisfaction rate was one in 700 queries, and NPS sat in the mid-80s ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/how-ai-tax-startup-blue-j-torched-its-entire-business-model-for-chatgpt-and)). Weekly active user rates ran between 75% and 85%, against 15% to 25% for traditional platforms, and the company served more than 3,500 organisations ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/how-ai-tax-startup-blue-j-torched-its-entire-business-model-for-chatgpt-and)). Those numbers describe the platform, not Massey personally — but the testing loop she runs is presented as one of the three reasons they moved.

## Two papers on AI and tax research

Massey's published work in 2023 shows both sides of her job: using machine learning to predict outcomes, and stress-testing generative AI before trusting it. The first paper, "Relief of Innocent Spouses — Not So Podlucky", appeared in Tax Notes on 24 February 2023, co-authored with Benjamin Alarie and Christopher Yan ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/relief-innocent-spouses-not-so-podlucky/2023/02/24/7fzml)). It applied Blue J's machine-learning algorithm to *Podlucky*, a Tax Court case about innocent-spouse relief, and showed the model predicting with greater than 95 percent confidence that the facts adopted by the Tax Court would not warrant relief under section 6015 of the Internal Revenue Code ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/relief-innocent-spouses-not-so-podlucky/2023/02/24/7fzml)). The model drew on a dataset of approximately 350 cases that had decided innocent-spouse or equitable relief, and the paper also showed that with favourable inferences about certain facts, the taxpayer's chance of partial relief under section 6015(b)(2) could have risen to 59 percent ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/relief-innocent-spouses-not-so-podlucky/2023/02/24/7fzml)). The Tax Court had denied relief, and the taxpayers' appeal to the Ninth Circuit was dismissed for lack of jurisdiction ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/relief-innocent-spouses-not-so-podlucky/2023/02/24/7fzml)).

The second paper, "The Rise of Generative AI in Tax Research", appeared in Tax Notes Federal on 29 May 2023, co-authored with Alarie, Kim Condon and Christopher Yan ([Tax Notes Federal](https://3838611.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/3838611/tax%20notes%20articles%202023/Rise%20of%20Generative%20AI%20in%20Tax%20Research%20%5BMay%2026%5D.pdf)). The authors put identical tax questions to three systems — [ChatGPT](/organizations/chatgpt/) (GPT-3.5), ChatGPT Plus (GPT-4), and Ask Blue J — priming ChatGPT to answer under US federal income tax law and to provide IRS resources ([Tax Notes Federal](https://3838611.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/3838611/tax%20notes%20articles%202023/Rise%20of%20Generative%20AI%20in%20Tax%20Research%20%5BMay%2026%5D.pdf)). The signature finding was a hallucination: asked "What is section 1292?", ChatGPT confidently described a tax provision that does not exist — there is no section 1292 in the Internal Revenue Code — and supplied a broken link to its supposed text; GPT-4 made the same class of error, describing a nearby provision instead ([Tax Notes Federal](https://3838611.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/3838611/tax%20notes%20articles%202023/Rise%20of%20Generative%20AI%20in%20Tax%20Research%20%5BMay%2026%5D.pdf)). The paper's conclusion was caution: practitioners should use the new tools thoughtfully, because confident tone is no guarantee of accuracy ([Tax Notes Federal](https://3838611.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/3838611/tax%20notes%20articles%202023/Rise%20of%20Generative%20AI%20in%20Tax%20Research%20%5BMay%2026%5D.pdf)). That caution is the same principle Massey stated at the Ask Blue J launch three months later — a system should admit when it cannot support an answer.
