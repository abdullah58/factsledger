## Overview

Robin Li Yanhong is a Chinese software engineer and internet entrepreneur who co-founded [Baidu](/organizations/baidu/) in 2000 and has served as its chairman since inception and its chief executive officer since 2004 ([New Oriental](https://www.neworiental.org/english/who/board/201507/8216532.html); [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). Before Baidu, he invented RankDex, a link-analysis algorithm that ranked websites by how many other sites linked to them — developed in 1996, around the time Stanford students Larry Page and Sergey Brin devised a similar algorithm that became [Google](/organizations/google/) ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). Under Li, Baidu grew from a search startup into [China](/places/china/)'s dominant search engine, then into one of the country's biggest AI companies, built around the ERNIE foundation models and the [Apollo Go](/organizations/apollo-go/) driverless fleet ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)).

Born on 17 November 1968 in Yangquan, Shanxi Province, Li was the fourth of five children of factory workers and the family's only boy ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). His career has been defined by two bets: first, that search organised by link analysis could work in Chinese; second, made two decades later, that Baidu's future lay in AI rather than advertising. The first bet made him a billionaire. The second is still being judged — in 2026, the same year Li unveiled Baidu's agent strategy, he was named personally as a defendant in a securities class action alleging Baidu overstated AI's ability to offset its declining advertising business ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/11/09/3398632/3080/en/levi-korsinsky-reminds-baidu-inc-investors-of-the-pending-class-action-lawsuit-with-a-lead-plaintiff-deadline-of-november-13-2026-bidu.html)).

## From Yangquan to Silicon Valley

Li was a strong student: he entered Yangquan First High School with the second-highest grade on the entrance exam, studied computer programming there, and in 1987 scored among the highest in China on the National Higher Education Entrance Examination ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). He earned a bachelor's degree in information management from Peking University in 1991, then moved to the United States, where he took a master's degree in computer science at the [State University of New York at Buffalo](/universities/state-university-of-new-york-at-buffalo/) ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li); [Buffalo News](https://cedar.Buffalo.EDU/pub_docs/article125.pdf)).

He had planned to continue to a PhD, but in 1994 joined IDD Information Services, a New Jersey division of Dow Jones, where he helped build software for the online edition of *The Wall Street Journal* ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). Search was the problem that held him: how to sort the early web's information automatically. His answer was to rank pages by citations — by how many websites linked to them. The mechanism, named RankDex, was his breakthrough in 1996 and received a US patent in 1999 ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)).

In 1997 Li demonstrated his algorithms at a Silicon Valley computer conference, where William Chang, chief technology officer of the early search firm [Infoseek](/organizations/infoseek/), hired him as a staff engineer ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). He worked at Infoseek from July 1997 to December 1999, growing frustrated with the company's lacklustre interest in innovative search techniques ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li); [Buffalo News](https://cedar.Buffalo.EDU/pub_docs/article125.pdf)). The turn toward founding his own company came in 1998, when Eric Xu — a biochemist and sales representative for a US biotech company who was filming a documentary on American innovation — introduced Li to Yahoo! co-founder Jerry Yang ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). Inspired by Yang's success, Li and Xu resolved to build a major internet company of their own.

## Building Baidu — and the advertising controversies

Li and Xu started Baidu in a Beijing hotel room in 2000, armed with $1.2 million from the US venture firms Integrity Partners and Peninsula Capital; Draper Fisher Jurvetson and IDG Technology Ventures added another $10 million the same year ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). Baidu's search engine launched in 2001, its name taken from an roughly eight-hundred-year-old Song Dynasty poem and meaning "hundreds of times" ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). Li served as president from February 2000 to December 2003 before becoming CEO ([New Oriental](https://www.neworiental.org/english/who/board/201507/8216532.html)).

Baidu's rise was fast and, for a time, unchallenged at home. Google bought a 2.6% stake in 2004 but never became the threat many expected — EBSCO's biography credits Baidu's better handling of Chinese-language parsing and Google's own difficulties in China, including email hacking at its Chinese headquarters ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li); [Buffalo News](https://cedar.Buffalo.EDU/pub_docs/article125.pdf)). Baidu listed on Nasdaq on 5 August 2005, its shares jumping from $27 to $122 on the first day, the biggest one-day IPO gain since the dot-com boom; in 2007 it became the first Chinese company included in the NASDAQ-100 Index ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)).

Success brought scrutiny of how Baidu made its money. The company complied with Chinese government search restrictions, including blocking pornography and pages on politically sensitive topics, and was accused of penalising the search rankings of websites that cut their advertising spending and of internet piracy — allegations Li disputed ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). In 2008, state broadcaster CCTV aired programmes alleging Baidu earned millions by advertising unlicensed medical practitioners; the negative coverage stopped after Baidu increased its advertising spending with CCTV, according to EBSCO's biography ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)).

The advertising model produced its gravest crisis in 2016. College student Wei Zexi, 21, died on 12 April 2016 of a rare cancer, synovial sarcoma, after pursuing an experimental treatment at a Beijing hospital run by the armed police — a treatment he had found through a paid Baidu promotion ([NPR](https://www.publicradioeast.org/2016-05-03/china-opens-investigation-into-search-engine-baidu-after-students-death)). Before his death Wei accused Baidu of cheating him, and Chinese health and internet authorities opened an investigation amid public fury; Baidu's shares fell nearly 8% on Nasdaq in a day ([NPR](https://www.publicradioeast.org/2016-05-03/china-opens-investigation-into-search-engine-baidu-after-students-death)). Regulators summoned Li for questioning ([Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2016-05-ceo-china-baidu-summoned-student.amp)), and in a staff letter seen by Reuters he called the episode a crisis "greater than in any crisis Baidu has experienced before", warning: "If we lose the support of users, we lose hold of our values, and Baidu will truly go bankrupt in 30 days!" ([PRWeek](https://www.prweek.com/article/1394176/baidu-scandal-ceo-calls-company-win-back-consumers-greatest-crisis)). The Cyberspace Administration of China found Baidu had given "too much weight" to paid advertising and that "commercial promotions were not clearly marked", ordering cuts to paid healthcare advertising, fewer paid results and a ranking overhaul so results were not determined by fees paid; Baidu said it would implement all the changes ([PRWeek](https://www.prweek.com/article/1394176/baidu-scandal-ceo-calls-company-win-back-consumers-greatest-crisis)).

## The AI pivot — and the 2026 class action

Li had been interested in AI since the early 2000s and invested billions in AI research, and from 2023 his public profile centred on Baidu's generative-AI push ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). After Chinese regulators cleared the first batch of consumer chatbots, Baidu released its ERNIE chatbot publicly on 31 August 2023; Li presented the broad rollout as a way to gather large-scale human feedback to improve the model ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). That year TIME named him to its list of the 100 Most Influential People in AI ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). ERNIE's users grew from 100 million in late 2023 to 300 million by mid-2024, alongside rising enterprise adoption ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)).

Facing tighter US export controls in 2025, Li argued Baidu's AI roadmap would withstand semiconductor constraints by leaning on domestic supply and scaling its AI cloud, and signalled plans to open-source newer ERNIE models and make ERNIE free for consumers ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). In March 2026 Baidu launched DuMate, a general-purpose agent for everyday productivity, and in May 2026 it released ERNIE 5.1 with stronger reasoning and a more compact model size ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). Apollo Go, the driverless ride-hailing service, expanded to 28 cities globally by 2026, with fully driverless commercial operations in [Dubai](/places/dubai/) and open-road testing in London and Switzerland ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)).

At [Baidu Create](/events/baidu-create/) 2026 in Beijing, Li framed the industry's shift from competing on model scale to competing on task execution, arguing that "for the first time, what really made AI go viral was not the model, but the application" ([TechNode](https://technode.com/2026/05/14/baidu-create-2026-ceo-says-ai-is-moving-from-model-competition-to-ai-agent-era-foresees-rise-of-super-individuals/)). He proposed "Daily Active Agents" as the agent era's defining metric — the equivalent of the mobile internet's daily active users — and predicted global daily active agents could exceed 10 billion ([TechNode](https://technode.com/2026/05/14/baidu-create-2026-ceo-says-ai-is-moving-from-model-competition-to-ai-agent-era-foresees-rise-of-super-individuals/)).

The same year tested the AI story in court. A securities class action filed in the US District Court for the Southern District of New York names Li as an individual defendant, alleging Baidu made materially false and misleading statements between 18 November 2025 and 17 August 2026 by overstating AI's ability to offset its declining advertising business; Baidu's American Depositary Shares fell 12.73% to $90.87 on 18 August 2026, and the window to apply for lead plaintiff closed on 13 November 2026 ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/11/09/3398632/3080/en/levi-korsinsky-reminds-baidu-inc-investors-of-the-pending-class-action-lawsuit-with-a-lead-plaintiff-deadline-of-november-13-2026-bidu.html)). The claims are allegations, not findings, and the profile states them as such. Forbes, which had estimated Li's net worth at $5.5 billion in May 2025, ranked him 95th in China at $4.9 billion in its November 2026 list ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li); [Caproasia](https://www.caproasia.com/2026/11/07/forbes-top-100-richest-in-china-in-2026-1-3-trillion-net-worth-13-billion-average-net-worth-4-8-billion-net-worth-required-to-be-in-top-100-richest-in-china-top-10-richest-are-zhang-yiming-by/)).

Beyond Baidu, Li has served as an independent director of New Oriental Education & Technology Group since September 2006, and in 2014 UN Secretary-General Ban Ki-moon appointed him co-chair of the Independent Expert Advisory Group on the Data Revolution for Sustainable Development ([New Oriental](https://www.neworiental.org/english/who/board/201507/8216532.html); [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). He met his wife, Dongmin (Melissa) Ma, in 1995; they married on 10 October that year, have four children and live in Beijing ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)).

The profile sticks to Li's documented public and professional record and reaches for no personal details beyond what its sources cover.
