## Overview

Howie Liu is the co-founder and chief executive of [Airtable](/organizations/airtable/), the no-code platform that combines a spreadsheet's familiarity with a relational database's power so non-programmers can build their own business software ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). He built it unusually slowly — three years from first work to public launch — took it to a valuation above $11 billion in 2021, rebuilt it for the AI-agent era in 2025–2026, and sold it to [Bending Spoons](/organizations/bending-spoons/) for $1.28 billion in cash in 2026 ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/04/bending-spoons-to-buy-airtable-for-1-28b/)).

## Early life and education

Liu grew up in College Station, Texas, the son of Korean parents who had been raised in China ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). His father earned a biochemistry PhD at Texas A&M, while his mother, an engineer in China, worked minimum-wage jobs in the United States, including at McDonald's and as a seamstress ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). She gave him books on Steve Jobs and Bill Gates, and Liu later said he probably read four biographies of Gates ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)).

At 13, Liu taught himself to code in C++ after finding an unread training book in his father's office ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). At 16 he went to [Duke University](/universities/duke-university/), graduating in 2009 with a degree in mechanical engineering and public policy ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). He landed a software development job in San Jose at Accenture, at a salary higher than his parents' combined income, but never started: the night before his start date he decided instead to try a startup, saying he had no financial resources to fall back on ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)).

## Etacts, Salesforce, and the long road to Airtable

Liu's first company was Etacts, a four-person venture that aggregated messages from email, Facebook and Twitter ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). In 2010 it won a place in [Y Combinator](/organizations/y-combinator/), and later that year Liu sold it to [Salesforce](/organizations/salesforce/), netting what Forbes described as a million-dollar payday and a job building a chat product ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). At Salesforce he liked the people and the salary but felt the pull to start something new again, and he left in 2012 ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)).

What followed was deliberately unhurried. Liu travelled, read philosophy and design theory, and experimented with colour theory through painting and sculpture ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). Meanwhile Andrew Ofstad, a Duke classmate on sabbatical from a project manager job at Google, began working with him on small programs ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). Airtable took shape in 2012, was founded in 2013, and raised $3 million from Caffeinated Capital and Freestyle Capital in March 2013 ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). The Stanford Daily agrees on the timeline: Liu founded Airtable in early 2013, and he and co-founders Ofstad and Emmett Nicholas spent three years building it before its 2015 launch ([The Stanford Daily](https://stanforddaily.com/2021/04/29/airtable-co-founder-tells-young-entrepreneurs-to-be-patient-stay-true-to-vision/)).

Liu's explanation for the pace became his management signature. "Instead of trying to rush a new product out the door, we introduce a period of forced delay, so people have a chance to sleep on an idea," he said, calling the practice "the simmer" ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). He told a Stanford audience the same lesson: establish a clear vision early, build prototypes, and gather customer feedback ([The Stanford Daily](https://stanforddaily.com/2021/04/29/airtable-co-founder-tells-young-entrepreneurs-to-be-patient-stay-true-to-vision/)). In 2012 the founders had already written an internal vision deck describing a five-plus-year roadmap ([The Stanford Daily](https://stanforddaily.com/2021/04/29/airtable-co-founder-tells-young-entrepreneurs-to-be-patient-stay-true-to-vision/)).

## From public launch to unicorn scale

Airtable launched its first database to the public in the winter of 2015, with fewer than ten employees, and collected another $8 million in funding a few months later ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). The product was a collaborative spreadsheet that could store images, documents, videos and URLs in cells, with a relational database underneath; its "blocks" let users overlay data on maps, apply formulas, and send alerts, so anyone could assemble sales pipelines and editorial calendars that previously required code writers ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)).

By 2018 the slow build was paying off. Airtable had taken hold in 80,000 organisations, from [Netflix](/organizations/netflix/) — which used it to run its post-production pipeline — to small non-profits, and revenue was on track to jump 400 percent to $20 million, mostly on word of mouth ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). Investors followed: in March 2018 Airtable raised $59 million from CRV, Caffeinated Capital and Slow Ventures, and later that year another $100 million from Benchmark, Thrive Capital and Coatue Management at a post-money valuation of $1.1 billion ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)). Liu, whose equity stake had fallen to 10 percent by then, framed the dilution as buying insurance: capital in the bank let the company prioritise ideas that might not pay out for years ([Forbes](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/airtable-move-slow-and-make-things/52579/1)).

By the 2021 peak Airtable was valued at over $11 billion, having raised more than $1.4 billion across funding rounds ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/04/bending-spoons-to-buy-airtable-for-1-28b/)). Earlier in 2026, its shares were trading on secondary markets at about a $4 billion valuation ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/04/bending-spoons-to-buy-airtable-for-1-28b/)).

## The AI refounding and the 2026 sale

Liu's answer to the AI wave was to rebuild rather than bolt on features. In June 2025 Airtable relaunched itself as an AI-native app platform, calling the moment a "refounding": its new Omni agent builds apps conversationally from Airtable's existing production-tested components — in Liu's phrase, turning Airtable into "a powerful parts bin" — with AI capabilities on every plan, including the free plan ([Maginative](https://www.maginative.com/article/airtable-bets-big-on-ai-agents-with-omni-reboots-as-an-ai-native-app-platform/)). In January 2026 the company unveiled Superagent, an orchestration platform that lets users spin up a team of AI agents to do tasks ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/04/bending-spoons-to-buy-airtable-for-1-28b/)).

The sale followed within months. On 4 August 2026 Bending Spoons, in its first acquisition since going public at an $18 billion valuation in July, agreed to buy Airtable for $1.28 billion in cash ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/04/bending-spoons-to-buy-airtable-for-1-28b/)). With Airtable's net cash, the equity value was about $2.25 billion, against annual recurring revenue of approximately $480 million, growing over 20 percent year on year as of June 2026, from more than 500,000 organisations including 80 percent of the Fortune 100 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/04/bending-spoons-to-buy-airtable-for-1-28b/)). Liu framed the deal as giving Airtable "the resources and the long-term commitment" to build the AI-native platform of the future ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/04/bending-spoons-to-buy-airtable-for-1-28b/)). Bending Spoons completed the acquisition in September 2026, acquiring 100 percent of Airtable's shares in an all-cash transaction ([Business Wire](https://www.advfn.com/stock-market/NASDAQ/BSP/stock-news/99253854/bending-spoons-completes-the-acquisition-of-airtab)).
