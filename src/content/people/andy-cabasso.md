## Overview

Andy Cabasso is a growth operations manager at [ClickUp](/organizations/clickup/) who built 37 AI agents to do his own job — and became, in the spring of 2026, the human face of his employer's bet that a smaller workforce orchestrating AI could do more than a larger one doing the work directly ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-clickup-cuts-staff-ai-restructuring/)). Based in [New York City](/places/new-york-city/), he uses 15 to 20 of the agents daily and the rest as needed, spending most of his day reviewing their outputs and refining their prompts rather than pulling reports or building tables ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)).

Cabasso did not arrive at that role through engineering. He used to practise law, grew disenchanted with billable hours — getting faster at the work did not benefit him — and built a legal marketing agency instead, sold it for seven figures, and co-founded an email-outreach software company before joining ClickUp ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/); [Built to Sell](https://builttosell.com/radio/episode-305/)). He still cannot code, by his own account. The agents are the point: a non-technical operator who turned himself into a manager of systems that do not sleep.

## From law to growth operations

The agency began while Cabasso was in law school, applying for jobs at firms whose websites he found embarrassingly bad — stale copyright dates, disconnected messaging — and realising the firms would pay someone to fix that for them ([Mixergy](https://mixergy.com/interviews/jurispage-with-andy-cabasso/)). With a co-founder who had been a freelance designer, he first built Offsprout around 2011–2012, a drag-and-drop visual builder for WordPress, and grew a web-design agency out of it to bring revenue in early ([Mixergy](https://mixergy.com/interviews/jurispage-with-andy-cabasso/)). The agency narrowed to law firms and became JurisPage, co-founded in 2013; three years later it held service contracts with more than 200 law firms when Uptime Legal, an Inc. 5000 company selling technology and practice-management software to law firms, acquired it in a seven-figure deal that closed in 2016 ([Built to Sell](https://builttosell.com/radio/episode-305/)). Cabasso confirmed the seven-figure exit himself on Mixergy but said the agreements barred him from disclosing revenue figures ([Mixergy](https://mixergy.com/interviews/jurispage-with-andy-cabasso/)).

The next company came out of the agency's own bottleneck. Cabasso and his co-founder — his college roommate, with whom he had built the agency — kept hitting the same problem for clients: ranking better in search required links from other sites, and link building meant slow, manual outreach across many disconnected tools. So they built Postaga, an all-in-one email-outreach platform for link building, digital PR and sales outreach ([Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/stories/postaga)). It launched on Product Hunt in May 2020, went number one for the day and number two for the week, and took 1,000 sign-ups in its first week. Bootstrapped at first from the JurisPage sale proceeds, it joined the TinySeed accelerator in May 2021, and by mid-2022 had 20,000 users and a nine-person fully remote team ([Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/stories/postaga)).

ClickUp came later. Cabasso had joined the company more than a year before his April 2026 Business Insider essay, which puts his start in or before early 2025; no exact date was verified ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)). ClickUp's own blog author page describes his job as driving "operational excellence" by optimising processes, analysing data and keeping marketing, sales and revenue teams aligned, and says his work has been featured in Ahrefs, Search Engine Journal, Mixergy, Product Hunt and Indie Hackers ([ClickUp](https://clickup.com/blog/author/andy-cabasso/)).

## The 37 agents

Cabasso's agent fleet started small, with the tedious tasks he wanted off his plate. The first agent he built was a scheduling agent that looked at his calendar and task list and planned out his day ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)). It snowballed from there: one agent pulls analytics from the outbound platform, proactively compares campaigns against each other, recommends changes and implements them; another reviews meeting transcripts; a third creates follow-up tasks. A year before the essay he had spent much of his time pulling reports and building tables; by April 2026 the agents did that work and were waiting for him when he sat down in the morning ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)).

He built all of it without writing code, using ClickUp's Super Agent Builder, and describes himself as "the most technical non-technical person" on his team — someone who understands how databases are structured, how APIs connect and how data flows between tools from years working alongside technical co-founders and building marketing automation ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)). The role, as he describes it, has shape-shifted rather than disappeared: "I spend most of my day orchestrating agents, reviewing their outputs, and giving further directions… managing a team that doesn't sleep" ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)).

The most widely quoted detail is the personalities. Default AI outputs, he found, can be bland or sycophantic — tell some models he plans to jump the Grand Canyon on a bicycle and they will cheer him on — so he experimented with giving agents characters. His first test was an agent that calls him "Captain" and believes they are on the USS Enterprise from Star Trek. An "Atomic Habits" agent carries the personality of Matt Foley, the Saturday Night Live motivational-speaker character, and a vendor-negotiation agent carries the personality of FBI hostage negotiator Chris Voss ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)). Not every experiment survived: a Yoda-personality agent was discontinued because the phrasing wore thin, and he deliberately gives analytics-focused agents no personality at all, so it cannot interfere with the work ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)). His own rule is to model an agent on a real personality only where that person's knowledge is relevant to the task.

## The systems behind the numbers

The 37 agents were not an abstract headcount claim; they showed up in two concrete ClickUp systems with numbers attached. The first is the AI call-prep system Cabasso broke down for the Outbound Kitchen newsletter in February 2026. ClickUp's reps use Nooks, a parallel dialer that lets them call six prospects at once, and when someone picks up there are zero seconds to prepare — before the system, reps juggled Salesforce, LinkedIn, Pocus, Outreach and several dashboards mid-conversation ([Outbound Kitchen](https://newsletter.outbound.kitchen/p/clickup-built-this-before-scaling)). The Growth Ops team built a Retool workflow that fires on a webhook whenever a prospect enters a sales sequence, pulls CRM, activity, sequence and product-usage data from the warehouse with SOQL and SQL queries, enriches gaps, then runs three AI models in parallel — Perplexity for live company news, OpenAI's GPT for context and activity summary, Gemini for the talk track — and writes the result back into the CRM, synced to the dialer ([Outbound Kitchen](https://newsletter.outbound.kitchen/p/clickup-built-this-before-scaling)).

What made it stick, in Cabasso's account, was not the architecture but the failure modes and the feedback loop. The outputs were unusable on day one, and three things kept breaking: bad data queries, poor prompts, and misalignment between what the system delivered and what reps needed in the moment. His advice has become the piece's most quoted line: "if the AI output is wrong, check your SOQL and SQL first. The data is usually the problem" ([Outbound Kitchen](https://newsletter.outbound.kitchen/p/clickup-built-this-before-scaling)). Reps flagged wrong talk tracks, stale data and useless formats, and every iteration compounded; call volume went from about 35 calls a day to about 150, with a top performer booking 200 meetings in a quarter ([Outbound Kitchen](https://newsletter.outbound.kitchen/p/clickup-built-this-before-scaling)).

The second system is "Plug the Dam," featured on ClickUp's own Inspiration page: Cabasso mapped 17 pipeline leaks and built agents and automations that catch missed follow-ups, stale opportunities and no-shows, recovering $437K in pipeline ([ClickUp](https://clickup.com/clickup-inspiration)). The same page lists him as "Principal, Growth Operations," a title that differs from the "growth operations manager" used in the April–May 2026 press coverage — most likely a promotion, though the page is undated and the change is not verified.

Both systems landed in the middle of ClickUp's public AI restructuring. In May 2026 the company cut 22% of its workforce while deploying roughly 3,000 internal AI agents, with chief executive [Zeb Evans](/people/zeb-evans/) framing the move as building a "100x org" and promising million-dollar salary bands for employees who create outsized impact using AI ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-clickup-cuts-staff-ai-restructuring/)). Cabasso — one manager, 37 agents — was the example the coverage kept returning to. Whether that model proves out is, as TechRepublic noted, still an open question: cutting headcount lowers costs quickly, but does not by itself demonstrate that AI improved work quality or customer experience ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-clickup-cuts-staff-ai-restructuring/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Who is Andy Cabasso?** He is a growth operations manager at ClickUp, based in New York, who built 37 AI agents for his own work. Before ClickUp he practised law, co-founded and sold the law-firm marketing agency JurisPage, and co-founded the email-outreach platform Postaga ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/); [Built to Sell](https://builttosell.com/radio/episode-305/)).

**How many AI agents does Andy Cabasso oversee?** Thirty-seven, as he told Business Insider in April 2026; he uses 15 to 20 of them day to day and the rest as needed. TechRepublic independently confirmed the figure a month later in its coverage of ClickUp's layoffs ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-clickup-cuts-staff-ai-restructuring/)).

**What do his AI agents actually do?** They pull outbound campaign analytics and implement changes, review meeting transcripts, create follow-up tasks, and plan his day from his calendar and task list. Some carry personalities — a Star Trek "Captain" agent, a Matt Foley habits agent, a Chris Voss negotiation agent — while analytics agents get none ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)).

**What did he build before ClickUp?** JurisPage, a marketing agency for law firms co-founded in 2013 and sold to Uptime Legal in a seven-figure deal in 2016, and Postaga, an outreach platform that reached 20,000 users by mid-2022 ([Built to Sell](https://builttosell.com/radio/episode-305/); [Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/stories/postaga)).

**Can he code?** No — by his own account, nobody would pay him to code. He built the agents with ClickUp's no-code Super Agent Builder, drawing on years of working alongside technical co-founders and building marketing automation systems ([Business Insider](https://www.europesays.com/ai/21304/)).
