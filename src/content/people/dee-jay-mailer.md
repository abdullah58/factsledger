## Overview

Dee Jay Mailer is a Hawaiʻi healthcare and global-health executive who served as chief executive officer of [Kamehameha Schools](/schools/kamehameha-schools/) from January 19, 2004, until her retirement in 2014 — a ten-year tenure that followed a long healthcare career and a spell running the Geneva operations of a multibillion-dollar global health fund ([Kamehameha Schools](https://www.ksbe.edu/archives/faqs/executives-of-kamehameha-schools)). A 1970 graduate of Kamehameha Schools herself, Mailer earned a B.S. in Nursing in 1975 and an MBA in 1985, both from the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa ([Mālamalama](http://www.hawaii.edu/malamalama/2009/03/distinguished-alumni-award/)). She is a public figure in Hawaiʻi's education and civic life: in 2009 the University of Hawaiʻi honored her with its Distinguished Alumni Award, given to graduates who used their UH education to excel professionally and serve the community ([Mālamalama](http://www.hawaii.edu/malamalama/2009/03/distinguished-alumni-award/)).

## From nursing to the Global Fund

Mailer's healthcare career ran through some of Hawaiʻi's and the mainland's largest health organizations before it turned global. In 1995 she was appointed chief executive officer of Kaiser Permanente Hawaiʻi at a time when the managed-care industry was being reshaped by cost pressures, mergers, and new legislation — and colleagues credited her with bringing calm to the organization during the turbulence ([Honolulu Star-Bulletin](http://archives.starbulletin.com/2004/01/04/news/story4.html)). "She came at a time when there was a lot of changes and upheaval in the health care industry and Kaiser," recalled Kaiser's public-relations director Chris Pablo. "Dee Jay brought a lot of calm and peace to the organization" ([Honolulu Star-Bulletin](http://archives.starbulletin.com/2004/01/04/news/story4.html)).

She then became chief administrative officer of Health Net Inc., a California-based health plan, and moved on to Geneva as chief operating officer of the Global Fund, a Swiss trust set up to fight AIDS, tuberculosis and malaria in developing countries ([Honolulu Star-Bulletin](http://archives.starbulletin.com/2004/01/04/news/story4.html)). The move also had a family logic: both of her daughters had attended Kamehameha Schools, and by the end of 2003 she was preparing to come home ([Honolulu Star-Bulletin](http://archives.starbulletin.com/2004/01/04/news/story4.html)).

## A CEO for a trust in crisis

When the $6 billion Kamehameha Schools trust chose Mailer in December 2003, it was looking for a healer in more than name. She was picked from a pool of 200 applicants after 11 hours of interviews ([Mālamalama](http://www.hawaii.edu/malamalama/2004/05/f8_ks.html)). The position had been vacant since May, when her predecessor Hamilton McCubbin abruptly resigned from his $350,000-a-year post following an internal investigation into an alleged improper relationship with a female employee, and the trust was also facing a lawsuit from a non-Hawaiian student challenging its century-old policy limiting admission to native Hawaiian children ([Honolulu Star-Bulletin](http://archives.starbulletin.com/2004/01/04/news/story4.html)). She assumed the role on January 19, 2004 ([Kamehameha Schools](https://www.ksbe.edu/archives/faqs/executives-of-kamehameha-schools)).

A year in, the local press was already writing about how thoroughly she had settled the waters. MidWeek's March 2005 cover story, headlined "All Hands On Deck At Kamehameha," noted that even with her honeymoon period over, people kept singing her praises: she was the kind of chief executive who fetched coffee for her own staff, held morning meetings at Zippy's rather than Honolulu's exclusive clubs, and got down into the loʻi as she learned Hawaiʻi's heritage more deeply ([MidWeek](https://archives-new.midweek.com/content/story/midweek_coverstory/all_hands_on_deck_at_kamehameha/)). Trustee chairwoman Diane Plotts said Mailer had "built a lot of trust" by being "very straight, very fair and very honest," and marveled at her speed: "Her grasp of it all in just over a year is just unbelievable" ([MidWeek](https://archives-new.midweek.com/content/story/midweek_coverstory/all_hands_on_deck_at_kamehameha/)). The job's scope was enormous — from the education and outreach mission to managing the trust's vast land and investment assets — and Plotts summed it up: "She's got the whole ball of wax" ([MidWeek](https://archives-new.midweek.com/content/story/midweek_coverstory/all_hands_on_deck_at_kamehameha/)).

Mailer's own framing of the work was service-oriented from the start. In an early interview she put it in customer terms: "Whether your customer is a little kindergartener just entering school or a high school senior about to graduate, we are here to serve Hawaiian children with opportunities for education" ([Honolulu Magazine](https://www.honolulumagazine.com/q-a-dee-jay-mailer/)). Her stated priorities on taking the job included strengthening the school's endowment, extending its educational reach to Hawaiian children beyond its campuses, and connecting students with alumni mentors ([Honolulu Magazine](https://www.honolulumagazine.com/q-a-dee-jay-mailer/)).

## Early learning and the decade that followed

One of the most concrete records of Mailer's tenure comes from her own advocacy at the state capitol. Testifying before the Hawaiʻi Senate Committee on Education on February 11, 2011 in support of SB 931, a bill funding the Early Learning Council, she described a school system that by then served 5,400 K-12 students across Oʻahu, Hawaiʻi island and Maui plus 1,317 three- and four-year-olds at 31 preschool sites statewide — with more than 11,000 children having received their first preschool experience through the Early Childhood Education division ([Hawaiʻi State Senate](https://data.capitol.hawaii.gov/sessions/session2012/testimony/SB931_TESTIMONY_EDU_02-11-11.pdf)). Early childhood education, she wrote, was "the cornerstone" of the Education Strategic Plan the trust had adopted in 2005, which sought long-term change in how Native Hawaiian families approached learning ([Hawaiʻi State Senate](https://data.capitol.hawaii.gov/sessions/session2012/testimony/SB931_TESTIMONY_EDU_02-11-11.pdf)).

Her decade ended with a send-off that matched its tone. On February 11, 2014, the KS Maui ʻohana surrounded the soon-to-retire CEO at the campus's annual Pōʻalima ʻUlaʻula (Red Friday), a day of Hawaiian unity: more than 1,200 employees and students filled the Kaʻulaheanuiokamoku Gymnasium to honor her ten years of service, K-through-12 students learned the hula "ʻAuʻa ʻIa" as a farewell gift, and the gathering closed with one last makana — the Electric Slide, which Mailer joyously joined ([Kamehameha Schools](https://www.ksbe.edu/news/photos/aa-hui-hou-dee-jay-ks-maui)). Kahu Kalani Wong led the campus in prayer for Mailer and her husband Donny as they began the next chapter together ([Kamehameha Schools](https://www.ksbe.edu/news/photos/aa-hui-hou-dee-jay-ks-maui)). The school's official executive roster records her tenure as January 19, 2004 to 2014, with Livingston "Jack" Wong succeeding her ([Kamehameha Schools](https://www.ksbe.edu/archives/faqs/executives-of-kamehameha-schools)).
