## Overview

Invitation Homes is the largest owner of single-family rental homes in the [United States](/places/united-states/): a Dallas-based real estate investment trust, founded by [Blackstone](/organizations/blackstone/) in 2012, that owned 86,192 rental homes across 16 core markets at the end of 2025. It began by buying foreclosed houses after the 2008 crisis, invented the single-family-rental securitization, went public in 2017, and absorbed Starwood Waypoint Homes — then, in 2026, a new federal law barred it from buying more existing homes ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/2124697328/nations-largest-single-family-rental-landlord-changes-tack); [D Magazine](https://www.dmagazine.com/publications/d-ceo/2018/april/invitation-homes-rental-dallas/)).

The company trades on the New York Stock Exchange as INVH and earned $2.7 billion in revenue in 2025 ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/2124697328/nations-largest-single-family-rental-landlord-changes-tack)). Counting homes it owns outright, holds in joint ventures and manages for others, it touches more than 110,000 single-family rental properties ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/2124697328/nations-largest-single-family-rental-landlord-changes-tack)). Its typical home is a three-bedroom, two-bathroom house in a desirable suburb, and its typical resident is about 39 years old, stays about three years and chooses the home for its school district and commute ([Nareit](https://www.reit.com/news/reit-magazine/september-october-2019/single-family-home-rental-leader-invitation-homes-sees)). This profile covers that company — not Blackstone itself, and not the smaller landlords that followed its model.

## Buying the foreclosure crisis — and inventing an asset class

### The founding bet

Invitation Homes began as a bet that the 2008 foreclosure crisis had created a new business: buy distressed single-family houses cheaply, renovate them to a standard specification, and rent them at scale. Blackstone supplied the capital and Dallas Tanner — who had built a 3,000-unit Phoenix rental business through his Treehouse Group — supplied the operating know-how ([Nareit](https://www.reit.com/news/reit-magazine/september-october-2019/single-family-home-rental-leader-invitation-homes-sees)). Tanner's pitch was that single-family renting could be professionalised the way apartments had been in the 1970s and 1980s: "There are 15 million people that rent single-family homes. Why not do it professionally?" ([Nareit](https://www.reit.com/news/reit-magazine/september-october-2019/single-family-home-rental-leader-invitation-homes-sees)). The partners started buying homes with Blackstone in April 2012, and over the first five years built the company home by home to 50,000 homes in 13 markets, without a big acquisition ([Nareit](https://www.reit.com/news/reit-magazine/september-october-2019/single-family-home-rental-leader-invitation-homes-sees)). By the time it went public, Blackstone had invested somewhere between $9.6 and $13 billion in the venture ([D Magazine](https://www.dmagazine.com/publications/d-ceo/2018/april/invitation-homes-rental-dallas/)).

### The securitization and the IPO

The financing innovation mattered as much as the buying. In fall 2013 Blackstone issued the first single-family rental securitization, Invitation Homes 2013-SFR1, pooling 3,207 homes in Arizona, California, Florida, Georgia and Illinois into a $479 million bond arranged by Deutsche Bank, secured by first-priority mortgages and rated triple-A ([Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/real-estate-research-7198/single-family-rental-securitizations-stay-656460/fulltext)). The collateral was valued at $638 million against $479 million of loans — a 25 percent advance rate ([Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/real-estate-research-7198/single-family-rental-securitizations-stay-656460/fulltext)). Then came the public market: in February 2017 Invitation Homes went public, raising $1.77 billion in what D Magazine calls the second-largest REIT offering in U.S. history, with Fannie Mae agreeing to backstop up to $1 billion of its debt — a first for an institutional single-family landlord, as reported by the [Wall Street Journal](/organizations/wall-street-journal/) ([D Magazine](https://www.dmagazine.com/publications/d-ceo/2018/april/invitation-homes-rental-dallas/); [HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/39026-blackstones-invitation-homes-prepares-15-billion-ipo/)).

## The Starwood merger and Blackstone's $7 billion exit

### The merger

Within six months of the IPO, Invitation Homes doubled in size. Its November 2017 merger with Starwood Waypoint Homes — announced that August as a stock-for-stock merger of equals — combined roughly 50,000 Invitation homes with about 32,000 Starwood Waypoint homes into an 82,000-home portfolio, with expected annual cost synergies of $45 million to $50 million ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/41839-invitation-homes-starwood-waypoint-homes-merge-to-create-largest-single-family-landlord/)). Starwood Waypoint was itself a product of earlier consolidation: Starwood Waypoint Residential Trust had merged with Colony American Homes two years earlier to form Colony Starwood Homes ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/41839-invitation-homes-starwood-waypoint-homes-merge-to-create-largest-single-family-landlord/)). Fred Tuomi, the former Starwood Waypoint chief executive who had spent more than 20 years at [Equity Residential](/organizations/equity-residential/), became chief executive of the combined company, with Dallas Tanner remaining chief investment officer and the headquarters staying in Dallas ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/41839-invitation-homes-starwood-waypoint-homes-merge-to-create-largest-single-family-landlord/); [D Magazine](https://www.dmagazine.com/publications/d-ceo/2018/april/invitation-homes-rental-dallas/)).

### Blackstone's exit

By 2019 the company owned about 80,000 homes across 17 markets at 96.5% occupancy, with a market capitalisation of about $16 billion, and its residents stayed an average of three years — almost twice the multifamily average ([Nareit](https://www.reit.com/news/reit-magazine/september-october-2019/single-family-home-rental-leader-invitation-homes-sees)). That November, Blackstone sold its remaining stake — about 11% of the company — for $1.7 billion, taking its total profit from the venture to about $7 billion, more than twice what it invested, according to the Wall Street Journal ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/other/blackstone-profitably-exits-single-family-investment-which-is-still-growing-101937)). Investors had proven willing buyers: the shares had risen from $20.80 at the start of 2019 to $29.50 by mid-November ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/other/blackstone-profitably-exits-single-family-investment-which-is-still-growing-101937)). "The hardest part wasn't buying the homes, it was building the business," Blackstone president Jonathan Gray told the Journal ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/other/blackstone-profitably-exits-single-family-investment-which-is-still-growing-101937)).

## Fees, the FTC case and the 2026 purchase ban

### The FTC case

Scale brought scrutiny. In 2018 [Reuters](/organizations/reuters/) reported tenant dissatisfaction with property management and fees, a characterisation the company disputed ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/other/blackstone-profitably-exits-single-family-investment-which-is-still-growing-101937)). The fees were material: tenants could subscribe to integrated smart-home features for about $18 more a month, with 70 to 80 percent of residents subscribing in some markets ([D Magazine](https://www.dmagazine.com/publications/d-ceo/2018/april/invitation-homes-rental-dallas/)). In September 2024 the Federal Trade Commission sued, alleging the company deceived applicants about lease costs, charged renters undisclosed fees for services such as smart-home technology and utility management, failed to inspect homes before move-in, and unfairly withheld security deposits ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-sends-checks-totaling-more-472-million-consumers-deceived-invitation-homes-undisclosed-fees)). The company settled under an order requiring it to turn over $48 million, disclose leasing prices clearly and handle deposit refunds fairly ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-sends-checks-totaling-more-472-million-consumers-deceived-invitation-homes-undisclosed-fees)).

### The 2026 purchase ban

The money reached renters in March 2026, when the FTC sent checks totalling more than $47.2 million to 444,131 consumers who had paid $45 or more in covered fees from January 2021 to September 2024 ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-sends-checks-totaling-more-472-million-consumers-deceived-invitation-homes-undisclosed-fees)). Four months later the business model itself was capped: the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law on 11 July 2026, and from 7 January 2027 its Section 1001 bars large institutional investors — entities controlling 350 or more single-family homes — from buying more of them, with exceptions for newly built homes and build-to-rent programmes ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/congress-passes-21st-century-road-to-housing-act); [Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/admin/upload/SiteAttachments/21st-Century-ROAD-to-Housing-Act-Becomes-Law.pdf)). The Act was co-sponsored by Senator Tim Scott and Senator [Elizabeth Warren](/people/elizabeth-warren/) and passed the Senate 85–5 ([Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/admin/upload/SiteAttachments/21st-Century-ROAD-to-Housing-Act-Becomes-Law.pdf)). At December 2025 Invitation Homes owned 86,192 homes — more than any other single-family landlord — which means the purchase ban lands hardest on the company that built the model ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/2124697328/nations-largest-single-family-rental-landlord-changes-tack)). It had already begun adapting: after being a net buyer in 2025, it plans to be a net seller in 2026, funding buybacks from sales, and in January 2026 it bought build-to-rent developer ResiBuilt for $89 million ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/2124697328/nations-largest-single-family-rental-landlord-changes-tack)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Who founded Invitation Homes?** Blackstone founded Invitation Homes in 2012 with Dallas Tanner, whose Phoenix-based Treehouse Group had about 3,000 rental units before the deal ([Nareit](https://www.reit.com/news/reit-magazine/september-october-2019/single-family-home-rental-leader-invitation-homes-sees)). The partners bought their first home in April 2012 and built the company to 50,000 homes in five years ([D Magazine](https://www.dmagazine.com/publications/d-ceo/2018/april/invitation-homes-rental-dallas/)).

**How many homes does Invitation Homes own?** It owned 86,192 rental homes across 16 core markets at December 2025, making it the largest owner of single-family rental homes in the United States ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/2124697328/nations-largest-single-family-rental-landlord-changes-tack)). Including joint-venture and managed-only homes, it owns or manages more than 110,000 properties ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/2124697328/nations-largest-single-family-rental-landlord-changes-tack)).

**What happened in the FTC case against Invitation Homes?** The FTC sued in September 2024, alleging undisclosed fees, deceptive lease pricing, missed pre-move-in inspections and unfairly withheld deposits ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-sends-checks-totaling-more-472-million-consumers-deceived-invitation-homes-undisclosed-fees)). The company settled for $48 million, and in March 2026 the FTC sent $47.2 million in checks to 444,131 affected consumers ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-sends-checks-totaling-more-472-million-consumers-deceived-invitation-homes-undisclosed-fees)).

**Can Invitation Homes still buy houses after the 2026 law?** From 7 January 2027, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act generally bars investors controlling 350 or more single-family homes from buying more, with exceptions for newly built homes and build-to-rent programmes ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/congress-passes-21st-century-road-to-housing-act)). It does not force the company to sell homes it already owns ([Latham & Watkins](https://www.lw.com/admin/upload/SiteAttachments/21st-Century-ROAD-to-Housing-Act-Becomes-Law.pdf)).

**How much money did Blackstone make from Invitation Homes?** Blackstone sold its remaining stake in November 2019 for $1.7 billion, and stock sales plus dividends made it about $7 billion in total — more than twice what it invested, according to the Wall Street Journal ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/other/blackstone-profitably-exits-single-family-investment-which-is-still-growing-101937)). The exit closed a seven-year run that began when Blackstone founded the company in 2012 ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/national/other/blackstone-profitably-exits-single-family-investment-which-is-still-growing-101937)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not present the FTC's allegations as adjudicated findings: the case was settled by agreed order, and no fully-read source in this session establishes whether the company admitted wrongdoing ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-sends-checks-totaling-more-472-million-consumers-deceived-invitation-homes-undisclosed-fees)). It does not settle the IPO proceeds figure: it states the $1.77 billion gross figure and flags the lower net figure reported elsewhere ([D Magazine](https://www.dmagazine.com/publications/d-ceo/2018/april/invitation-homes-rental-dallas/)). It does not date the IPO debut more precisely than February 2017, because sources give both 31 January and 1 February. It does not claim S&P 500 membership, which was not verified from a fully-read tier1 or tier2 source. It does not treat the Atlanta Federal Reserve's eviction finding as an Invitation Homes figure: that paper measured corporate landlords in Atlanta generally, finding 22 percent of Fulton County rental households received an eviction notice ([D Magazine](https://www.dmagazine.com/publications/d-ceo/2018/april/invitation-homes-rental-dallas/)). Finally, it does not claim the 2026 purchase ban will shrink the company's portfolio, since the law requires no divestment — the shift to net seller is the company's own stated plan ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/congress-passes-21st-century-road-to-housing-act); [CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/2124697328/nations-largest-single-family-rental-landlord-changes-tack)).
