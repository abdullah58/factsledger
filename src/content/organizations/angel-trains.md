## Overview

Angel Trains is one of Britain's three rolling-stock leasing companies (ROSCOs), created in March 1994 from British Rail's passenger fleet during privatisation, and it owns the trains that other companies operate ([Companies House](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/02912655); [New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/angel-trains-is-sole-sponsor-of-national-rail-awards-2000-08-06-2000/)). Its business is simple in outline: buy trains, lease them to operators, and manage maintenance, refurbishment, and upgrades across each vehicle's life. In 2000 it owned 35 percent of Britain's trains, with 19 train operating companies as customers, and it has repeatedly been described as the country's largest rolling-stock company ([New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/angel-trains-is-sole-sponsor-of-national-rail-awards-2000-08-06-2000/); [Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/articles/hydrogen-rail-project-collaboration-launched-angel-trains-and-university-st-andrews)). Its most public moment came not from a deal but from a disaster: the Class 158 unit involved in the 2008 [Barrow upon Soar](/places/barrow-upon-soar/) derailment was Angel Trains-owned, a fact that ties the leasing model directly to passenger safety ([Barrow upon Soar rail crash](/events/barrow-upon-soar-rail-crash/)).

The company has changed hands four times without ever changing its core job. It passed from Terra Firma to the Royal Bank of Scotland in 1997, then to a Babcock & Brown-led consortium in 2008 for £3.6 billion, and later to majority ownership by AMP Capital-managed investors ([Terra Firma](https://www.terrafirma.com/portfolio/angel-trains/); [Railway Gazette International](https://www.railwaygazette.com/news/train-leasing-company-sold-for-36bn/33045.article); [Infrastructure Finance & Investment](https://www.infrapppworld.com/news/amp-capital-leads-acquisition-of-stake-in-angel-trains)). Each sale treated the same thing as the prize: a fleet of thousands of vehicles generating long-term, contracted lease payments from Britain's train operators.

## From British Rail to a landlord of trains

Angel Trains began as Angel Train Contracts Limited, incorporated on 21 March 1994, one of three companies created to own the former British Rail fleet when the railways were privatised ([Companies House](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/02912655); [New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/angel-trains-is-sole-sponsor-of-national-rail-awards-2000-08-06-2000/)). The three ROSCOs split the national fleet between them, and Angel Trains took the largest share: around 3,600 electric and diesel vehicles, making it the largest passenger fleet in the country at the time of Terra Firma's 1996 acquisition ([Terra Firma](https://www.terrafirma.com/portfolio/angel-trains/)). The name changed from Angel Train Contracts to Angel Trains in May 2000, the same year the company presented itself as the leading UK financier of new passenger trains and freight locomotives ([Companies House](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/02912655); [New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/angel-trains-is-sole-sponsor-of-national-rail-awards-2000-08-06-2000/)).

The early ownership churn was fast. Terra Firma bought the business in 1996, repositioned it as a commercial enterprise, securitised a large portion of its government-guaranteed future lease payments — the first time securitisation had financed rather than refinanced a UK acquisition — and sold its equity position in December 1997 ([Terra Firma](https://www.terrafirma.com/portfolio/angel-trains/)). The Royal Bank of Scotland Group bought Angel Trains that same month and held it for a decade ([New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/angel-trains-is-sole-sponsor-of-national-rail-awards-2000-08-06-2000/)). In June 2008, RBS agreed to sell it to a consortium led by Babcock & Brown — including AMP Capital Investors, Deutsche Bank, and pension funds advised by Access Capital Advisers — at an enterprise value of £3.6 billion, with completion expected by the end of 2008 ([Railway Gazette International](https://www.railwaygazette.com/news/train-leasing-company-sold-for-36bn/33045.article)).

The 2008 deal also reshaped the company's geography. Following completion of the £3.6 billion acquisition on 6 August, the new owners announced that Angel Trains would be split into stand-alone UK and continental European businesses, so each could focus on the differing requirements of its market ([Railway Gazette International](https://www.railwaygazette.com/news/2008/08/22/split-follows-angel-trains-sale/)). Later, AMP Capital-managed investors bought out the Arcus European Infrastructure Fund 1 stake, giving AMP Capital a majority holding, while PSP Investments raised its stake to 30 percent ([Infrastructure Finance & Investment](https://www.infrapppworld.com/news/amp-capital-leads-acquisition-of-stake-in-angel-trains)). The attraction for each buyer was the same: long-term contracted revenue and stable cash flows from leasing essential assets ([Infrastructure Finance & Investment](https://www.infrapppworld.com/news/amp-capital-leads-acquisition-of-stake-in-angel-trains)).

## The leasing model and the fleet

The ROSCO model exists because Britain separated train ownership from train operation at privatisation. Operators run services under franchises or contracts; Angel Trains owns the vehicles and leases them, with leases generally independent of franchise length, so a train can outlast the operator that runs it ([New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/angel-trains-is-sole-sponsor-of-national-rail-awards-2000-08-06-2000/)). That separation is why the Barrow crash train could be owned by one company and operated by another: Angel Trains owned unit 158856, while [East Midlands Trains](/organizations/east-midlands-trains/) ran the 06:13 Nottingham to Norwich service it was working. The model also explains why leasing companies, not operators, finance most new trains.

The fleet numbers tell a story of steady growth. In 2000, Angel Trains owned about 3,600 vehicles — 535 high-speed trains, 2,030 electric multiple units, and 1,035 diesel multiple units — and had invested £1.3 billion in new trains over the previous two years ([New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/angel-trains-is-sole-sponsor-of-national-rail-awards-2000-08-06-2000/)). By 2008 it was providing UK operators with around 4,100 passenger vehicles and 280 locomotives, plus 240 locomotives and 180 multiple units to operators in 11 other European countries ([Railway Gazette International](https://www.railwaygazette.com/news/train-leasing-company-sold-for-36bn/33045.article)). By 2015, the UK fleet stood at approximately 4,500 vehicles, leased to all 19 franchised train operating companies and two open-access operators ([Infrastructure Finance & Investment](https://www.infrapppworld.com/news/amp-capital-leads-acquisition-of-stake-in-angel-trains)).

One financing innovation stands out. Angel Trains pioneered the largest-ever rail bond issue to fund Virgin Trains' West Coast Main Line fleet of tilting Pendolino trains, built jointly by Fiat in Italy and Alstom in Birmingham ([New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/angel-trains-is-sole-sponsor-of-national-rail-awards-2000-08-06-2000/)). The Pendolino deal showed the model's logic at full scale: a leasing company raising private capital for trains that a franchised operator could never have financed alone, with the trains themselves as the underlying asset. The same logic underpinned the three ROSCOs' combined £2.4 billion of new-train investment in the two years to 2000, against £900 million invested by British Rail in the five years before privatisation ([New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/angel-trains-is-sole-sponsor-of-national-rail-awards-2000-08-06-2000/)).

## Green experiments and the Barrow crash

In its recent years, Angel Trains has positioned itself as a backer of low-carbon rail experiments. It supported the delivery of Scotland's first zero-emission hydrogen-powered train, a three-car Class 314 converted to a hydrogen fuel cell electric powertrain, and donated a hydrogen electrolyser to the University of St Andrews to support green hydrogen research ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/articles/hydrogen-rail-project-collaboration-launched-angel-trains-and-university-st-andrews)). The company's chief executive, Malcolm Brown, said Angel Trains continued to invest heavily in green hydrogen refuelling infrastructure and innovation to drive down operating costs ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/articles/hydrogen-rail-project-collaboration-launched-angel-trains-and-university-st-andrews)). The hydrogen project was presented as a step toward net-zero targets, with the electrolyser enabling a trial of a full green-energy fuel cycle ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/articles/hydrogen-rail-project-collaboration-launched-angel-trains-and-university-st-andrews)).

The Barrow upon Soar crash is the darker entry in the company's record. On 1 February 2008, the Angel Trains-owned Class 158 unit 158856, working the 06:13 Nottingham to Norwich service for East Midlands Trains, collided with debris from a footbridge demolished by a tipper lorry's raised body and derailed, running on for 170 metres ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar)). The driver, guard, and one passenger were taken to hospital, and the driver was released the next morning ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar)). The investigation blamed the lorry driver's distraction and [Network Rail](/organizations/network-rail/)'s lack of a formal process for managing road-vehicle risk near live lines — not the train's owner or its condition ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar)). Network Rail, the infrastructure owner, later pleaded guilty to health-and-safety breaches and was fined £80,000 with £32,000 costs ([New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/network-rail-fined-for-bridge-collapse-and-train-derailment-04-10-2011/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Angel Trains?**
Angel Trains is one of Britain's three rolling-stock leasing companies, created in 1994 from British Rail's fleet. It buys passenger trains and freight locomotives, leases them to train operators, and manages maintenance, refurbishment, and upgrades across each vehicle's working life.

**Who owns Angel Trains?**
Ownership has changed four times: Terra Firma (1996–1997), the Royal Bank of Scotland (1997–2008), a Babcock & Brown-led consortium from 2008, and later AMP Capital-managed investors holding a majority, with PSP Investments at 30 percent after a 2015 transaction.

**Why do leasing companies own Britain's trains?**
Privatisation separated train ownership from train operation. Operators run services under franchises or contracts, while leasing companies own the vehicles and lease them, with leases generally independent of franchise length, so trains can outlast any single operator.

**Was Angel Trains responsible for the Barrow upon Soar crash?**
No. It owned the Class 158 unit involved, but the investigation blamed the lorry driver's distraction and Network Rail's missing road-vehicle risk process. Network Rail pleaded guilty to health-and-safety breaches and was fined; no fault was attributed to the train's owner.
