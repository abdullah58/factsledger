## Overview

The House of Commons Standing Committee on Employment and Immigration was the parliamentary committee that oversaw [Canada](/places/canada/)'s labour and immigration files together — formally the Standing Committee on Labour, Employment and Immigration from 28 February 1985 until a restructuring on 25 January 1994 split it in two. Its immigration half became today's Standing Committee on Citizenship and Immigration, and its labour half became the human-resources committee now called HUMA ([House of Commons of Canada](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/annotated-standing-orders/23_asoii_endnotes_chap13-e.html)).

The short name is the one people actually used: Hansard, the 2019 tribute to its chair Jim Hawkes, [Colleges and Institutes Canada](/organizations/colleges-and-institutes-canada/) and academic writing all call it the "Standing Committee on Employment and Immigration", while the formal name in the Standing Orders kept the word "Labour" ([House of Commons Debates](https://openparliament.ca/debates/2019/5/14/ron-liepert-1/only/); [Colleges and Institutes Canada](https://www.collegesinstitutes.ca/cicans-50th-anniversary/advocacy/)). It was a working committee, not a ceremonial one: it ran its own studies, heard witnesses, reviewed Order in Council appointments and reported to the House on everything from the points system to unemployment insurance.

## A name with three lives

The committee's lineage reaches back to at least 1969, when its minutes were published under the name Standing Committee on Labour, Manpower and Immigration; library holdings of those minutes run from 1968/69 through 1993 ([HathiTrust Digital Library](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/000067697)). On 28 February 1985 the House agreed to rename it the Standing Committee on Labour, Employment and Immigration, swapping "Manpower" for "Employment" ([House of Commons of Canada](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/annotated-standing-orders/23_asoii_endnotes_chap13-e.html)). The pairing of labour and immigration in one committee mirrored the department it oversaw — [Employment and Immigration Canada](/organizations/employment-and-immigration-canada/) and its Canada Employment and Immigration Commission — and academic writing of the period calls it the "CEIC/HRD Committee" ([Canadian Public Policy](http://qed.econ.queensu.ca/pub/cpp/Dec1998/Hale.pdf)). Like other standing committees it could make reports and send for persons, papers and records, and it worked through two subcommittees, one on Immigration and one on Labour and Employment ([House of Commons of Canada](https://www.ourcommons.ca/Committees/en/CIMM/About?parl=41&session=1); [Government of Canada Publications](https://publications.gc.ca/site/eng/9.510210/publication.html)).

## What the committee actually did

In the last three months of 1986 alone the committee held 14 meetings under its chair Jim Hawkes — on functional illiteracy, labour force adjustment, immigration levels, the detention of immigrants, immigrant women, the points system, medical guidelines for assessing immigrants, entrepreneurial immigration and unemployment insurance ([Government of Canada Publications](https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2022/spac-pspc/ic6/IC6-1-34-4.pdf)). That pace shows what the committee was for: it was the House's standing forum where the two halves of the Employment and Immigration portfolio — jobs and newcomers — were examined together, in public, with witnesses.

In 1987 Colleges and Institutes Canada appeared before it in a long meeting on the 1985 Canadian Jobs Strategy, which was shifting training funds from colleges into privatized industry, and said its efforts helped improve the strategy's language ([Colleges and Institutes Canada](https://www.collegesinstitutes.ca/cicans-50th-anniversary/advocacy/)). Its Ninth Report, on family reunification, drew a brief from the National Organization of Immigrant and Visible Minority Women of Canada on 30 May 1988, urging faster processing and a broader definition of family ([National Organization of Immigrant and Visible Minority Women of Canada](https://riseupfeministarchive.ca/wp-content/uploads/NOIVMWC-nationalupdate-aug-1988-OCR-1.pdf)). And on 5 June 1990 its chair Jean-Pierre Blackburn tabled a Third Report, an interim report on demography and immigration levels prepared at the minister's request to set out what immigration levels should be for the next five years ([House of Commons Debates](https://www.lipad.ca/full/1990/06/05/1/)).

The committee also had a sharp internal life. In the mid-1980s, after the June 1985 McGrath report declared committees "masters of their own destinies", government members removed chairman Fernand Jourdenais and barred members from contacting the committee's researchers without the chair's permission; MP John R. Rodriguez of Nickel Belt raised the matter as a question of privilege, arguing the researchers were hired with Parliament's money and the committee could retain staff under the then Standing Order 97(1) ([House of Commons Debates](https://www.lipad.ca/full/permalink/3695474)). Academic assessment later described the committee as a frequent platform for social-policy advocates, beneficiary groups, trade unions and government-funded agencies lobbying against cuts to unemployment insurance ([Canadian Public Policy](http://qed.econ.queensu.ca/pub/cpp/Dec1998/Hale.pdf)).

## The 1994 split and what it became

The committee ended not by abolition but by division. On 25 January 1994, at the start of the 35th Parliament under Prime Minister Jean Chrétien, the House restructured its committees: "Labour and Employment became Human Resources Development" and "Immigration and Citizenship were combined into one committee Citizenship and Immigration" ([House of Commons of Canada](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/annotated-standing-orders/23_asoii_endnotes_chap13-e.html)). The immigration successor, the Standing Committee on Citizenship and Immigration (CIMM), studies immigration, citizenship and federal multiculturalism policy and oversees Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada and the Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/corporate/transparency/committees/cimm-nov-07-2023/committee-overview.html)).

It is still working: in the 45th Parliament CIMM is studying Bill C-12 on border security and the integrity of the immigration system, and examining Canada's immigration system as a whole ([House of Commons of Canada](https://www.ourcommons.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/CIMM/meeting-10/evidence)). The labour successor passed through Human Resources Development, then Human Resources Development and the Status of Persons with Disabilities, and is now the Standing Committee on Human Resources, Skills and Social Development and the Status of Persons with Disabilities (HUMA), which in November 2025 opened a study of the Temporary Foreign Worker Program's effects on the labour market ([House of Commons of Canada](https://www.ourcommons.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/HUMA/news-release/13773654)). The old pairing of jobs and newcomers is gone, but both halves still sit in the House — separately.

## FAQ

### What was the Standing Committee on Employment and Immigration?

It was a standing committee of the Canadian House of Commons that oversaw labour and immigration together. Formally named the Standing Committee on Labour, Employment and Immigration from 28 February 1985 to 25 January 1994, it ran studies, heard witnesses and reported to the House on the Employment and Immigration portfolio, including immigration levels and unemployment insurance.

### What is it called today?

It has no single successor. The 1994 restructuring split it: the immigration half became the Standing Committee on Citizenship and Immigration (CIMM), still active in the 45th Parliament, and the labour half became the human-resources committee now called HUMA, the Standing Committee on Human Resources, Skills and Social Development and the Status of Persons with Disabilities.

### Who chaired the committee?

Jim Hawkes, MP for Calgary West from 1979 to 1993, chaired it in the mid-1980s, including the 14 meetings of late 1986. Jean-Pierre Blackburn chaired it from 1989 to 1991 and tabled its interim report on immigration levels, and Robert Wenman chaired it from 1991 to 1993. Fernand Jourdenais chaired it earlier in the 1980s before government members removed him.

### What did the committee actually study?

In late 1986 it examined functional illiteracy, labour force adjustment, immigration levels, the detention of immigrants, immigrant women, the points system, medical guidelines for immigrants, entrepreneurial immigration and unemployment insurance. Later work included family reunification (its Ninth Report, 1988) and an interim report on demography and immigration levels (its Third Report, 1990).

### Is this the same as the Senate committee on immigration?

No. This profile covers the House of Commons body only. The Senate has its own separate committee system with its own committees, and this committee's work, minutes and reports all belong to the House of Commons, not to any Senate committee.
