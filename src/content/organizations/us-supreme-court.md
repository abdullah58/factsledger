## Overview

The Supreme Court of the United States closed its October 2025 term on June 30, 2026, with a verdict that pulled in two directions at once. The Court struck down President Donald Trump's tariff regime and his executive order ending birthright citizenship — and, on the same docket, expanded his power to fire the heads of independent agencies by overturning *Humphrey's Executor*, the precedent protecting [Federal Trade Commission](https://factsledger.com/organizations/federal-trade-commission/) commissioners from at-will removal ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)). It decided 66 cases over the term, with 23 percent — 15 cases — splitting along ideological lines, more than double the average of the previous five terms ([Wisconsin Lawyer](https://www.wisbar.org/NewsPublications/WisconsinLawyer/Pages/Article.aspx?Volume=99&Issue=8&ArticleID=31767)). Beyond the argued docket, the Court's emergency "shadow docket" has decided 32 applications challenging administration actions since January 20, 2025, ruling for the administration at least partially in 24 of them ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/supreme-court-shadow-docket-tracker-challenges-trump-administration)).

## Reining in the president: tariffs and birthright citizenship

The term's most consequential economic ruling was *Learning Resources v. Trump*, decided 6–3 with Chief Justice John Roberts writing the majority opinion. The Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the president to impose tariffs. Roberts wrote that "against that backdrop of clear and limited delegations, the Government reads IEEPA to give the president power to unilaterally impose unbounded tariffs and change them at will. That view would represent a transformative expansion of the President's authority over tariff policy" ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)). Justice Brett Kavanaugh's main dissent argued Trump could use IEEPA "in light of the statutory text, longstanding historical practice, and relevant Supreme Court precedents" ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)).

On the term's final day, the Court also blocked the executive order attempting to end birthright citizenship in *Trump v. Barbara*. Roberts held that children born in the United States to unlawfully or temporarily present parents are citizens under the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause; Kavanaugh concurred on the statutory ground of 8 U.S.C. §1401(a), while Justices Thomas and Gorsuch dissented ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)). The judgment was 6–3, with Roberts, Justice Amy Coney Barrett and Kavanaugh voting alongside the liberal bloc ([Wisconsin Lawyer](https://www.wisbar.org/NewsPublications/WisconsinLawyer/Pages/Article.aspx?Volume=99&Issue=8&ArticleID=31767)). A Marquette poll found 68 percent of the public agreed with the outcome — including 43 percent of Republicans, 71 percent of independents and 91 percent of Democrats ([Wisconsin Lawyer](https://www.wisbar.org/NewsPublications/WisconsinLawyer/Pages/Article.aspx?Volume=99&Issue=8&ArticleID=31767)).

## Expanding the president: removal power

The same term gave the presidency one of its largest structural victories in decades. In *Trump v. Slaughter*, a 6–3 majority with Roberts writing held that statutory protections shielding Federal Trade Commission commissioners from presidential removal — removable only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office" — are contrary to the separation of powers, overturning *Humphrey's Executor* ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)). The ruling extends at-will presidential removal across independent agencies.

The Court drew one conspicuous line around that expansion. In *Trump v. Cook*, it denied the administration's request to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook while her challenge proceeds, splitting 5–4 with Justices Thomas, Alito and Gorsuch dissenting ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)). The Federal Reserve, in other words, kept a carve-out the rest of the independent agencies did not.

## The rest of the docket

The term reshaped several other areas of law. In *Louisiana v. Callais*, a 6–3 majority with Justice Samuel Alito writing narrowed the ability of states to use race as a determining factor in creating election districts, focusing on Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act of 1965; Justice Elena Kagan's dissent called it "the latest chapter in the majority's now-completed demolition of the Voting Rights Act" ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)). In *West Virginia v. B.P.J.* and *Little v. Hecox*, the Court held that states may bar transgender athletes from girls' and women's sports teams, with Kavanaugh writing that Title IX permits sex-separated teams and Justice [Sonia Sotomayor](/people/sonia-sotomayor/) dissenting in part ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)). Sixty-three percent of the public agreed with the trans-athlete rulings, per the Marquette poll ([Wisconsin Lawyer](https://www.wisbar.org/NewsPublications/WisconsinLawyer/Pages/Article.aspx?Volume=99&Issue=8&ArticleID=31767)).

On speech and guns, the Court was less predictable. *Chiles v. Salazar* struck down Colorado's ban on conversion therapy for minors as viewpoint discrimination under the First Amendment, 8–1, with Justice Neil Gorsuch writing and Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissenting ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)). *Wolford v. Lopez* invalidated Hawaii's private-property gun restriction under the Second and Fourteenth Amendments, 6–3 with Alito writing and Kagan dissenting — while *U.S. v. Hemani* unanimously held that prosecuting a marijuana user for gun possession under 18 U.S.C. §922(g)(3) is inconsistent with the Second Amendment, in a Gorsuch opinion ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)).

Other notable rulings: *Watson v. RNC* (5–4, Barrett) held that late-arriving postmarked ballots can be counted; *Chatrie v. U.S.* (6–3, Kagan) held that geofence location data is a Fourth Amendment search; *Mullin v. Doe* (6–3, Alito) ended Temporary Protected Status for Syrian and Haitian nationals while barring courts from reviewing non-constitutional claims; and *Mullin v. Al Otro Lado* (6–3, Alito) required physical border crossing for asylum claims ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)). The term's final day also included a 6–3 decision striking down campaign-finance restrictions in *NRSC v. FEC* — an outcome only 26 percent of the public agreed with, the poll found ([Wisconsin Lawyer](https://www.wisbar.org/NewsPublications/WisconsinLawyer/Pages/Article.aspx?Volume=99&Issue=8&ArticleID=31767)).

## The shadow docket

Running alongside the argued cases was the Court's emergency docket, which the Brennan Center's tracker describes as now operating at an unprecedented rate. In the first 20 weeks of the second Trump administration, the government made 19 emergency applications — as many as the Biden administration made over four years; the Obama and George W. Bush administrations combined made eight over sixteen years ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/supreme-court-shadow-docket-tracker-challenges-trump-administration)). Since January 20, 2025, the Court has issued 32 shadow-docket decisions on administration actions: 24 ruled for the administration at least partially and 8 against it, with 8 carrying no written explanation at all and most others offering only brief analysis ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/supreme-court-shadow-docket-tracker-challenges-trump-administration)).

The Court has ordered full oral argument in five of the shadow-docket cases — *Noem v. Doe* and *Trump v. Miot* (consolidated), *Trump v. Slaughter*, *Trump v. CASA*, and *Trump v. Cook* — and one application remained pending as of late September 2026: whether the Bureau of Prisons can implement restrictions on gender-affirming care for transgender inmates, filed September 28, 2026 ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/supreme-court-shadow-docket-tracker-challenges-trump-administration)). Kagan dissented from the pattern, writing: "Our emergency docket should never be used, as it has been this year, to permit what our own precedent bars. Still more, it should not be used, as it also has been, to transfer government authority from Congress to the President, and thus to reshape the Nation's separation of powers" ([Brennan Center](https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/supreme-court-shadow-docket-tracker-challenges-trump-administration)).

## The institution and the bench

The Court describes itself as "the highest tribunal in the Nation for all cases and controversies arising under the Constitution or the laws of the United States" — the final arbiter of the law and the guardian and interpreter of the Constitution ([Supreme Court of the United States](https://www.supremecourt.gov/about/about.aspx)). It consists of the Chief Justice and eight associate justices, a number fixed by Congress; justices are nominated by the President and appointed with the advice and consent of the Senate ([Supreme Court of the United States](https://www.supremecourt.gov/about/about.aspx)).

The current bench, as characterized by the Wisconsin Lawyer, divides into six generally conservative justices — Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Alito, Barrett, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh and Thomas — and three liberals — Justices Jackson, Kagan and Sotomayor ([Wisconsin Lawyer](https://www.wisbar.org/NewsPublications/WisconsinLawyer/Pages/Article.aspx?Volume=99&Issue=8&ArticleID=31767)). The factions do not always disagree: the Court was unanimous in 44 percent of the term's cases, and in 15 percent some conservatives aligned with the liberal justices ([Wisconsin Lawyer](https://www.wisbar.org/NewsPublications/WisconsinLawyer/Pages/Article.aspx?Volume=99&Issue=8&ArticleID=31767)). Arguments for the next term resume October 5, 2026, with 20 cases granted as of July 9, 2026 — including an assault-weapons case from Cook County, Illinois, and Connecticut accepted on the term's final day ([National Constitution Center](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/the-supreme-court-in-review-another-landmark-term)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not recount the Court's founding history — the 1789 establishment and *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) — because no source verifying those facts was read in full for this profile. It presents the conservative/liberal labels as the Wisconsin Lawyer's characterizations ("generally considered"), not as the justices' self-descriptions. The Marquette poll figures are reported polling, not findings of this profile. And it treats shadow-docket rulings as emergency decisions, not final rulings on the legal merits — the Brennan Center notes these rulings are not final merits decisions even though they have significant real-world impact while cases remain pending.
