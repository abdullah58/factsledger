## Overview

Fujifilm Holdings Corporation is a [Japanese](/places/japan/) conglomerate headquartered in Tokyo that began in 1934 as Fuji Photo Film, a maker of photographic film. When digital photography destroyed its core market in the 2000s, it reinvented itself around healthcare, electronics and imaging, and in the year to March 2026 it reported record revenue of ¥3,357.0 billion ([FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://holdings.fujifilm.com/en/about/companyprofile); [FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://ir.fujifilm.com/en/investors/ir-materials/earnings-presentations/main/01113/teaserItems5/00/linkList/0/link/ff_fy2025q4_001e.pdf)).

Fuji Photo Film Co., Ltd. was founded on 20 January 1934 to manufacture photographic film domestically, with operations beginning that February at the Ashigara Factory ([Fujifilm](https://www.fujifilm.com/au/en/about/region/history)). The company grew far beyond film: it entered medical imaging with its first X-ray film in 1936, launched the Fuji Xerox document joint venture in 1962, and built a camera business that Fujifilm says produced the world's first single-use camera in 1986 and the world's first digital camera with removable media, the DS-1P, in 1988 ([Fujifilm](https://www.fujifilm.com/au/en/about/region/history)). On 1 October 2006 the old company was transformed into a holding company renamed FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation, with its businesses taken over by the newly established FUJIFILM Corporation ([FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://holdings.fujifilm.com/en/about/companyprofile)).

Today the group is run from Akasaka, Tokyo, by president and CEO Teiichi Goto, employs 73,526 people across 258 consolidated subsidiaries, and operates in four segments — Healthcare, Electronics, Business Innovation and Imaging ([FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://holdings.fujifilm.com/en/about/companyprofile)). Its two operating companies are FUJIFILM Corporation and FUJIFILM Business Innovation Corp., the former Fuji Xerox ([FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://holdings.fujifilm.com/en/about/companyprofile)).

## From film to the Second Foundation

For most of the twentieth century, Fujifilm was a film company that happened to do other things. Film sales peaked around 2000, and then the decline arrived not as a gradual fade but as a sudden break: within six months in 2003, Japanese photo-processing shops went from handling close to 5,000 rolls of film a day to fewer than 1,000 ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)). A market that had produced nearly 70 percent of Fujifilm's profit had effectively ceased to exist ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)).

Under chief executive Shigetaka Komori, Fujifilm asked a different question from its great rival Eastman Kodak, which had invented the digital camera in 1975 and still led the American market in 2005, yet filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on 19 January 2012 listing debts of $6.75 billion ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)). Instead of asking how to protect film, Komori asked what Fujifilm actually knew how to do — chemistry, optics and nanotechnology — and while closing film plants and cutting thousands of jobs, he simultaneously spent $400 million building a new research facility dedicated entirely to new business development ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)). The company called the strategy "intellectual fusion" and the broader project the "Second Foundation", a name Komori chose deliberately to frame survival as a second founding rather than a restructuring ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)).

Komori also changed how the company thought. He replaced the standard Plan-Do-Check-Act improvement cycle with STPD — See-Think-Plan-Do — on the argument that in a collapsing market, planning from past experience is a trap, because the plan is built from the logic of a business that no longer exists ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)). Fujifilm's own governance documentation describes the shift: "With the growing speed of social change, our previous business experience and accumulated knowledge no longer apply to a growing range of our business activities. For this reason, the Fujifilm Group has upgraded its existing approach to PDCA and established STPD independently to facilitate action from new perspectives" ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)).

The reinvention was not only a matter of redirecting old know-how. The same precision coating that made photographic emulsion underpins Fujifilm's position as the world's leading manufacturer of data-storage tape, the antioxidation chemistry that stopped colour prints from fading became the active science in its Astalift skincare range, and thin-film coating built for film is now a core material in manufacturing semiconductors ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)). Fujifilm holds the world's largest market share in the chemical slurry used to polish copper wiring on chips, which makes the former film company an unobvious supplier to the AI chip boom ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)). But the biologics business at the heart of the new Fujifilm was bought, not built: it originated in 2011 with the purchase of Merck's biomanufacturing network and has grown largely through further acquisition, absorbing more than $8 billion in investment ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)).

## Healthcare and electronics: the new core

Healthcare is now Fujifilm's largest growth engine, and its expansion has been driven by acquisition. In March 2019 Fujifilm agreed to pay about $890 million for Biogen's biologics manufacturing site in Hillerød, Denmark, a plant opened in 2003 with 90,000 litres of production capacity across six 15,000-litre bioreactors, whose roughly 800 employees joined Fujifilm ([Chemical & Engineering News](https://centest.acs.org/business/mergers-&-acquisitions/Fujifilm-acquire-Biogen-plant0/97/i11)). The site became Fujifilm's fourth biopharmaceutical contract development and manufacturing site, run by its FUJIFILM Diosynth Biotechnologies arm, which had already acquired biologics plants in North Carolina, Texas and England ([Chemical & Engineering News](https://centest.acs.org/business/mergers-&-acquisitions/Fujifilm-acquire-Biogen-plant0/97/i11)). As part of the deal Fujifilm supplies products to Biogen, including its multiple sclerosis drug Tysabri, while bringing third-party customers into the facility ([Chemical & Engineering News](https://centest.acs.org/business/mergers-&-acquisitions/Fujifilm-acquire-Biogen-plant0/97/i11)).

Two years later Fujifilm completed the acquisition of Hitachi's diagnostic imaging business on 31 March 2021, a deal initially valued at ¥179 billion ($1.618 billion) when announced in December 2019 and delayed by the COVID-19 pandemic ([AuntMinnie](https://www.auntminnie.com/imaging-informatics/advanced-visualization/image-processing/article/15628126/fujifilm-wraps-up-hitachi-imaging-acquisition)). The purchase added Hitachi's CT, MRI and ultrasound offerings to Fujifilm's own digital radiography, PACS, point-of-care ultrasound, endoscopy and in vitro diagnostics, and the combined business began operating as the wholly owned subsidiary Fujifilm Healthcare ([AuntMinnie](https://www.auntminnie.com/imaging-informatics/advanced-visualization/image-processing/article/15628126/fujifilm-wraps-up-hitachi-imaging-acquisition)). Fujifilm reorganised healthcare into two divisions, Medical System and Life Sciences, the latter covering Bio CDMO, iPS cells, cell culture media and reagents, pharmaceutical products, and consumer healthcare including cosmetics and supplements ([AuntMinnie](https://www.auntminnie.com/imaging-informatics/advanced-visualization/image-processing/article/15628126/fujifilm-wraps-up-hitachi-imaging-acquisition)).

The medical business also reaches patients directly. In Bengaluru in 2021, Fujifilm India and Dr. Kutty's Healthcare opened a health-screening centre called NURA, where a patient undergoes imaging across multiple organs and receives results within two hours, with scans analysed by Fujifilm's REiLI platform running on low-dose CT imaging ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)). Fujifilm chose India as its first market because the five-year cancer survival rate there is roughly 30 percent, against 70 percent in Japan, a gap the company traces largely to the near-absence of routine screening ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)). The network now covers four Indian cities, Mongolia and Vietnam, with plans for 100 centres globally by 2030 ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/)).

The scale of the transformation shows in the accounts. In the year ended 31 March 2026, Fujifilm reported record revenue of ¥3,357.0 billion, up 5.0 percent, with operating income of ¥350.2 billion and net income of ¥276.7 billion, also records ([FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://ir.fujifilm.com/en/investors/ir-materials/earnings-presentations/main/01113/teaserItems5/00/linkList/0/link/ff_fy2025q4_001e.pdf)). Growth was driven by Healthcare, where new Bio CDMO facilities in Denmark contributed, by Electronics, on strong sales of semiconductor materials including CMP slurries, and by Imaging, where digital cameras posted robust sales ([FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://ir.fujifilm.com/en/investors/ir-materials/earnings-presentations/main/01113/teaserItems5/00/linkList/0/link/ff_fy2025q4_001e.pdf)).

## Two courtroom sagas: the JPEG patent and Xerox

Fujifilm's history includes two legal episodes that the standard company histories skip. The first began in April 2004, when Forgent Networks — the company now known as [Asure Software](/organizations/asure-software/) — sued 31 companies through its [Compression Labs](/organizations/compression-labs/) subsidiary, alleging they infringed U.S. Patent No. 4,698,672, a "coding system for reducing redundancy" that Forgent said was integral to JPEG image compression ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/171812/forgent.html)). Fuji Photo Film Co. U.S.A. was among the companies named, alongside Apple, Adobe, [IBM](/organizations/ibm/), [Dell](/organizations/dell/), [HP](/organizations/hp/), Eastman [Kodak](/organizations/kodak/) and [Xerox](/organizations/xerox/) ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/171812/forgent.html)). The patent had been filed by Compression Labs in October 1986, Forgent had acquired Compression Labs in 1997, and Forgent had been asserting the patent since June 2002, when it announced sole and exclusive rights to license it ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2004/04/23/forgent_jpeg_suit/)).

Forgent had already collected about $90 million in licensing royalties from roughly 30 companies by April 2004, with [Sony](/organizations/sony/) as the highest-profile licensee ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/171812/forgent.html)). But the campaign collapsed: the [Public Patent Foundation](/organizations/public-patent-foundation/) succeeded in May 2006 in having the patent rejected in an initial review, and in June 2006 a court limited what portions of the patent Forgent could assert ([BetaNews](http://betanews.com/article/forgent-settles-jpeg-patent-litigation/)). In November 2006 Forgent settled with about 30 companies for about $8 million — far less than the $1 billion it had once hoped to extract — having collected over $110 million in royalties from more than 60 companies over the life of the patent ([BetaNews](http://betanews.com/article/forgent-settles-jpeg-patent-litigation/)). The patent itself expired in the United States in October 2006 ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2004/04/23/forgent_jpeg_suit/)).

The second saga was Fujifilm's own. On 31 January 2018, Xerox and Fujifilm announced a transaction under which Fujifilm would take a 50.1 percent stake in Xerox for $6.1 billion, contributing its 75 percent interest in Fuji Xerox, with existing Xerox stockholders receiving a $2.5 billion special dividend ([Harvard Law Forum on Corporate Governance](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/10/30/new-ruling-on-the-fujifilm-xerox-transaction/)). Activist investors Carl Icahn and Darwin Deason, Xerox's largest shareholders, moved to block the deal, and on 27 April 2018 the New York Supreme Court preliminarily enjoined it after a two-day hearing, finding Xerox's chief executive had breached his fiduciary duties ([Harvard Law Forum on Corporate Governance](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/10/30/new-ruling-on-the-fujifilm-xerox-transaction/)). Xerox then settled with Icahn and Deason, removing its chief executive and six board members, and in June 2018 Fujifilm sued Xerox claiming damages for the unilateral cancellation of the agreement ([Harvard Law Forum on Corporate Governance](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/10/30/new-ruling-on-the-fujifilm-xerox-transaction/); [FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://ir.fujifilm.com/en/investors/ir-news/auto_20191105419209/pdfFile.pdf)).

Fujifilm won the legal argument but lost the deal. On 16 October 2018 the New York Appellate Division unanimously reversed the injunction and dismissed all claims against Fujifilm, yet the combination was already dead ([Harvard Law Forum on Corporate Governance](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/10/30/new-ruling-on-the-fujifilm-xerox-transaction/)). The ending came a year later on different terms: on 5 November 2019 Fujifilm agreed to pay $2.3 billion for Xerox's 25 percent stake in Fuji Xerox and a related interest, bringing its ownership to 100 percent, withdrawing its damages suit, and ending a 57-year joint venture that Fujifilm described as one of the most successful cross-border joint ventures between Japanese and non-Japanese companies ([FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://ir.fujifilm.com/en/investors/ir-news/auto_20191105419209/pdfFile.pdf)).

## Imaging today: instax and the camera business

Photography did not disappear from Fujifilm — it shrank, changed shape, and became profitable again. The instax line of instant cameras and smartphone printers, launched in 1998, passed 100 million units in cumulative global sales by the end of the 2024 fiscal year, and is sold in over 100 countries across mini, SQUARE and WIDE film formats ([FUJIFILM Corporation](https://www.fujifilm.com/jp/en/news/hq/12295)). ## Frequently asked questions

**What does Fujifilm do now?**
Today Fujifilm is a Tokyo-headquartered conglomerate organised in four segments: Healthcare (medical systems, Bio CDMO biologics manufacturing and life-sciences solutions), Electronics (semiconductor and display materials), Business Innovation (office and document solutions) and Imaging (cameras and instax instant photography) ([FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://holdings.fujifilm.com/en/about/companyprofile)).

**When did Fujifilm stop being a film company?**
There was no single moment. Film sales collapsed in 2003, the company restructured as a holding company in October 2006, and it spent the following two decades buying into healthcare and electronics — Biogen's Danish plant in 2019 and Hitachi's imaging business in 2021 among them ([GLOBIS Europe](https://globis.eu/fujifilms-masterclass-in-corporate-rebirth/); [FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://holdings.fujifilm.com/en/about/companyprofile)).

**What happened between Fujifilm and Xerox?**
In January 2018 Fujifilm agreed to take a 50.1 percent stake in Xerox for $6.1 billion, but activist shareholders blocked the deal in court and Xerox walked away. Fujifilm sued and won on appeal, then in November 2019 paid $2.3 billion for Xerox's 25 percent stake in Fuji Xerox ([Harvard Law Forum on Corporate Governance](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2018/10/30/new-ruling-on-the-fujifilm-xerox-transaction/); [FUJIFILM Holdings Corporation](https://ir.fujifilm.com/en/investors/ir-news/auto_20191105419209/pdfFile.pdf)).

**Was Fujifilm sued over the JPEG patent?**
Yes. In April 2004 Forgent Networks sued 31 companies, including Fuji Photo Film Co. U.S.A., claiming U.S. Patent 4,698,672 covered JPEG compression. Forgent settled its remaining cases for about $8 million in November 2006 after the patent was rejected on initial review ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/171812/forgent.html); [BetaNews](http://betanews.com/article/forgent-settles-jpeg-patent-litigation/)).

**What is instax?**
instax is Fujifilm's line of instant cameras, hybrid cameras and smartphone printers, launched in 1998. Cumulative global sales passed 100 million units by the end of fiscal 2024, and the brand is sold in over 100 countries in mini, SQUARE and WIDE formats ([FUJIFILM Corporation](https://www.fujifilm.com/jp/en/news/hq/12295)).
