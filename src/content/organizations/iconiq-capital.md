## Overview

ICONIQ Capital is a San Francisco investment firm that began in 2011 as the wealth manager for a small circle of technology billionaires and has grown into a roughly $100 billion manager that also runs one of Silicon Valley's most concentrated venture portfolios ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). It was founded by Divesh Makan, Michael Anders and Chad Boeding, three advisers who left [Morgan Stanley](/organizations/morgan-stanley/) to serve their clients independently; Makan had met Facebook co-founder Mark Zuckerberg while advising him at Morgan Stanley and [Goldman Sachs](/organizations/goldman-sachs/), and Zuckerberg became one of the new firm's earliest clients ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). What sets ICONIQ apart is the hybrid it built from that start: a multi-family office managing the personal fortunes of founders and royalty, fused to a venture arm that by 2026 managed about $26 billion and had made its largest-ever single bet — about $4 billion — on the AI lab Anthropic ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)).

## From family office to venture investor

The firm's first decade was a slow build on relationships. Makan's early clients introduced him to the rest of Facebook's founding circle — Dustin Moskovitz, Sheryl Sandberg and Reid Hoffman — and the client list grew by referral among technology founders, Middle Eastern and Asian government figures, royal families and entertainment stars including Tom Cruise and Pharrell Williams, with Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang signing on more recently ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). The venture arm arrived in 2013, when Makan and Will Griffith — a former TCV general partner who became the venture arm's founding partner — raised a first fund of $509 million, deliberately chasing software-as-a-service companies while rivals crowded into consumer startups ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). The early record justified the focus: by 2016 the firm had backed 11 unicorns, including Uber and Flipkart, and its first exits were Viv Labs, acquired by Samsung, and Coupa, which went public at an $866 million valuation ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/iconiq-capital-funding-teardown/)). The fund returns were unusually strong — the first fund returned investors 2.6 times their money, the second $1.02 billion fund returned 4.2 times, and the third $1.33 billion fund returned 4.7 times, driven by Snowflake, GitLab and Procore — with the first four funds all ranking in the top 25% of their peer groups on Cambridge Associates benchmarks ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). The most recent venture fund raised $5.75 billion, and the firm is almost always the lead investor in the deals it does, keeping its portfolio concentrated so it can devote resources to each company ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)).

## The Chime courtship

ICONIQ's investment in [Chime](/organizations/chime/) shows how the firm works a deal. Partners Yoonkee Sull and Greg Stanger first met Chime co-founders [Chris Britt](/people/chris-britt/) and [Ryan King](/people/ryan-king/) in 2017 — going to Chime's offices for the meeting — and then spent two years watching the founders before writing a cheque, preferring outbound deals with well-vetted founders over inbound pitches ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/13/iconiq-vcs-spent-two-years-courting-chime-and-the-firm-isnt-selling-its-stake/)). The patience mattered: Chime had nearly run out of cash in 2016, when King was turned down by more than 100 venture firms before a $9 million Series A extension saved the company ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/13/iconiq-vcs-spent-two-years-courting-chime-and-the-firm-isnt-selling-its-stake/)). Convinced by two years of the founders delivering on their promises, ICONIQ elbowed into Chime's oversubscribed $200 million Series D in 2019, paying $5.22 a share, and followed on in later rounds at about $17, $41 and $69 a share ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/13/iconiq-vcs-spent-two-years-courting-chime-and-the-firm-isnt-selling-its-stake/)). When Chime went public in June 2025 at $27 a share — opening at $43 and raising $864 million — Sull said the firm was keeping its shares: "We have our shares, and we're not selling in the IPO" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/13/iconiq-vcs-spent-two-years-courting-chime-and-the-firm-isnt-selling-its-stake/)). The holding was a small irony for a firm built on serving billionaires: Chime sells banking and credit-building to average earners and the working class, the opposite of ICONIQ's wealth-management clientele ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/13/iconiq-vcs-spent-two-years-courting-chime-and-the-firm-isnt-selling-its-stake/)).

## The Anthropic gamble

Anthropic is the bet that now defines the firm. ICONIQ first met the AI lab in 2023 and passed, then re-entered in 2025 and led its $13 billion Series F at a $183 billion valuation — and organised a 2025 trip taking chief executive Dario Amodei to meet sovereign investors in the Middle East, including Qatar's fund and Abu Dhabi-based MGX ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). By April 2026 ICONIQ had put about $4 billion into Anthropic, a majority of its $5.2 billion in AI investments to date, for a stake of under 2% that Bloomberg estimated at roughly $7 billion at the then-current valuation; Makan joined Anthropic's board as an observer ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). In February 2026 a new Anthropic round co-led by ICONIQ and MGX, with the Qatar Investment Authority participating, effectively doubled the company's valuation to $380 billion ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). The firm has also put more than $150 million into OpenAI, via a direct investment and an earlier bet on Statsig, which OpenAI acquired — backing both leading AI labs at once, which partner Matthew Jacobson defended by saying "I don't think their success comes at the cost of each other" ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). The gamble paid off in public: Anthropic began trading on [Nasdaq](/organizations/nasdaq/) on November 19, 2026, under the ticker ANT, in an $81.3 billion IPO priced at $83 a share, closing its first day at $97.17 for a fully diluted market capitalisation of about $1.75 trillion ([Tech Funding News](https://techfundingnews.com/these-vcs-won-the-most-from-anthropics-81-3b-ipo/)). The Information's pre-trading estimates put ICONIQ's cumulative Anthropic investment at more than $6 billion and its stake at about $28 billion, roughly 4.5 times its money, making it the second-largest financial investor in the company by ownership ([Tech Funding News](https://techfundingnews.com/these-vcs-won-the-most-from-anthropics-81-3b-ipo/)).

## Data centers and the IPI sale

Beyond venture capital, ICONIQ built a substantial infrastructure business. IPI Partners, founded in 2016 as a joint venture between ICONIQ and Iron Point, grew into one of the largest private developers and investors in data centers, with $10.5 billion in assets under management, 82 data centers and more than 2.2 gigawatts of leased capacity across the US, EMEA and APAC ([IPE Real Assets](https://realassets.ipe.com/news/blue-owl-expands-digital-infrastructure-strategy-with-1bn-ipi-purchase/10076091.article)). In October 2024 Blue Owl Capital agreed to acquire IPI for about $1 billion — 80% in Blue Owl equity and 20% in cash — from ICONIQ and Iron Point, and to partner with an ICONIQ affiliate to help grow the business ([IPE Real Assets](https://realassets.ipe.com/news/blue-owl-expands-digital-infrastructure-strategy-with-1bn-ipi-purchase/10076091.article)). Makan called it "a privilege to help build IPI over the past eight years" and to earn the trust of the world's largest data-center customers ([IPE Real Assets](https://realassets.ipe.com/news/blue-owl-expands-digital-infrastructure-strategy-with-1bn-ipi-purchase/10076091.article)).

## Secrecy, service, and conflicts

ICONIQ's discretion is itself a selling point — and a source of tension. The firm is famously secretive, repeatedly declines to comment on its client list, has fired people for leaking information, and put its San Francisco office on enhanced security protocol when a Bloomberg reporter visited for a 50-minute meeting ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). It is deliberately selective: it generally takes clients with $25 million in net worth, and in practice its median client holds about $1 billion in assets, because, as Makan put it, "this business is not scalable" ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). For those clients it promises "spine-tingling" service — arranging private jets to evacuate a client from a snowed-in ski resort, exclusive real-estate purchases, a soccer scrimmage with David Beckham, or a film screening attended by Tom Cruise — and more than 100 introductions to potential customers for a startup, sometimes before investing at all ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). The hybrid model also creates the firm's most obvious criticism: as its venture operation grows, it presents conflicts of interest with its core business of managing money. Its filings with the [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](/organizations/us-securities-and-exchange-commission/) mention the term "conflict of interest" 21 times, and the firm says its venture and wealth teams operate with strict confidentiality, so portfolio-company information "never travels across that boundary" ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)). The tension is real enough that co-founder Chad Boeding left in 2018 to launch a competitor that specifically promised not to peddle its own venture or private-equity funds to wealth clients ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/artificial-intelligence/iconiq-go-to-wealth-adviser-for-tech-s-elite-is-putting-billions-into-ai)).

It does not treat the Anthropic stake values as confirmed holdings — about $7 billion (Bloomberg, April 2026) and about $28 billion (The Information, November 2026) are estimates tied to different valuations, and post-IPO selling is not asserted. It does not name ICONIQ's full client list, which the firm does not disclose; the clients named are those reported by Bloomberg. It does not assert the existence or launch date of ICONIQ Impact, or Blue Owl Capital's reported 6% stake in the firm, both of which rest on leads that were not independently verified. And it does not resolve whether Will Griffith counts as a founder: Bloomberg names Makan, Anders and Boeding as founders, and this profile follows that account.
