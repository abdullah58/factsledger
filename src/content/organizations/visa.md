## Overview

**This is not about travel visas.** Visa Inc. is a U.S. payments network company — not a bank, not a card issuer, and not a government document. When you tap a card that says "Visa," your bank issued it and your bank carries the credit risk; Visa runs the network in the middle. Its own SEC filing puts it bluntly: "Visa is not a financial institution and does not issue cards, [extend](/organizations/extend/) credit or set rates and fees for account holders of Visa products" ([Visa 10-Q](https://files.sec2pdf.com/Visa%20Inc.%20(V)/SEC_Filings_PDF/10-Q/20250131-10-Q-VISA%20INC.-0001403161-25-000017.pdf)). The company operates VisaNet, one of the world's largest electronic payments networks — authorization, clearing, and settlement across more than 200 countries and territories — while account-holder and merchant relationships belong to its bank clients ([Visa 10-Q](https://files.sec2pdf.com/Visa%20Inc.%20(V)/SEC_Filings_PDF/10-Q/20250131-10-Q-VISA%20INC.-0001403161-25-000017.pdf)).

That network position is enormously lucrative — global operating income of $18.8 billion at a 64% operating margin in 2022, with $12.3 trillion in total payment volume, according to the Justice Department's 2024 release ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-visa-monopolizing-debit-markets)) — and it is precisely what has made Visa the [U.S. Department of Justice](/organizations/us-department-of-justice/)'s most repeated antitrust target in payments.

## Two lawsuits, one enforcement arc

In November 2020 the DOJ sued to block Visa's planned $5.3 billion acquisition of [Plaid](/organizations/plaid/), alleging that Visa was a monopolist in online debit and that Plaid — "a successful fintech firm" developing a payments platform that would challenge that monopoly — had to be stopped from being bought out before it could succeed ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/visa-and-plaid-abandon-merger-after-antitrust-division-s-suit-block)). Visa and Plaid abandoned the deal in January 2021, and the DOJ filed a Joint Stipulation of Dismissal; the case had been headed for a June 2021 trial ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/visa-and-plaid-abandon-merger-after-antitrust-division-s-suit-block)). NPR later described Visa's view of the takeover as an "insurance policy" against Plaid infringing on its debit business ([NPR](https://www.kdlg.org/as-heard-on-npr/2024-09-24/justice-department-accuses-visa-of-stifling-competition-in-the-debit-card-business)).

Four years later the DOJ came back with a bigger weapon: on September 24, 2024, it filed a civil antitrust lawsuit in the Southern District of New York alleging monopolization and other unlawful conduct in debit network markets under Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-visa-monopolizing-debit-markets)). The complaint alleges that more than 60% of U.S. debit transactions run on Visa's network, letting it charge over $7 billion a year in processing fees; that Visa uses exclusionary agreements to penalize merchants and banks that route transactions elsewhere; and that it pays would-be competitors to stay out — quoting Visa's then-CFO: "Everybody is a friend and partner. Nobody is a competitor" ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-visa-monopolizing-debit-markets)). "We allege that Visa has unlawfully amassed the power to extract fees that far exceed what it could charge in a competitive market," said Attorney General Merrick Garland ([justice.gov](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-visa-monopolizing-debit-markets)).

Visa called the suit meritless and vowed a vigorous defense, arguing it is one competitor among many in an expanding payments universe ([NPR](https://www.kdlg.org/as-heard-on-npr/2024-09-24/justice-department-accuses-visa-of-stifling-competition-in-the-debit-card-business)). But in June 2025, U.S. District Judge John Koeltl denied Visa's motion to dismiss, ruling that Visa was asking for "the premature resolution of factual issues" and finding the government's allegations plausible; all three of Visa's dismissal arguments were rejected and the litigation continues ([Payments Dive](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/visa-loses-bid-to-toss-us-debit-lawsuit-DOJ-antitrust-payments-cards/751607/)). Per the DOJ complaint, Americans make more than $4 trillion in debit purchases a year, and Visa's debit business is so large that in 2022 it earned more from debit than from credit cards ([Payments Dive](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/visa-loses-bid-to-toss-us-debit-lawsuit-DOJ-antitrust-payments-cards/751607/)).

## The rival, the origins, and the acquisition that went through

Visa's closest network rival is [Mastercard](/organizations/mastercard/) — a distant second in U.S. debit, with less than a quarter of the market to Visa's 60%+ ([Payments Dive](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/visa-loses-bid-to-toss-us-debit-lawsuit-DOJ-antitrust-payments-cards/751607/)). As NPR noted, "Visa — and to a lesser extent Mastercard — continues to dominate the market," even though banks issuing debit cards have been required since late 2011 to let merchants route transactions over multiple networks ([NPR](https://www.kdlg.org/as-heard-on-npr/2024-09-24/justice-department-accuses-visa-of-stifling-competition-in-the-debit-card-business)).

Visa began as a Bank of America project: BankAmericard, launched in 1958 as a paper card with a $300 credit limit, turned over to issuing banks by 1970, and renamed Visa in 1976 — "a simple name that sounds the same in every language" ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2015/12/14/visa-5-fun-facts-you-ought-to-know.aspx?source=eptfxblnk0000004)). Until early 2008 it was owned by its member banks; its $17.9 billion IPO was then the largest in history ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2015/12/14/visa-5-fun-facts-you-ought-to-know.aspx?source=eptfxblnk0000004)).

Not every Visa acquisition gets blocked. The same 10-Q that disclaims banking discloses that "in December 2024, Visa acquired Featurespace Limited, a developer of real-time artificial intelligence payments protection technology that prevents and mitigates payments fraud and financial crime risks, for a purchase consideration of $946 million" ([Visa 10-Q](https://files.sec2pdf.com/Visa%20Inc.%20(V)/SEC_Filings_PDF/10-Q/20250131-10-Q-VISA%20INC.-0001403161-25-000017.pdf)). And in September 2026, Visa joined Mastercard and [Stripe](/organizations/stripe/) as equal founding partners and investors in Open Standard, the company behind the Open USD stablecoin, with the five founding partners committing more than $1 billion in liquidity ([CoinDesk](https://Www.coindesk.com/business/2026/09/24/open-usd-takes-on-tether-circle-with-a-different-stablecoin-model-that-s-building-money)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim the DOJ's allegations are proven — they are allegations in ongoing litigation, and Visa denies them; the June 2025 ruling decided only that the case could proceed, not its merits. It does not claim Visa sets consumer interest rates or fees — the company's filing explicitly disclaims that. The $9.51 billion revenue figure cited is quarterly (quarter ended December 31, 2024), not annual. The BankAmericard history rests on investment-press reporting (tier3), corroborated by the company's own account quoted therein; it is treated as supporting detail, not a load-bearing claim. The profile does not speculate on the outcome of the 2024 suit, Visa's CEO succession, or whether regulators will act on Open Standard. "Visa" here means only Visa Inc., the payments network — not a travel visa.
