## Overview

Al-Shabaab is a Somali militant Islamist group and an official al-Qaeda affiliate that has fought the government of Somalia since 2006 ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)). It began as the militant wing of the Islamic Courts Union, which briefly controlled southern Somalia in 2006, and survived the movement's collapse to become the country's most durable insurgent force ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)). Since the end of 2006 it has waged a violent insurgency using guerrilla warfare and terrorist tactics against Somalia's governments, and has carried out attacks in Somalia, Kenya, Uganda, and Djibouti ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)). The [United States](/places/united-states/) designated it a Foreign Terrorist Organization on 18 March 2008 and a Specially Designated Global Terrorist the next day, blocking its property under US jurisdiction and barring US persons from transacting with it ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)).

## Origins and rise

The group emerged from the Islamic Courts Union (ICU), a coalition of eleven sharia courts formed in June 2004 that named Sharif Sheikh Ahmed its leader ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)). Backed by al-Shabaab militants, the ICU wrested control of Mogadishu from a coalition of warlords in June 2006 ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)). That December, Ethiopian forces invaded and took the capital with little ICU opposition, and the ICU disbanded — but al-Shabaab remained active, galvanised by the invasion into what the Council on Foreign Relations describes as the most powerful Somali guerrilla group, well funded and thousands strong ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)). The [United Nations](/organizations/united-nations/) approved the African Union force AMISOM in February 2007 to support the transitional government against it, and the United States designated the group a foreign terrorist organization in February 2008 ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)).

Al-Shabaab formalised its place in the global jihadist network in February 2012, when al-Qaeda chief Ayman al-Zawahiri announced its affiliation with al-Qaeda ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)). Rewards for Justice describes it as an official al-Qaeda affiliate with ties to other affiliates, including al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula and al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)). The group suffered territorial setbacks in 2011–2012, when AMISOM and government forces pushed it out of Mogadishu and Kenyan forces retook the port city of Kismayo, cutting off a major funding source ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)). Leadership changed in September 2014, when Ahmed Abdi Godane, one of the group's founders, was killed in a US air strike and Ahmed Diriye — also known as Ahmed Umar Abu Ubaidah — succeeded him ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)).

## Attacks beyond Somalia

Al-Shabaab's first attack outside Somalia came on 11 July 2010, when suicide bombings in Kampala, Uganda, during the World Cup killed 76 people, including a US citizen ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)). Its highest-profile regional attack followed in September 2013: a multi-day siege of the Westgate Mall in Nairobi, Kenya, that killed at least 65 civilians as well as six soldiers and police officers, with hundreds more injured ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)). In April 2015 it raided Garissa University College in Kenya, killing 148 people ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)).

Inside Somalia, the deadliest single incident was the October 2017 Mogadishu truck bombings, which killed more than 500 people and injured 300 others; al-Shabaab is widely believed to have been behind the attack, though it did not claim responsibility ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)). The group also continued attacks into 2019–2020, including a December 2019 suicide truck bombing in Mogadishu that killed 82 people and an attack on a Kenyan military base housing US troops that killed three US personnel ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)).

## Designation and sanctions

The designation history is the part of this profile most relevant to sanctions and compliance work. The US State Department designated al-Shabaab a Foreign Terrorist Organization on 18 March 2008 under section 219 of the Immigration and Nationality Act, and as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist on 19 March 2008 under Executive Order 13224 ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)). As a result, all of its property and interests in property subject to US jurisdiction are blocked, US persons are generally prohibited from engaging in transactions with it, and it is a crime to knowingly provide, or attempt or conspire to provide, material support or resources to the group ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)). The US Department of the Treasury has separately designated individual al-Shabaab leaders under the same counterterrorism authorities.

At the United Nations, the sanctions regime was refocused onto the group itself in December 2023: Security Council Resolution 2713 imposed a new arms embargo on Somalia that exempted the federal government and its security forces, and renamed the Sanctions Committee and Panel of Experts from "pursuant to resolution 751 (1992)" (Somalia) to "pursuant to resolution 2713 (2023)" (Al-Shabaab) ([UN Sanctions App](https://unsanctionsapp.com/cases/somalia/episodes/somalia-ep-6)). The same day, Resolution 2714 terminated the general arms embargo on Somalia that had been in place since 1992 ([UN Sanctions App](https://unsanctionsapp.com/cases/somalia/episodes/somalia-ep-6)). Resolution 2713 also noted with concern al-Shabaab's ability to generate revenue and launder, store, and transfer resources, and added notification requirements for improvised-explosive-device components ([UN Sanctions App](https://unsanctionsapp.com/cases/somalia/episodes/somalia-ep-6)).

## Financing and the current fight

The financing picture explains the group's durability. The UN Panel of Experts estimated al-Shabaab's total financial income for 2023 at over USD 150 million, within a 20% margin of its 2019 income of USD 163 million, and concluded the group generates "more than enough revenue to fund its insurgency through checkpoints and the extortions of businesses" with "an uninterrupted illicit supply of weapons" ([UN Sanctions App](https://unsanctionsapp.com/cases/somalia/episodes/somalia-ep-6)). That solid financial base is the citation-magnet fact of this profile: a stat, its source, and its implication — sanctions and territorial losses have not broken the group's funding model.

On the battlefield, the African Union mission changed shape twice in three years. AMISOM became the transition mission ATMIS in April 2022, set to hand security responsibilities to Somali forces at the end of 2024 ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)). The Security Council then authorised its successor, the African Union Support and Stabilisation Mission in Somalia (AUSSOM), on 27 December 2024 in Resolution 2767 ([UN Sanctions App](https://unsanctionsapp.com/cases/somalia/episodes/somalia-ep-6)). Despite territorial losses in 2022 and early 2023, al-Shabaab turned to guerrilla tactics, made increasing use of improvised explosive devices in 2024, and launched a major offensive in February 2025 that regained territory before the front returned to stalemate ([UN Sanctions App](https://unsanctionsapp.com/cases/somalia/episodes/somalia-ep-6)). The 2025 Panel of Experts concluded al-Shabaab "remains an adaptable, opportunistic, and flexible organization capable of shifting its approach to retain influence in Somalia" ([UN Sanctions App](https://unsanctionsapp.com/cases/somalia/episodes/somalia-ep-6)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is al-Shabaab?**
Al-Shabaab is a Somali militant Islamist group and official al-Qaeda affiliate that has fought Somalia's government since 2006. It began as the militant wing of the Islamic Courts Union and seeks to overthrow the federal government, expel foreign forces, and establish a fundamentalist Islamic state ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)).

**When did the United States designate al-Shabaab a terrorist organization?**
The State Department designated it a Foreign Terrorist Organization on 18 March 2008 and a Specially Designated Global Terrorist on 19 March 2008 under Executive Order 13224. The designations block its US-jurisdiction property and bar US persons from transacting with it ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)).

**Who leads al-Shabaab?**
Ahmed Diriye, also known as Ahmed Umar Abu Ubaidah, has led the group since September 2014, when founder Ahmed Abdi Godane was killed in a US air strike ([Council on Foreign Relations](https://www.cfr.org/articles/al-shabaab-in-east-africa)).

**Where has al-Shabaab carried out attacks?**
It has conducted attacks in Somalia, Kenya, Uganda, and Djibouti, including the 2010 Kampala bombings, the 2013 Westgate Mall siege in Nairobi, and the 2015 Garissa University College raid in Kenya ([Rewards for Justice](https://rewardsforjustice.net/rewards/al-shabaab/)).

**How is al-Shabaab funded?**
Mainly through checkpoints and extortion of businesses. The UN Panel of Experts estimated its 2023 income at over USD 150 million, roughly in line with its 2019 income of USD 163 million ([UN Sanctions App](https://unsanctionsapp.com/cases/somalia/episodes/somalia-ep-6)).
