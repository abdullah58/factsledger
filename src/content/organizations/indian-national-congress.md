## Overview

**The Indian National Congress (INC, the Congress Party)** is a broadly based political party of [India](/places/india/), founded in 1885, that dominated the movement for independence from Great Britain and subsequently formed most of India's governments from independence until recent decades ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). Since 2014 it has been out of power at the central government level, and it is now the principal opposition party: in the 2024 general election it nearly doubled its [Lok Sabha](/organizations/lok-sabha/) standing to 99 seats as the most prominent party in the INDIA opposition alliance, which won 234 seats ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12686)). This profile is about the Indian National Congress, a political party — not the United States Congress, the federal legislature of the [United States](/places/united-states/), and not a legislature of any kind.

The Congress Party's arc is the longest in Indian politics: from an elite debating society of 72 delegates in Bombay to the mass movement that won independence, to the dominant party of the republic's first half-century, to a party that in 2019 held just 52 Lok Sabha seats — short even of the 55 required to officially lead the opposition — before its partial recovery in 2024 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress); [Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12686)). Its story is inseparable from the Nehru-Gandhi family, which supplied three of its prime ministers — though, as Britannica notes, the Nehru-Gandhi family is not related to Mahatma Gandhi: Indira Gandhi's surname came from her husband, Feroze Gandhi ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)).

## From moderate reform to mass movement

The Congress was founded by several Indian nationalist leaders, including Dadabhai Naoroji and Surendranath Banerjee, together with Allan Octavian Hume, a British official in the Indian civil service, and first convened in December 1885 in Bombay (now Mumbai), with 72 members and W. C. Bonnerjee as president ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). During its first several decades the party passed fairly moderate reform resolutions, seeking constitutional changes within the colonial framework through meetings, petitions, and press campaigns ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). The moderates nonetheless built a damaging economic critique of colonialism: exploitative British policies combined with famines and epidemics had reduced India to a supplier of raw materials to England and a consumer of imported finished goods, and the "drain of wealth" through ruinous taxation and transfers of income to England was detailed in the writings of Naoroji, Romesh Chunder Dutt, and Dinshaw Wacha ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)).

The party's transformation into a nationwide movement began with the partition of Bengal (1905–11), when an "extremist" faction — the "Lal Bal Pal" trio of Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, and Bipin Chandra Pal, plus Annie Besant — endorsed *swadeshi* ("of our own country"), calling on Indians to boycott imported British goods and promote Indian-made goods ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). Disagreements between extremists and moderates led by Gopal Krishna Gokhale culminated in an acrimonious session at Surat in 1907, where shoes and chairs were thrown and the session was suspended; the factions reconciled at the 1915 session in Bombay, and Besant — who had started the Home Rule League in 1916 — became the party's first woman president in 1917 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). In the 1920s and '30s the party, led by Mahatma Gandhi, advocated nonviolent noncooperation, precipitated by the Rowlatt Act of early 1919 and outrage at the massacre of civilians at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar that April; notable acts included the Salt March of 1930 led by Gandhi, organized through the All India Congress Committee, formed in 1929 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). The party's goal evolved from constitutional reform to dominion status, articulated in the Nehru Report of 1928, and then to Purna Swaraj ("Complete Self-Rule"), resolved at Lahore in 1929 and made public on 26 January 1930 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). When World War II began in 1939, Britain made India a belligerent without consulting Indian elected councils; the Congress sponsored the mass civil disobedience of the Quit India Movement in 1942, and British authorities imprisoned the entire Congress leadership, many until 1945 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). The British Parliament passed an independence bill in July 1947, independence followed the next month, and the subcontinent was partitioned into a Hindu-majority India and a Muslim-majority Pakistan ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)).

## Dominance, splits, and the Emergency

From 1951 until his death in 1964, Jawaharlal Nehru, the first prime minister of India, dominated the Congress Party, which won overwhelming victories in the elections of 1951–52, 1957, and 1962 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). The party united in 1964 to elect Lal Bahadur Shastri and in 1966 Indira Gandhi, Nehru's daughter, as party leader and thus prime minister ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). In 1967 Indira Gandhi faced open revolt, and in 1969 she was expelled by a group called the "Syndicate," led by K. Kamaraj and Morarji Desai, which formed Congress (Organisation); Gandhi's New Congress, also called Congress (Requisitionists), scored a landslide victory in the 1971 elections, leaving it unclear for a period which party was the rightful heir to the Indian National Congress label ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). In the mid-1970s the New Congress Party's support fractured: from 1975 Gandhi's government grew increasingly authoritarian, and the Emergency — 21 months in which the Constitution was suspended — was declared in June 1975 and severely criticized for the curtailment of civil liberties ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). In the March 1977 elections the opposition Janata Party scored a landslide, winning 295 out of 544 seats in the Lok Sabha against 154 for Congress, and Gandhi herself lost to her Janata opponent ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)).

On 2 January 1978, Gandhi and her followers seceded and formed Congress (I) — the "I" signifying Indira — which attracted enough legislators to become the official opposition, and in 1981 the national election commission declared it to be the "real" Indian National Congress (the "I" designation was dropped in 1996) ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). In November 1979 Gandhi regained a parliamentary seat, and the following year she was again elected prime minister; her son Rajiv Gandhi became nominal head of the party in 1982, and upon her assassination in October 1984 he became prime minister and led Congress to an overwhelming victory of 401 seats that December ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). Although Congress remained the largest party in Parliament in 1989, Rajiv Gandhi was unseated as prime minister by a coalition of opposition parties; while campaigning to regain power in May 1991 he was assassinated by a suicide bomber associated with the Tamil Tigers, a separatist group in Sri Lanka, and P. V. Narasimha Rao was elected prime minister in June 1991 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress)). The party has traditionally supported socialist economic policies within a mixed economy, but in the 1990s it endorsed market reforms including privatization and deregulation; it also supported secular policies encouraging equal rights for all citizens, including those in lower castes ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress/Policy-and-structure)).

## The long fall and the mechanics of survival: 2004–2024

The Congress Party's modern history is a slow reversal: a return to power in 2004, a decade of government, a collapse to its worst-ever result in 2014, a near-death experience in 2019, and a partial recovery in 2024. In 2004 Congress raised its Lok Sabha representation to 145 seats and formed a coalition, the United Progressive Alliance (UPA), after Sonia Gandhi declined the premiership and recommended Manmohan Singh; in 2009 it increased its total to 206, and Singh formed a second UPA cabinet, becoming the first prime minister since Nehru to win a second term after a full five-year first term ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/India/Congress-Party-rule-under-Manmohan-Singh)). Singh's government pursued a pro-farmer agenda and rural programs, presided over firsts — Kocheril Raman Narayanan, a Dalit, had served as president of India in 1997–2002; Pratibha Patil became the country's first woman president in 2007; Meira Kumar became the first woman Speaker of the Lok Sabha in 2009 — and negotiated free-trade agreements with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) and South Korea in 2010 and a trade pact with Japan in 2011 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/India/Congress-Party-rule-under-Manmohan-Singh)). But its second term was marked by a string of corruption scandals that implicated government officials, including Singh himself in 2013, and by declining growth and rising inflation; in 2013 Congress lost key state elections including Delhi, long a stronghold ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/India/Congress-Party-rule-under-Manmohan-Singh)). In 2014 Congress won only 44 seats in the Lok Sabha, with UPA partners contributing just 16 more, while the BJP won 282 outright and [Narendra Modi](/people/narendra-modi/) became prime minister ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/India/Congress-Party-rule-under-Manmohan-Singh)). Even a 2018 bright spot — Congress winning the largest share of seats in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, and Chhattisgarh and picking up more than 100 state assembly seats across five states — did not translate nationally: in 2019 Congress won only 52 seats ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/India/Congress-Party-rule-under-Manmohan-Singh); [Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12686)).

What kept the party alive as an organization through that decade is less covered than its defeats, and it is the information-gain core of this profile: structure, internal elections, and vote efficiency. Structurally, Congress is hierarchical: delegates from state and district parties attend an annual national conference, which elects a president and the All India Congress Committee — but the 20-member Congress Working Committee, the majority of whose members are appointed by the party president, wields enormous influence ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress/Policy-and-structure)). The party's membership fell from nearly 40 million in the mid-1990s to under 20 million at the beginning of the 21st century, mirroring its declining fortunes ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress/Policy-and-structure)). Its internal democracy is real but constrained: in October 2022 the party held a contested presidential election in which Mallikarjun Kharge, an 80-year-old veteran, defeated Shashi Tharoor by 7,897 votes (84.14 percent) to 1,072 (11.4 percent), with 9,385 votes polled and 416 invalid — becoming the first non-Gandhi president in more than two decades after Sonia Gandhi's interim tenure since 2019, when Rahul Gandhi stepped down ([Frontline](https://frontline.thehindu.com/news/mallikarjun-kharge-elected-congress-president-with-massive-majority/article66031032.ece)). Yet the electoral college comprised Pradesh Congress Committee delegates nominated by the party president, and Tharoor's camp had written to the party's central election authority alleging irregularities in polling in Uttar Pradesh before the count — a letter that was leaked to the press and that the election authority declined to discuss publicly ([Frontline](https://frontline.thehindu.com/news/mallikarjun-kharge-elected-congress-president-with-massive-majority/article66031032.ece)). The 2024 recovery then showed the arithmetic of survival under first-past-the-post voting: Congress raised its vote share only 1.7 points, from 19.5 percent in 2019 to 21.2 percent in 2024, yet nearly doubled its seats to 99 — because its voters were spread efficiently enough to win seats with smaller margins, even though 21.2 percent of votes still yielded only 18.2 percent of seats ([Firstpost](https://www.firstpost.com/explainers/2024-lok-sabha-election-results-bjp-vote-share-seats-voter-turnout-13779061.html)). That combination — a shrunken but still national organization, a genuinely contested but centrally managed internal election, and vote distribution efficient enough to convert a fifth of the vote into nearly a fifth of Parliament — implies that Congress's floor as a national party is organizational rather than electoral: it survives where its committees still function, not where its vote share peaks.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the Indian National Congress?**
The Indian National Congress is one of two major political parties in India. Founded in 1885, it led the movement for independence from Great Britain and dominated much of the republic's early politics; since 2014 it has been out of power at the central government level and is now the principal opposition party, with 99 Lok Sabha seats after the 2024 election.

**Is the Indian National Congress the same as the U.S. Congress?**
No. The Indian National Congress is a political party in India. The United States Congress is the federal legislature of the United States — a government institution, not a party. The shared word "Congress" reflects 19th-century naming conventions, not any institutional relationship, and the two should never be confused.

**Is the Congress Party's Gandhi family related to Mahatma Gandhi?**
No. The Nehru-Gandhi family — Jawaharlal Nehru, his daughter Indira Gandhi, her son Rajiv Gandhi, and Rajiv's wife Sonia Gandhi — is not related to Mahatma Gandhi. Indira Gandhi's surname came from her husband, Feroze Gandhi, who was not related to the Mahatma, though Nehru and Indira were his close associates.

**Who leads the Congress Party now?**
Mallikarjun Kharge has been party president since October 2022, when he defeated Shashi Tharoor in a contested internal election by 7,897 votes to 1,072. He is the first non-Gandhi president in more than two decades, succeeding Sonia Gandhi, who had served as interim president since 2019 after Rahul Gandhi stepped down.

**Why did Congress recover in 2024 after winning only 52 seats in 2019?**
Its vote share rose only modestly, from 19.5 percent to 21.2 percent, but under first-past-the-post voting that was enough to nearly double its seats to 99, because its voters were spread efficiently across constituencies. Congress also contested as the most prominent party in the INDIA opposition alliance, which won 234 seats collectively.
