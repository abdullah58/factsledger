## Overview

Compugraphic Corporation was the American phototypesetting company that made "cold type" cheap enough for small newspapers — and then, as the dominant incumbent of that market, turned away the young [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/) and lost the next revolution to its underdog rival ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Founded in 1960 by Bill Garth, a former president of Photon, with the aim of building small, inexpensive typesetting systems ([Prepressure](https://www.prepressure.com/prepress/history/events-1960-1965)), it rose by undercutting hot-metal typesetting, and fell when it refused the digital-font deal that [Linotype](/organizations/linotype/) accepted instead. Agfa acquired it in 1988 ([Reference for Business](https://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/21/Agfa-Gevaert-Group-N-V.html)).

Phototypesetting — setting type by exposing film rather than casting molten metal — was already replacing the Linotype machine when Compugraphic found its opening: price. Its first typesetting computers, the Linasec I and II of 1963, prepared punched tape to drive the Linotype machines newspapers already owned, with design and initial manufacturing outsourced to Wang Laboratories ([Prepressure](https://www.prepressure.com/prepress/history/events-1960-1965)). From there the company moved to machines that set type directly, without tape, and aimed them at the thousands of small papers that could never afford the established systems.

## The small-paper machine

The local-newspaper record shows what that meant in practice. In Watervliet, Michigan, the Tri-City Record's history records that by the mid-1960s "the Compugraphic phototypesetting system had been devised, becoming much less cumbersome, and more efficient than lead type", and that owner Gordon Banasik "bought one of these machines, sight unseen" ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecord.com/post/the-tri-city-record-creating-and-capturing-history-for-tomorrow-as-current-owners-celebrate-40-years)). It carried serial number 32, and with it the Record became "the very first newspaper to use this new direct entry typesetting equipment in Southwest Michigan" ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecord.com/post/the-tri-city-record-creating-and-capturing-history-for-tomorrow-as-current-owners-celebrate-40-years)). Two decades later the same paper replaced its pioneer machine with two Editwriters, computerized phototypesetters storing copy on large floppy disks ([Tri-City Record](https://www.tricityrecord.com/post/the-tri-city-record-creating-and-capturing-history-for-tomorrow-as-current-owners-celebrate-40-years)).

At Princeton in 1979, the arithmetic was a lease, not a purchase. The Nassau Weekly's founders launched their alternative paper on a Compugraphic EditWriter 7500 — "a $50,000 digital typesetter" the size of two file cabinets and an old-fashioned television, "leasable for about $800 a month" ([Nassau Weekly](https://nassauweekly.com/the-compugraphic-editwriter-7500/)). As the paper's designer later wrote, "anyone who could sell enough advertising to pay for the lease could start a newspaper" ([Nassau Weekly](https://nassauweekly.com/the-compugraphic-editwriter-7500/)). That sentence is Compugraphic's real-world story in miniature: a technology company that lowered the entry price of publishing itself, a decade before desktop publishing lowered it again.

## The refusal that reversed the market

By the early 1980s Compugraphic was the company to beat, and that was precisely its problem. Adobe co-founder Charles Geschke told Wharton that Adobe went to Compugraphic first for font licensing "because they were an American company and we thought that would be easier" — and found the incumbent unmoved: "They were very arrogant. They had the lion's share of the market at the time" ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). The context mattered: as Geschke explained of the old equipment market, switching suppliers was a major undertaking "because nobody's equipment was compatible" — customers were locked in, so the incumbent felt no pressure to move ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

Linotype, needing an edge, agreed instead. Geschke's account of the reversal is stark: "Within about two years they had the lion's share of the business in the United States because they had [PostScript](/creative-works/postscript/) compatibility. Compugraphic had to sell itself to Agfa and basically went out of business" ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). The business-history record dates that end to 1988: Agfa's acquisition of the United States' Compugraphic, "a manufacturer of electronic pre-press equipment", after which its Wilmington headquarters "became the home base for much of Agfa's American operations, renamed Agfa Corporation" ([Reference for Business](https://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/21/Agfa-Gevaert-Group-N-V.html)). Compugraphic had helped kill hot metal; desktop publishing, built on the PostScript compatibility it declined, then killed phototypesetting in turn.

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not resolve two conflicts in the record. First, the Agfa year: Reference for Business says 1988, while tertiary summaries say 1981 with the brand removed by 1988 — the profile follows the tier2 business history and flags the other date as unresolved, since Wharton gives no date. Second, the headquarters state: Reference for Business says Wilmington, Delaware, while tertiary summaries say Wilmington, Massachusetts; no fully-read independent source in this pass confirms the state, so the profile says only "Wilmington". It does not present Geschke's "arrogant" and "lion's share" characterisations as measured market data; they are his account of a negotiation he participated in. It does not claim a precise founding incorporation date, which rests on a specialist history site, and it does not confuse Compugraphic with the unrelated British photomask company Compugraphics — a different company with a similar name, which this profile does not cover.
