## Overview

The Rail Safety and Standards Board (RSSB) is the independent safety, standards, and research body for Great Britain's rail network — a not-for-profit company, owned by its industry members, that writes the standards, runs the safety data, and funds the research the whole railway relies on ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/); [Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/Rail-News/business-performance-and-safety-go-hand-in-hand)). It was established in April 2003, implementing a recommendation of the public inquiry into the 1999 Ladbroke Grove crash, in which 31 people died and over 258 were seriously injured ([RSSB](https://www.rssb.co.uk/about-rssb/insights-and-news/news/Remembering-the-Ladbroke-Grove-tragedy)). Its most visible products are the Railway Group Standards and the Rule Book — the technical and operating rules for the main heavy-rail network — and its least visible is the Safety Management Information System, the national database where the industry records safety events ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/); [Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/Rail-News/business-performance-and-safety-go-hand-in-hand)). After the 2008 Barrow upon Soar derailment, the Rail Accident Investigation Branch turned to the RSSB with a design question: examine how trains could be designed to mitigate the consequences of derailment ([Barrow upon Soar rail crash](/events/barrow-upon-soar-rail-crash/)).

The RSSB is deliberately not a regulator. It manages the standards process and owns the outputs on behalf of the industry, but enforcement belongs to the Office of Rail Regulation ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/)). That separation — standards here, enforcement there, investigation at the RAIB — was the Cullen inquiry's answer to a railway in which the infrastructure owner had previously run its own safety regime.

## Born from Ladbroke Grove

On 5 October 1999, a passenger train bound for Bedwyn passed a signal at danger near Ladbroke Grove and collided head-on with a train heading for Paddington; 31 people, including both drivers, were killed and over 258 were seriously injured ([RSSB](https://www.rssb.co.uk/about-rssb/insights-and-news/news/Remembering-the-Ladbroke-Grove-tragedy)). The tragedy prompted a public inquiry led by Lord Cullen, whose reports highlighted signal sighting, train protection, driver training, and learning from operational experience, as well as deeper structural weaknesses in the industry's approach to safety and standards ([RSSB](https://www.rssb.co.uk/about-rssb/insights-and-news/news/Remembering-the-Ladbroke-Grove-tragedy)). One outcome was a reorganisation of who did what: the formal responsibilities for managing and regulating railway safety were separated, and an independent industry safety and standards body — the RSSB — was created in 2003 ([RSSB](https://www.rssb.co.uk/about-rssb/insights-and-news/news/Remembering-the-Ladbroke-Grove-tragedy)).

The context mattered. Before the RSSB, British Rail had a Safety and Standards Directorate, and after privatisation Railtrack adopted its own Safety and Standards Directorate, independent of its line activities ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/)). Having the infrastructure operator run the industry-wide safety organisation caused tensions that came to a head after Ladbroke Grove, when the deputy prime minister announced Railtrack was being stripped of its safety role ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/)). Lord Cullen recommended a new independent safety body to provide safety leadership and to set and review industry standards, and a temporary Railway Safety organisation bridged the gap until the RSSB was established in April 2003 ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/)). The RSSB was formally established to lead and facilitate the rail industry's efforts to meet its obligations under the Health and Safety at Work Act ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/Rail-News/business-performance-and-safety-go-hand-in-hand)).

## What the RSSB actually does

The RSSB's original roles were defined by the Office of Rail Regulation: manage Railway Group Standards on behalf of the industry; lead the development of long-term safety strategy, including annual Railway Strategic Safety Plans; facilitate change by leading a research programme and providing education and awareness; support cross-industry groups working on national programmes addressing major safety concerns; and facilitate effective representation of the UK rail industry in European legislation and standards ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/)). It builds industry-wide consensus and facilitates resolution of difficult cross-industry issues, providing knowledge, analysis, technical expertise, information, and risk management tools ([Railway Gazette International](https://www.railwaygazette.com/data/premium/rail-safety-standards-board-ltd-rssb/)). Its membership spans the industry: Network Rail and other infrastructure managers, train operating companies, freight operating companies, rolling stock leasing companies, infrastructure contractors, and suppliers are full members, with affiliates and partner universities alongside ([Railway-News](https://railway-news.com/associations/rail-safety-and-standards-board-uk-rssb/)).

The data work is the engine room. Incident reports and research feed the Safety Management Information System (SMIS), the national web-based system used to record all safety-related events on Network Rail-managed infrastructure, and that data feeds the Safety Risk Model and the Precursor Indicator Model, which analyse potential situations before they become accidents ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/); [Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/Rail-News/business-performance-and-safety-go-hand-in-hand)). The RSSB publishes the collated statistics in the Strategic Safety Plan and a detailed Annual Safety Performance Report, plus monthly, quarterly, and half-year reports ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/Rail-News/business-performance-and-safety-go-hand-in-hand)). Its research and development programme, funded primarily by the Department for Transport, covers systems issues across the whole railway, and the Department later added £15 million over five and a half years for a strategic research programme ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/Rail-News/business-performance-and-safety-go-hand-in-hand)).

Two early examples show the method. When campaigners pressed for seat belts on trains, RSSB research showed trains were safer without them, provided carriages were fitted with laminated glass rather than toughened glass to contain passengers in an accident — and all new trains now have laminated glass ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/)). When a noise-abatement campaign over train horns threatened to cost the industry £20 million, RSSB research and analysis showed alternative action could be taken without spending such sums ([Rail UK](https://railuk.com/people/the-rssb-what-do-they-do/)). In both cases, the RSSB's contribution was the same: evidence that let the industry spend money where it actually reduced risk.

The same consensus method shaped Taking Safe Decisions, published by the RSSB in June 2008. The document was the product of research, analysis, and consultation, developed by a think tank of industry experts and approved through the Safety Policy Group and the RSSB board ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/Rail-News/business-performance-and-safety-go-hand-in-hand)). It describes the industry consensus on how decisions affecting safety should be taken: to meet legal requirements, or because they are sensible from a commercial perspective ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/Rail-News/business-performance-and-safety-go-hand-in-hand)). It also clarifies that societal concern about risk should not be taken into account by duty holders deciding whether a measure is necessary to ensure safety so far as is reasonably practicable, though it may matter optionally for business reasons such as reputation ([Rail Technology Magazine](https://www.railtechnologymagazine.com/Rail-News/business-performance-and-safety-go-hand-in-hand)).

## The Barrow recommendation

The RSSB's connection to Barrow upon Soar is a single recommendation. After the 1 February 2008 derailment, the Rail Accident Investigation Branch recommended that Network Rail assess risks during road-vehicle transit operations near the running line and review protection arrangements for work on or near it — and separately recommended that the RSSB examine designing trains to mitigate the consequences of derailment ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar)). The derailment itself was caused by a tipper lorry's raised body demolishing a footbridge onto the line; the train, travelling at 65 mph, could not stop before striking the debris ([RAIB](https://www.gov.uk/raib-reports/collision-of-a-train-with-a-demolished-footbridge-barrow-upon-soar)). The recommendation asked the RSSB to look beyond the immediate cause — the lorry and the missing risk process — to the crashworthiness question: when a derailment does happen, how can train design limit the harm. ## Frequently asked questions

**What is the RSSB?**
The Rail Safety and Standards Board is the independent safety, standards, and research body for Great Britain's railways. It is a not-for-profit, member-owned company that manages Railway Group Standards and the Rule Book, runs the industry's safety data systems, and leads its research programme.

**When and why was the RSSB created?**
It was established in April 2003, implementing a recommendation of Lord Cullen's public inquiry into the 1999 Ladbroke Grove crash. The inquiry found structural weaknesses in how the industry handled safety, and the RSSB was created as an independent body to lead safety and standards work.

**Is the RSSB the rail regulator?**
No. The RSSB manages standards and owns the outputs on behalf of the industry, but it does not enforce them. Enforcement belongs to the Office of Rail Regulation, and accident investigation belongs to the Rail Accident Investigation Branch. The three roles are deliberately separate.

**What did the RSSB have to do with the Barrow upon Soar crash?**
After the 2008 derailment, the RAIB recommended that the RSSB examine designing trains to mitigate the consequences of derailment. The recommendation concerned future train design, not blame for the accident, which the investigation attributed to the lorry driver and Network Rail's risk process.
