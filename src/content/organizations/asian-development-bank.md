## Overview

The Asian Development Bank (ADB) is a Manila-headquartered multilateral development bank that finances economic and social development across Asia and the Pacific. Founded in 1966 and owned by 69 member countries, it lends to governments and the private sector through loans, grants, equity investments, guarantees, and technical assistance ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/videos/responding-asia-and-pacific-s-development-challenges)), providing a record $44 billion in development finance in 2025 ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/masato-kanda-begins-new-term-adb-president-pledges-stronger-regional-links)).

This is the regional bank for Asia and the Pacific — not the [World Bank](/organizations/world-bank/), the separate global development lender, and not the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, a separate institution that began operations in Beijing in 2016 with an infrastructure-focused mission ([Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank](https://www.aiib.org/en/what-we-do/infrastructure-for-tomorrow/index-m.html)). ADB's own mission is a prosperous, inclusive, resilient, and sustainable Asia and the Pacific, while sustaining its efforts to eradicate extreme poverty ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/who-we-are/about)).

## Origins and history

ADB was conceived in the early 1960s as a financial institution that would be Asian in character and foster economic growth and cooperation in one of the poorest regions in the world. A resolution at the first Ministerial Conference on Asian Economic Cooperation, held by the [United Nations](/organizations/united-nations/) Economic Commission for Asia and the Far East in 1963, set that vision on its way to becoming reality, and Manila was chosen to host the new institution, which opened on 19 December 1966 with 31 members under its first President, Takeshi Watanabe ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/who-we-are/about)). Its early work focused on food production and rural development; it made its first bond issue in Asia in 1970 — $16.7 million, issued in [Japan](/places/japan/) — and established the Asian Development Fund in 1974 to provide low-interest loans to its poorest members, opening its first field office in Bangladesh in 1982 ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/who-we-are/about)).

Crises repeatedly reshaped the bank. In 1995 it became the first multilateral organization with a Board-approved governance policy, and during the 1997 Asian financial crisis it approved its largest single loan, a $4 billion emergency loan to the Republic of Korea. Poverty reduction became its overarching goal in 1999, and it committed more than $850 million for recovery after the December 2004 tsunami in [India](/places/india/), Indonesia, Maldives, and Sri Lanka, plus a $1 billion line of assistance after the October 2005 earthquake in [Pakistan](/places/pakistan/) ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/who-we-are/about)). In 2009 its Board of Governors agreed to triple its capital base from $55 billion to $165 billion, and in January 2017 it merged the lending operations of the Asian Development Fund and its ordinary capital resources, lifting annual lending and grant approvals by 50% ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/who-we-are/about)).

## Scale, regions and strategy

In 2024 ADB committed $24.3 billion through loans, grants, equity investments, guarantees, and technical assistance, with a further $14.9 billion in cofinancing from partners, and its Board approved 151 new projects, programs, and financing facilities, up from 133 the previous year ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/videos/responding-asia-and-pacific-s-development-challenges); [Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/annual-report/editions/2024)). Sovereign financing totaled $19.2 billion, while nonsovereign financing reached $4.8 billion across a record 58 private sector projects, including 34 in frontier economies and new sectors, and climate finance hit a record $11.1 billion for adaptation and mitigation ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/videos/responding-asia-and-pacific-s-development-challenges)). That year it updated Strategy 2030 — its corporate master plan — around five focus areas: climate action, private sector development, regional cooperation and public goods, digital transformation, and resilience and empowerment, with cumulative climate financing from 2019 to 2030 expected to exceed $100 billion ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/annual-report/editions/2024)). The Asian Development Fund remains its largest source of grant financing, prioritized for small island developing states and countries classified as fragile and conflict-affected ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/annual-report/editions/2024)). Its developing member countries span five subregions, from [Kazakhstan](/places/kazakhstan/) and [Uzbekistan](/places/uzbekistan/) in Central and West Asia to the Pacific island states ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/annual-report/editions/2024)).

## The bigger-bank reforms and Kanda's second term

The bank's recent growth is a product of balance-sheet reform rather than new shareholder money. In September 2023 ADB updated its Capital Adequacy Framework, unlocking $100 billion in new funding capacity over the next decade and lifting annual new-commitments capacity to more than $36 billion — about 40% higher — while creating a Countercyclical Lending Buffer for members facing unexpected crises and safeguarding its AAA credit rating ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/adb-capital-management-reforms-unlock-100-billion-new-funding-over-next-decade-support-asia)). Months earlier it had established the Innovative Finance Facility for Climate in Asia and the Pacific (IF-CAP), which lets donors guarantee parts of its existing sovereign loan portfolio to release capital for new climate projects, and its commencement was announced with partners at COP29 in Baku ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/annual-report/editions/2024)).

In 2025 ADB provided a record $44 billion in development finance — $29.3 billion from its own resources and $14.7 billion in cofinancing ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/masato-kanda-begins-new-term-adb-president-pledges-stronger-regional-links)) — and the Charter amendment behind that scale, the first in the bank's 60-year history, lifted its annual financing capacity by 50% to more than $36 billion without requiring additional capital from shareholders, meaning the bank's growth is now funded by reforming how it uses its existing capital rather than by asking members for more. President Masato Kanda, who took office on 24 February 2025 after four decades in Japan's Ministry of Finance, including as Vice Minister of Finance for International Affairs with key roles in the G7 and G20, has made the private sector his first priority: private sector operations generated $9.5 billion in 2025, up from $6.8 billion in 2024, with a target of $13 billion a year by 2030 ([Center for Global Development](https://www.cgdev.org/event/fireside-chat-asian-development-bank-president-kanda); [Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/masato-kanda-begins-new-term-adb-president-pledges-stronger-regional-links)). He points to concrete results on the ground — a partnership with Hamkor Bank in Uzbekistan that connected 50,000 small and medium enterprises, most of them women-led, to markets and services; tourism infrastructure in Lao PDR that connected 50,000 people to services and new jobs; and a $2 billion public-private partnership advisory for Manila International Airport, serving 62 million annual visitors ([Center for Global Development](https://www.cgdev.org/event/fireside-chat-asian-development-bank-president-kanda)). Unanimously reelected on 1 September 2026, he began a new five-year term on 24 November 2026 under the vision "One Region, One Connected Future," pledging to mobilize $70 billion by 2035 to connect power grids and digital networks, including a $50 billion Pan-Asia Power Grid Initiative for 200 million people and a $20 billion Asia-Pacific Digital Highway for up to 650 million people ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/masato-kanda-begins-new-term-adb-president-pledges-stronger-regional-links)).

## FAQ

### What is the Asian Development Bank?

ADB is a multilateral development bank headquartered in Manila, Philippines, that finances economic and social development across Asia and the Pacific. Founded in 1966 and owned by 69 member countries, it provides loans, grants, equity investments, guarantees, and technical assistance to governments and private-sector clients in its developing member countries.

### How is ADB different from the World Bank and the AIIB?

ADB is a regional bank serving only Asia and the Pacific, while the World Bank is a separate global development lender. The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank is a separate, younger institution that began operations in Beijing in 2016, focuses on infrastructure, and has 111 approved members, compared with ADB's 69.

### Who leads the Asian Development Bank?

Masato Kanda, ADB's 11th President, has led the bank since 24 February 2025 and chairs its Board of Directors. A former Japanese Vice Minister of Finance for International Affairs, he was unanimously reelected on 1 September 2026 for a five-year term beginning 24 November 2026.

### How much financing does ADB provide each year?

In 2024 ADB committed $24.3 billion from its own resources plus $14.9 billion in cofinancing. In 2025 it provided a record $44 billion in total development finance — $29.3 billion of its own resources and $14.7 billion in cofinancing — after reforms lifted its annual capacity above $36 billion.

### What are ADB's main priorities now?

Under its updated Strategy 2030, ADB focuses on climate action, private sector development, regional cooperation and public goods, digital transformation, and resilience and empowerment. It aims to provide $100 billion in cumulative climate finance from 2019 to 2030 and $13 billion a year in private-sector financing by 2030.
