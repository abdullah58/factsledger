## Overview

Google Brain no longer exists as a standalone team. In April 2023, [Google](/organizations/google/) merged its Brain research group with DeepMind to form [Google DeepMind](https://factsledger.com/organizations/google-deepmind/), folding the two halves of its AI research into a single unit led by DeepMind co-founder [Demis Hassabis](/people/demis-hassabis/) ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/google-merges-deepmind-brain-research-units-as-ai-race-intensifies/), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/20/google-consolidates-ai-research-divisions-into-google-deepmind/)). The reorganization reshuffled the company's AI leadership: Jeff Dean, a Brain co-founder, left management to become chief scientist of both Google Research and Google DeepMind, reporting directly to CEO [Sundar Pichai](/people/sundar-pichai/), while Google Research stayed an independent division under James Manyika ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/google-merges-deepmind-brain-research-units-as-ai-race-intensifies/), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/20/google-consolidates-ai-research-divisions-into-google-deepmind/)). Pichai said the combination would "significantly accelerate our progress in AI" ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/google-merges-deepmind-brain-research-units-as-ai-race-intensifies/)).

## The part-time project that taught machines to see

What the merger absorbed was one of the most consequential research teams in machine-learning history. Google Brain began in 2011 as a part-time research collaboration between Jeff Dean, Google researcher Greg Corrado, and Stanford professor Andrew Ng — a year after DeepMind's own founding ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/20/google-consolidates-ai-research-divisions-into-google-deepmind/)). Hassabis would later say that the Brain and DeepMind teams had made "many of the seminal research advances that underpin modern AI, from Deep RL to Transformers" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/20/google-consolidates-ai-research-divisions-into-google-deepmind/)).

Brain's most famous early demonstration came in 2012. Google scientists wired together 16,000 computer processors — more than a billion connections between them — and threw 10 million random YouTube thumbnails at the system, letting the software learn from the data on its own ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/with-no-human-supervision-16000-computers-learn-to-recognize-cats-141653602/)). After millions of images, the network had taught itself to recognize cats — and, it turned out, human faces — without ever being told what either was. "We never told it during the training, 'This is a cat,'" Dean told The New York Times. "It basically invented the concept of a cat." ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/with-no-human-supervision-16000-computers-learn-to-recognize-cats-141653602/))

## The tool that scaled the field

The team's infrastructure work had the longest afterlife. In November 2015, Google open-sourced TensorFlow, its new machine-learning system — "faster, smarter, and more flexible than our old system," in the company's own announcement ([Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/tensorflow-smarter-machine-learning-for/)). It was the second generation of Brain's internal tooling: engineers could build and train neural networks up to five times faster than with the first-generation system it replaced, on anything from a single smartphone to thousands of machines in datacenters ([Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/tensorflow-smarter-machine-learning-for/)). TensorFlow was already inside Google's products at launch — speech recognition in the Google app, Smart Reply in Inbox, search in Google Photos — and the point of open-sourcing it, Google said, was to let the machine-learning community exchange ideas through working code rather than just research papers, and in turn accelerate research for everyone ([Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/tensorflow-smarter-machine-learning-for/)). Brain is also credited with enhancements to Google Translate ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/20/google-consolidates-ai-research-divisions-into-google-deepmind/)).

## Why the merger happened

The Brain–DeepMind combination landed the Brain team in the same unit as the researchers behind the Transformer work that, as Bloomberg noted, powers Google's Bard chatbot and its rival ChatGPT ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/google-merges-deepmind-brain-research-units-as-ai-race-intensifies/)). It came as Google was scrambling: the Bard launch had been rushed and poorly received, [Alphabet](https://factsledger.com/organizations/alphabet/)'s shares had dropped, and Microsoft's close collaboration with OpenAI was threatening Google's search core ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/20/google-consolidates-ai-research-divisions-into-google-deepmind/)). DeepMind itself moved out of Alphabet's "Other Bets" — the bucket for long-shot projects — and into corporate costs, a signal that its work was now central to the company rather than a side bet ([Bloomberg](https://www.moneyweb.co.za/news/tech/google-merges-deepmind-brain-research-units-as-ai-race-intensifies/)).

As a standalone name, Google Brain lasted twelve years. Its record — the 2012 experiment that put self-taught image recognition in the headlines, and the open-sourced TensorFlow system that put industrial-scale machine learning into everyone's hands — is the clearest window into how Google's AI research became the industry's. What it became is now called Google DeepMind ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/20/google-consolidates-ai-research-divisions-into-google-deepmind/)).
