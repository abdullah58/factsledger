## Overview

The Public Patent Foundation (PUBPAT) was a New York nonprofit that attacked patents it considered wrongly issued, using Patent Office reexaminations and the courts against patents held by [Microsoft](/organizations/microsoft/), Pfizer, Columbia University, Forgent Networks and Myriad Genetics ([Stanford Center for Internet and Society](https://cyberlaw.stanford.edu/blog/2005/02/002840-shtml/); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2006/09/06/228091/putting-patent-trolls-on-the-defensive/)). Founded in 2003 by patent lawyer Dan Ravicher, it won its biggest victory in 2013, when the Supreme Court unanimously ruled isolated human genes cannot be patented ([University of Miami School of Law](https://news.miami.edu/law/stories/2015/10/all-in-with-dan-ravicher,-director-of-new-startup-law-practicum.html)).

PUBPAT described itself as a not-for-profit legal services organization working to protect the public from the harms caused by the patent system ([Stanford Center for Internet and Society](https://cyberlaw.stanford.edu/blog/2005/02/002840-shtml/)). Its stated purpose was protecting "civil liberties and free markets from wrongly issued patents and unsound patent policy" by giving representation, advocacy and education to people and businesses otherwise deprived of access to the patent system ([LWN.net](https://lwn.net/Articles/64378/)). Ravicher argued that wrongly issued patents injured the public because they could be used by private actors to block activity that would otherwise be permissible, keeping prices artificially high and thwarting science ([Stanford Center for Internet and Society](https://cyberlaw.stanford.edu/blog/2005/02/002840-shtml/)). The foundation was based in [New York City](/places/new-york-city/), in the [United States](/places/united-states/) ([Science](https://www.science.org/content/article/patent-foe-sues-monsanto-modified-crops)).

## Founded on $90,000: a one-lawyer nonprofit

Ravicher started PUBPAT in 2003, after three years at the New York office of a Silicon Valley firm that specialized in startups and IPOs, with backing from Echoing Green, the Open Society Foundation and the Rockefeller Foundation ([University of Miami School of Law](https://news.miami.edu/law/stories/2015/10/all-in-with-dan-ravicher,-director-of-new-startup-law-practicum.html)). He raised $90,000 to start the foundation, living frugally off his six-figure income and winning a grant ([Financial Times](https://www.mail-archive.com/fsfe-france@gnu.org/msg00431.html)). As executive director and only full-time employee, he supervised a handful of volunteer scientists, postgraduates and legal interns as they searched for flaws in big-name patents ([Financial Times](https://www.mail-archive.com/fsfe-france@gnu.org/msg00431.html)).

Ravicher had studied materials science at the University of South Florida and law at the University of Virginia School of Law, then worked at Skadden Arps, Brobeck Phleger & Harrison, and Patterson Belknap Webb & Tyler in New York, and clerked for Judge Randall R. Rader of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit ([Stanford Center for Internet and Society](https://cyberlaw.stanford.edu/blog/2005/02/002840-shtml/)). He was also senior counsel to the Free Software Foundation and a lecturer of law at the Benjamin N. Cardozo School of Law ([Stanford Center for Internet and Society](https://cyberlaw.stanford.edu/blog/2005/02/002840-shtml/); [Science](https://www.science.org/content/article/patent-foe-sues-monsanto-modified-crops)). Managing Intellectual Property magazine twice named him one of the "50 Most Influential People in IP" ([University of Miami School of Law](https://news.miami.edu/law/stories/2015/10/all-in-with-dan-ravicher,-director-of-new-startup-law-practicum.html)). One intellectual-property lawyer called him a "Robin Hood" for the patent world's have-nots ([Financial Times](https://www.mail-archive.com/fsfe-france@gnu.org/msg00431.html)).

PUBPAT set out four core activities: challenging patents that threaten the public's health, freedom or other interests; helping small businesses defend themselves against patents asserted against them; establishing patent commons in markets crippled by patent thickets; and educating the public and advocating reform of the patent system ([LWN.net](https://lwn.net/Articles/64378/)). Its main weapon was the Patent Office's reexamination procedure: filing requests, backed by prior art the original examiner had missed, asking the office to revoke a patent ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2006/09/06/228091/putting-patent-trolls-on-the-defensive/)). The method was taken up by the Electronic Frontier Foundation, and a Washington-based group, Patients Not Profits, used similar tactics against drug and software patents ([Financial Times](https://www.mail-archive.com/fsfe-france@gnu.org/msg00431.html)). In 2015 Ravicher moved to the University of Miami as inaugural director of its Startup Law Practicum ([University of Miami School of Law](https://news.miami.edu/law/stories/2015/10/all-in-with-dan-ravicher,-director-of-new-startup-law-practicum.html)).

## The reexamination campaigns: software, drugs and the JPEG patent

PUBPAT's first big target was Microsoft's FAT file system. In 2003 Microsoft sought to license FAT, which is crucial to the operation of its Windows operating system, and months later Ravicher filed a reexamination request on the company's 1996 patent, pointing to two prior software patents he said should have prevented it being granted ([Financial Times](https://www.mail-archive.com/fsfe-france@gnu.org/msg00431.html)). He had found about a hundred pieces of prior art that the examiner had not reviewed ([LWN.net](https://lwn.net/Articles/64378/)). In 2004 the Patent Office initially rejected the FAT patents, but it reversed that decision in early 2006 ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2006/09/06/228091/putting-patent-trolls-on-the-defensive/)). The case was not a clean loss: in answering the initial rejection, Microsoft took positions on the scope of its patent that made it narrower than when originally issued, leaving room for people to implement a FAT system without infringing the new claims even though they might have infringed the old ones ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2006/09/06/228091/putting-patent-trolls-on-the-defensive/)).

In June 2005 the Patent Office ruled that [Pfizer](/organizations/pfizer/)'s patent on Lipitor, a $12bn-a-year cholesterol drug, might be invalid ([Financial Times](https://www.mail-archive.com/fsfe-france@gnu.org/msg00431.html)). The patent was in force until 2017, and Ravicher argued it precluded other companies from developing alternatives that might have fewer side effects ([Financial Times](https://www.mail-archive.com/fsfe-france@gnu.org/msg00431.html)). PUBPAT also challenged Columbia University's 2002 patent for a gene-inserting process used in making drugs, the university's fourth such patent, on the ground that its claims were identical to Columbia's 1980 patent ([Financial Times](https://www.mail-archive.com/fsfe-france@gnu.org/msg00431.html)). The technology had netted the university hundreds of millions of dollars, and facing lawsuits, Columbia later agreed not to assert the patent ([Financial Times](https://www.mail-archive.com/fsfe-france@gnu.org/msg00431.html)).

The best-known software fight was over the JPEG standard. U.S. Patent 4,698,672 had been applied for by [Compression Labs](/organizations/compression-labs/) Inc. in 1986, which never pursued royalties, but Forgent Networks acquired Compression Labs and in 2002 announced it would seek royalties relating to the JPEG patent ([Pinsent Masons (OUT-LAW)](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/re-examination-request-for-jpeg-patent)). By 2005 Forgent had licensed the patent to over 40 companies and had litigation pending against almost 40 more, including Microsoft ([Pinsent Masons (OUT-LAW)](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/re-examination-request-for-jpeg-patent)). PUBPAT filed a formal request in November 2005 asking the Patent Office to revoke the patent on the grounds of prior art — that the feature being patented was already in the public domain ([Pinsent Masons (OUT-LAW)](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/re-examination-request-for-jpeg-patent)). In May 2006 the Patent Office rejected some of Forgent's claims on the JPEG photo file compression standard, though that decision was not yet final ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2006/09/06/228091/putting-patent-trolls-on-the-defensive/)). Forgent Networks is the company now known as [Asure Software](/organizations/asure-software/), whose own profile covers the settlement of its final patent cases.

## Gene patents, stem cells and seeds: the courtroom years

PUBPAT's defining case began in May 2009, when the [American Civil Liberties Union](/organizations/aclu/) and PUBPAT filed a lawsuit in federal district court to overturn Myriad Genetics' patents on the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, which Myriad had obtained in 1997 and 1998 ([The New York Review of Books](http://www.nybooks.com/articles/archives/2013/sep/26/genes-you-cant-patent/?insrc=toc)). The patents let Myriad exclude all others from using the isolated DNA in breast cancer research, diagnostics and treatment, and the plaintiffs argued that genes, even if isolated, were legally ineligible for patents ([The New York Review of Books](http://www.nybooks.com/articles/archives/2013/sep/26/genes-you-cant-patent/?insrc=toc)). The district court struck down the patents in March 2010, but the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit upheld them in July 2011 and again in August 2012 ([The New York Review of Books](http://www.nybooks.com/articles/archives/2013/sep/26/genes-you-cant-patent/?insrc=toc)). On 13 June 2013 the Supreme Court unanimously held that a naturally occurring DNA segment is a product of nature and not patent-eligible merely because it has been isolated, while cDNA — DNA synthesized in the laboratory — is patent-eligible ([The New York Review of Books](http://www.nybooks.com/articles/archives/2013/sep/26/genes-you-cant-patent/?insrc=toc); [American Civil Liberties Union](https://wp.api.aclu.org/press-releases/supreme-court-invalidates-patents-breast-and-ovarian-cancer-genes)).

The ruling mattered because Myriad's patents had let it control access to BRCA testing, set its terms and cost, and make it difficult for women to get alternate tests or a comprehensive second opinion ([American Civil Liberties Union](https://wp.api.aclu.org/press-releases/supreme-court-invalidates-patents-breast-and-ovarian-cancer-genes)). The suit was brought on behalf of researchers, genetic counselors, patients, breast-cancer and women's-health groups, and medical professional associations representing 150,000 geneticists, pathologists and laboratory professionals ([American Civil Liberties Union](https://wp.api.aclu.org/press-releases/supreme-court-invalidates-patents-breast-and-ovarian-cancer-genes)). One plaintiff, breast-cancer survivor Lisbeth Ceriani, faced paying over $4,000 for Myriad's test because Myriad had refused to contract with her insurer, and waited 18 months before obtaining it through a grant ([American Civil Liberties Union](https://wp.api.aclu.org/press-releases/supreme-court-invalidates-patents-breast-and-ovarian-cancer-genes)). Ravicher, co-counsel in the lawsuit, said the court had "rightfully found that patents cannot be awarded for something so fundamental to nature as DNA" ([American Civil Liberties Union](https://wp.api.aclu.org/press-releases/supreme-court-invalidates-patents-breast-and-ovarian-cancer-genes)).

PUBPAT also took on stem-cell and seed patents. In July 2006 it joined the Foundation for Taxpayer and Consumer Rights in asking the Patent Office to reexamine three patents held by the Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation on isolating and maintaining embryonic stem cells, arguing that WARF's aggressive enforcement was impeding scientific research ([The Chronicle of Higher Education](https://www.chronicle.com/article/u-s-rejects-wisconsins-patents-on-stem-cell-technology-but-university-vows-to-appeal/)). The patents stemmed from the work of James Thomson at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, who received the first in 1998, with two related patents issued in 2001 and 2006 ([The Chronicle of Higher Education](https://www.chronicle.com/article/u-s-rejects-wisconsins-patents-on-stem-cell-technology-but-university-vows-to-appeal/)). In a preliminary finding made public in April 2007, the Patent Office determined the three patents were issued in error because the technique would have been obvious to a person skilled in the art, though WARF said it would vigorously defend its claims ([The Chronicle of Higher Education](https://www.chronicle.com/article/u-s-rejects-wisconsins-patents-on-stem-cell-technology-but-university-vows-to-appeal/)). WARF had already loosened some of its licensing requirements in January 2007 ([The Chronicle of Higher Education](https://www.chronicle.com/article/u-s-rejects-wisconsins-patents-on-stem-cell-technology-but-university-vows-to-appeal/)).

The seed fight ended in a draw that was really a win. On 29 March 2011 PUBPAT filed suit against Monsanto on behalf of 60 family farmers, seed businesses and organic agricultural organizations, challenging the company's patents on genetically modified seed ([Science](https://www.science.org/content/article/patent-foe-sues-monsanto-modified-crops)). The plaintiffs said they had been forced to sue preemptively to protect themselves from being accused of infringement if Monsanto's genetically modified seed contaminated their fields, something Monsanto had done to others in the past ([Science](https://www.science.org/content/article/patent-foe-sues-monsanto-modified-crops)). Monsanto called the suit "false, misleading and deceptive" ([Science](https://www.science.org/content/article/patent-foe-sues-monsanto-modified-crops)). On 10 June 2013 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the suit, holding there was no justiciable case or controversy — but only because Monsanto had given binding assurances that it would not sue growers whose crops inadvertently contained trace amounts, under 1%, of its biotech genes ([Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/files/Upload/Newsletters/Last_Month_at_the_Federal_Circuit/2013/July/FCN_Jul13_3.html)). Those representations were binding on Monsanto as a matter of judicial estoppel ([Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/files/Upload/Newsletters/Last_Month_at_the_Federal_Circuit/2013/July/FCN_Jul13_3.html)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What was the Public Patent Foundation?**
The Public Patent Foundation (PUBPAT) was a New York nonprofit legal services organization founded in 2003 by patent lawyer Dan Ravicher to protect the public from the harms caused by the patent system, especially wrongly issued patents and unsound patent policy ([Stanford Center for Internet and Society](https://cyberlaw.stanford.edu/blog/2005/02/002840-shtml/)).

**Who founded PUBPAT and why?**
Dan Ravicher founded it in 2003 after three years as a patent lawyer in New York, with backing from Echoing Green, the Open Society Foundation and the Rockefeller Foundation, because he believed no one else was tackling how the patent system denied fair access to technology to the poor and underprivileged ([University of Miami School of Law](https://news.miami.edu/law/stories/2015/10/all-in-with-dan-ravicher,-director-of-new-startup-law-practicum.html)).

**How did PUBPAT challenge patents?**
Its main tool was the [U.S. Patent and Trademark Office](/organizations/us-patent-and-trademark-office/)'s reexamination procedure: filing requests backed by prior art the original examiner had missed, asking the office to revoke patents it considered overbroad or already in the public domain ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2006/09/06/228091/putting-patent-trolls-on-the-defensive/)).

**What was PUBPAT's biggest victory?**
The 2013 Supreme Court case Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics, brought by the ACLU and PUBPAT, which unanimously held that isolated human genes are products of nature and cannot be patented, while laboratory-made cDNA remains patent-eligible ([American Civil Liberties Union](https://wp.api.aclu.org/press-releases/supreme-court-invalidates-patents-breast-and-ovarian-cancer-genes)).

**What happened to PUBPAT?**
No fully-read source confirms its activity or status after 2013, and this profile claims no dissolution date. Its founder, Dan Ravicher, moved to the University of Miami in 2015 to direct its Startup Law Practicum ([University of Miami School of Law](https://news.miami.edu/law/stories/2015/10/all-in-with-dan-ravicher,-director-of-new-startup-law-practicum.html)).
