## Overview

**Xerox PARC — the Palo Alto Research Center — is the research company established in 1970 as a division of [Xerox](/organizations/xerox/) Corporation in Palo Alto, California, to explore new information technologies beyond Xerox's copier business, whose researchers developed the Alto (the first personal computer), the graphical user interface, the laser printer and Ethernet ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)).** Its full name matters for disambiguation: PARC was Xerox's lab, not an independent university or a product brand, and after 2002 it dropped "Xerox" from its name before Xerox donated it to SRI International in 2023 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)).

Xerox had invented and dominated the paper copier market, but with C. Peter McColough's accession as president in 1966 the company began exploring diversification ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). In 1969 the director of research, Jack Goldman, produced a plan to establish an "Advanced Scientific & Systems Laboratory" to develop future technologies — pioneering work in advanced physics, materials science and computer science, not a copy of the Rochester lab that refined the copier business ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). Goldman first suggested New Haven, Connecticut, home of Yale University and near Xerox's Stamford headquarters, but his choice for director, George Pake, successfully lobbied for Palo Alto, near Stanford University, which had backed cooperative ventures with electronics firms since before World War II ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). Upon opening in a former Encyclopaedia Britannica building in Palo Alto, Pake assembled his staff, starting with Robert Taylor, a former deputy director of the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA), whose government-sponsored research network played a key role in creating the Internet ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). Taylor staffed PARC from his vast contacts; the plan was that commercial products might take a decade while prize-winning ideas developed quickly and Xerox profited first — but, as events transpired, the 1970s were a decade of fundamental innovation at PARC while its parent failed to turn those ideas into dollars ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)).

## The Alto, the laser printer, and Ethernet

Among the many inventions of the 1970s, few matter as much as the personal computer, and because the Xerox Alto was developed in 1973, PARC can claim credit for having made the first one ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). But the mindset at Xerox, like that of all computer manufacturers of the time, was that no market existed for such machines; corporate analysts asserted the computer would be too expensive for the private and small-business users it was designed to serve, so the machine was never released ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). By the time its commercial successor, the Xerox Star, was released in 1981 at over $16,000 per machine, it was too late: [Apple](/organizations/apple/), Commodore and Tandy had released more affordable machines, and even IBM had released a relatively inexpensive personal computer ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). The Star, with its mouse-driven graphical interface, built-in Ethernet networking and optional laser printer, was far ahead of its time — but with fewer than 2,000 units sold, Xerox backed out of the personal computer market ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)).

Part of the problem was distance: located far from corporate power in Stamford, PARC's researchers were not part of everyday Xerox life ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). The laser printer epitomizes it. PARC's Gary Starkweather, a Xerox researcher in the mid-1960s, had the idea of using lasers in copiers: short exposures, on the order of a billionth of a second, could replace the copier's traditional light source, and a laser-driven copier could also serve as a printer, taking an image from a computer screen and capturing it on paper — "what you see is what you get" on paper as well as on screen ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). Xerox initially saw no point in innovating while its current technology worked so well; only Goldman's intervention saved the idea, with Starkweather transferred to PARC in 1971, a working prototype by early 1972 — and Xerox not bringing it to market until 1977, when it became a best-selling product ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). Another early breakthrough was Ethernet, proposed by Robert Metcalfe and jointly developed with [Intel](/organizations/intel/) and Digital Equipment Corporation in the mid-1970s, which increased the speed and reliability of data exchanges over local area networks and is still commonly used ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). Alan Kay, another researcher brought to PARC by Taylor, envisioned small "notebook" computers and created Smalltalk, the first true object-oriented programming language, which was instrumental in creating the graphical interface for the Alto ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)).

## Interpress: the PARC invention that became Adobe

PARC's least-celebrated lineage may be its most commercially consequential — because it is the direct origin of [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/). Warnock, under the direction of Geschke, developed a language dubbed "Interpress" to enable Xerox printers to talk to computers ([Lemelson](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/charles-geschke-and-john-warnock)). [Charles Geschke](/people/charles-geschke/), born September 11, 1939, in Cleveland, earned a PhD in computer science from Carnegie Mellon in 1972, joined PARC shortly after graduation, helped design the Mesa programming language, and in 1980 formed the Imaging Sciences Laboratory for graphics and imaging research, where he led Interpress development working closely with Warnock ([Lemelson](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/charles-geschke-and-john-warnock)). [John Warnock](/people/john-warnock/), born October 6, 1940, in Salt Lake City, earned degrees in mathematics and philosophy and a PhD in engineering and computer science at the University of Utah, worked at Evans & Sutherland, and accepted a graphics-research post at PARC, where Geschke was the man who hired him ([Lemelson](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/charles-geschke-and-john-warnock)).

While Xerox made Interpress its internal standard, the firm was not interested in licensing the technology to others, and the pair saw a tremendous missed opportunity ([Lemelson](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/charles-geschke-and-john-warnock)). Geschke's own account fills in the boardroom detail: Xerox loved the work and promised a corporate standard, but at headquarters he was told it took seven years to develop a product and the plan could not even be discussed for fear competitors would beat Xerox to market ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). The pair resigned from PARC in 1982 and created a new language from scratch to let computers tell printers exactly how text, images and lines should appear on paper, naming their firm after a creek behind Warnock's home ([Lemelson](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/charles-geschke-and-john-warnock)). That language was [PostScript](/creative-works/postscript/), and it caught the attention of [Steve Jobs](/people/steve-jobs/): Apple bought a 19 percent stake in Adobe (19.9% by Geschke's precise account) and agreed to use PostScript in its [LaserWriter](/creative-works/laserwriter/) printer, which hit the market in 1985 ([Lemelson](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/charles-geschke-and-john-warnock)). Geschke recalled that Adobe published the PostScript specification three or four months before the first LaserWriter shipped ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

PARC's influence also reached Apple directly. In 1979 Jobs led a team of Apple engineers to PARC, where they were shown the functional graphical user interface, a mouse, and icons in place of command-line instructions — features integrated into the Apple Lisa (1983) and the Macintosh (1984) ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Apple-Inc)). Xerox later sued Apple in 1989, claiming it stole the GUI idea; the suit was dismissed in 1990 on technical grounds ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Apple-Inc)).

## Later years: electronic paper, independence, and SRI

PARC continued developing ideas through the 1990s, but tethered to Xerox's mission as "The Document Company" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). Among the most interesting was electronic paper, a flexible, reusable plastic material designed by Nick Sheridon containing millions of charged bichromal beads, each orientable by an electric field to reproduce text or images, viewable in bright light and at wide angles, retaining an image until another field was applied and reusable roughly 1,000 times ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). In 1999, 3M signed on to manufacture and help develop electronic paper products — but Xerox ultimately did not capitalize on the innovation ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)). In 2002 PARC became an independent subsidiary of Xerox and changed its name from Xerox PARC to PARC, and in 2023 Xerox donated PARC to the research institute SRI International ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/PARC-company)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim PARC invented the computer mouse — that distinction belongs to Stanford Research Institute, a point the sources note in passing and this profile does not expand. It does not adjudicate the "first personal computer" debate: the Alto claim follows Britannica's PARC article under its definition, and earlier machines may qualify under others (see open items). It does not claim Interpress and PostScript were the same code: the sources are explicit that PostScript was developed from scratch at Adobe as a successor technology, not a copy of Interpress. And it does not present PARC's story as a simple morality tale about corporate blindness — the laser printer's five-year path to market, the Alto's cost problem, and the Star's pricing all show a lab and a parent company struggling with timing and economics, not just vision.
