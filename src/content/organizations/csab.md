## Overview

CSAB, Inc. — formerly the Computing Sciences Accreditation Board — exists because of a gap no exam could fill. Civil and mechanical engineers have the PE license; computer scientists have nothing of the kind. So in the early 1980s, the Association for Computing Machinery and the [IEEE](/organizations/ieee/) (for the Computer Society) built an accreditor for a profession without licensure: CSAB would "provide accreditation for computer science in the same way that ABET provides accreditation for engineering degrees." Its commission, the CSAC, granted its first accreditations in June 1986; in 1998–2001 the whole apparatus merged into [ABET](/organizations/abet/) as its fourth commission, the Computing Accreditation Commission (CAC) ([Communications of the ACM](https://cacm.acm.org/research/changes-in-computer-science-accreditation/)). Today CSAB survives as an ABET member society — the lead society for computer science, information systems, and software engineering accreditation.

## Why computing needed its own accreditor

The [National Council of Examiners for Engineering and Surveying](/organizations/ncees/) can vouch for an engineer with an exam. No equivalent exists for a programmer — "computer science — like math and physics — does not have a professional license." Accreditation was the substitute: an external check that a degree program teaches what it claims. CSAB's general purpose, as stated at its formation, was "to advance the development and practice of computing sciences and to enhance the quality of educational programs in computing sciences" ([CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/research/changes-in-computer-science-accreditation/)).

The CSAC's initial criteria were "strongly influenced" by ABET's Engineering Accreditation Commission — the older sibling's template, adapted. Over sixteen years the criteria were clarified, made less prescriptive, and extended to social and ethical implications of computing and to program assessment. By 2001, 171 programs held CSAC accreditation — slightly more than half housed in arts-and-science colleges, about a third in engineering colleges, a split that itself says something about where computer science lived in the university.

## The merger into ABET

Computing accreditation had collaborated with ABET from the start — for years the CSAC and ABET's EAC cooperated on programs qualifying for both. In October 1998, "several years of discussions between the two boards culminated in a memorandum of agreement to integrate CSAC accreditation activities into the ABET structure over a two-year period." In July 2001 the transition completed: the CSAC became the Computing Accreditation Commission, ABET's fourth commission alongside ANSAC, EAC, and ETAC, and every CSAB-accredited program became an ABET-accredited program. The reorganized CSAB became a member society of ABET — by ABET's account, immediately "one of our largest member societies, with more than 300 accredited programs" ([ABET](https://www.abet.org/about-abet/history/)).

In its new role CSAB functions as the computing disciplines' professional society inside ABET: it develops the accreditation criteria and trains the Program Evaluators for the fields where it leads — computer science, information systems, software engineering — while the CAC itself conducts the accreditation visits. For computer engineering it cooperates with other member societies. In 2001 the Association for Information Systems joined CSAB, and the CAC's remit broadened to information systems.

## A note on the founding date

The sources genuinely disagree: the peer-reviewed CACM account says the board was established in 1982 by the ACM and IEEE; other accounts date the founding to 1984 (first president) or 1985 (ABET's account of helping establish it). This profile uses the early-1980s range rather than adjudicating — a reminder that even institutional birthdays are claims that need sources.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is CSAB?**
CSAB, Inc. (formerly the Computing Sciences Accreditation Board) is the nonprofit behind computing-program accreditation in the U.S. — now an ABET member society and the lead society for computer science, information systems, and software engineering accreditation.

**When was CSAB founded?**
In the early 1980s — sources say 1982 (established by the ACM and IEEE), 1984, or 1985 (ABET's involvement). The profile does not pick a single year because the sources disagree.

**What happened to CSAB's accreditation work?**
In 1998–2001 it merged into ABET: its Computer Science Accreditation Commission became ABET's Computing Accreditation Commission (CAC), the fourth ABET commission, and all CSAB-accredited programs became ABET-accredited.

**Why does computing need accreditation instead of licensure?**
Unlike engineering, computer science has no professional licensing exam. CSAB was created to provide the quality signal accreditation-style — external review of degree programs — for a field licensure doesn't cover.

**What is CSAB's role today?**
As an ABET member society, it develops accreditation criteria and trains program evaluators for computer science, information systems, and software engineering, and cooperates on computer engineering accreditation.
