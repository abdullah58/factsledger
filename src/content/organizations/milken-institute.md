## Overview

The Milken Institute is a nonprofit, nonpartisan think tank based in Santa Monica, California, founded in 1991 by financier Michael Milken. It publishes research on finance, health and regional economies, runs the annual Global Conference in Los Angeles, and in 2025 opened a museum of the American Dream in Washington, D.C. ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424); [Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/milken-institute-2025-global-conference-convenes-world-leaders-visionaries-and-innovators-los)).

Milken formalised his philanthropy by founding the Institute, whose early stated goals were to study the dynamics of economic growth and to examine deep-seated social problems through the lens of finance ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424)). Today the Institute describes itself as "a nonprofit, nonpartisan think tank focused on accelerating measurable progress on the path to a meaningful life" ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/milken-institute-2025-global-conference-convenes-world-leaders-visionaries-and-innovators-los)). Its work spans research, convening and programmes: an annual ranking of American cities, a medical-research accelerator, a quarterly magazine, and a conference that has become one of finance's most prominent gatherings. The Institute's founder remains its defining figure — and its most contested one.

## The founder: from junk bonds to philanthropy

No account of the Institute makes sense without Michael Milken's first career. At Drexel Burnham Lambert in the 1970s and 1980s, Milken virtually reinvented the market for high-yield "junk bonds" — speculative, below-investment-grade securities — arguing that a diversified portfolio of them could deliver strong returns and open capital markets to risky start-ups shut out of normal financing ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424); [NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2020-02-19/a-look-at-the-newly-pardoned-michael-milken-a-junk-bond-king-turned-philanthropist)). The innovation financed new ventures and corporate takeovers alike, and in 1987 alone Milken earned $550 million ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424)). NPR credits the junk-bond market with funding cable television, early mobile-phone companies and casino empires ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2020-02-19/a-look-at-the-newly-pardoned-michael-milken-a-junk-bond-king-turned-philanthropist)).

The same career ended in criminal conviction. Milken was the target of a 98-count indictment and, in a plea bargain, pleaded guilty to six securities violations; he was fined $600 million, sentenced to ten years in prison, served 22 months, and was barred from the securities industry for life ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424)). NPR's account agrees on the shape of it: indicted on more than 90 charges, he pleaded guilty to six felonies, including fraud and conspiracy, went to prison for about two years and was banned from securities trading for life ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2020-02-19/a-look-at-the-newly-pardoned-michael-milken-a-junk-bond-king-turned-philanthropist)).

After prison, Milken remade himself as a philanthropist, donating hundreds of millions of dollars to medical research and education, and the Institute became the institutional home of that second act ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2020-02-19/a-look-at-the-newly-pardoned-michael-milken-a-junk-bond-king-turned-philanthropist)). He remained the Institute's chairman, described by its then-president as a "hands-off manager" who helped set its priorities ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424)). In February 2020, President [Donald Trump](/people/donald-trump/) pardoned Milken for the 1990 conviction, with the White House citing his philanthropy ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2020-02-19/a-look-at-the-newly-pardoned-michael-milken-a-junk-bond-king-turned-philanthropist)). The pardon did not erase the tension at the Institute's core: as one contemporary profile put it, it has proved difficult to separate Milken's life and work from the Institute's mission ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424)).

## Research: cities, cures and capital

The Institute's longest-running research product is its ranking of American cities. The Best-Performing Cities Index, published annually since 1999, measures metropolitan areas' effectiveness at promoting economic growth, based on job creation, wage growth, high-tech output growth, housing affordability and broadband coverage ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/austin-texas-and-idaho-falls-idaho-top-milken-institutes-2024-annual-ranking-best-performing-cities)). The 2024 edition ranked 200 large and 203 small metropolitan areas, with Austin, Texas first among large cities and Idaho Falls, Idaho first among small cities, and added new metrics for community resilience and income inequality ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/austin-texas-and-idaho-falls-idaho-top-milken-institutes-2024-annual-ranking-best-performing-cities)). The 2024 rankings were based mainly on 2022 data, when the US labour market added more than 6 million jobs, 90.7 percent of them in metropolitan areas ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/austin-texas-and-idaho-falls-idaho-top-milken-institutes-2024-annual-ranking-best-performing-cities)).

In health, the Institute's best-known programme is FasterCures, founded in 2003 with funding from the Sumner Redstone Charitable Foundation, the Milken Family Foundation and other donors to accelerate the movement of ideas from laboratory experiment to patient treatment ([Philanthropy Roundtable](https://www.philanthropyroundtable.org/?p=22483)). In 2007 it joined with the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation and the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation to create a Philanthropy Advisory Service guiding donors to medical investments, and it has influenced decision-making at the National Institutes of Health ([Philanthropy Roundtable](https://www.philanthropyroundtable.org/?p=22483)). FasterCures grew out of Milken's own experience: diagnosed with advanced prostate cancer, he founded the Prostate Cancer Foundation in 1993, and a 2004 *Fortune* cover story, "The Man Who Changed Medicine," summarised his approach as cutting grant wait times, flooding the field with fast cash, and funding therapy-driven ideas rather than basic science ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424); [Philanthropy Roundtable](https://www.philanthropyroundtable.org/?p=22483)).

The Institute's early work also reached into government. In 1997 it produced the final report for "Rebuild L.A.," the city's official response to the 1992 [Los Angeles](/places/los-angeles/) riots, and it worked with the Commerce Department's Minority Business Development Agency and the California treasurer's office on ways to increase access to capital for minority-owned businesses ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424)). Its quarterly magazine, the *Milken Institute Review*, launched in the late 1990s with Peter Passell as editor and an advisory board including Paul Krugman and Jagdish Bhagwati ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424)).

## The Global Conference

The Institute's most visible product is its annual Global Conference. The idea dates to 1998 — bringing together serious, influential people with widely varying backgrounds but a shared desire to address the world's most pressing challenges — and by 2017 the event marked its 20th year, drawing 3,500 participants from more than 50 countries to Los Angeles for nearly 200 panels and private sessions over four days ([Milken Institute Review](https://www.milkenreview.org/index.php/articles/from-the-ceo-17?IssueID=23)).

The 2019 conference ran from 28 April to 1 May at the Beverly Hilton under the theme "Driving Shared Prosperity" ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/events/global-conference-2019)). By the 28th annual conference in May 2025, the event had grown to 1,000 speakers and 5,000 attendees from more than 80 countries — including CEOs, government officials, philanthropists, scientists, investors and cultural figures — with 170 public sessions livestreamed ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/milken-institute-2025-global-conference-convenes-world-leaders-visionaries-and-innovators-los)). The Institute's chief executive, Richard Ditizio, described the conference as "where the world comes to connect ideas with capital, science with policy, and vision with action" ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/milken-institute-2025-global-conference-convenes-world-leaders-visionaries-and-innovators-los)).

The conference is also where the Institute's network becomes concrete. Among its speakers was [Erkin Adylov](/people/erkin-adylov/), founder and CEO of [Behavox](/organizations/behavox/), the AI compliance-surveillance firm — the Institute's speaker bio for Adylov records that he founded Behavox in 2014 after working as an equities analyst at [Goldman Sachs](/organizations/goldman-sachs/) and a partner at Man GLG. That bio was verified in full for the sibling Behavox profile; in this session the bio page redirected to the general 2019 conference page, so the Adylov facts rest on that earlier verification (see open items).

## The American Dream museum

In September 2025 the Institute opened its most public-facing project, the Milken Center for Advancing the American Dream, in Washington, D.C. ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/milken-center-advancing-american-dream-opens-flagship-exhibition-american-dream-experience)). The Center occupies five historic buildings at the corner of 15th Street and Pennsylvania Avenue NW, across from the White House and the US Treasury, renovated over 12 years — including the former Riggs Bank, known as "The Bank of Presidents," and the American Security and Trust Company ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/milken-center-advancing-american-dream-opens-flagship-exhibition-american-dream-experience)). Its flagship exhibition, the American Dream Experience, uses galleries and immersive exhibits to show how ingenuity and perseverance have shaped the American Dream, organised around four pillars: education, health, finance and entrepreneurship ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/milken-center-advancing-american-dream-opens-flagship-exhibition-american-dream-experience)). Admission is free, with tickets required, and the Kenneth C. Griffin Holodeck Experience costs $15 ([Milken Institute](https://milkeninstitute.org/content-hub/news-releases/milken-center-advancing-american-dream-opens-flagship-exhibition-american-dream-experience)).

## Controversy: opportunity zones

The Institute's closest brush with scandal in its own right came in October 2019, over the federal "opportunity zone" programme, which offers tax incentives for investment in low-income communities. The *New York Times* reported that Treasury Secretary Steven Mnuchin had intervened in the programme's implementation in a way that benefited Milken, who held investments in the affected areas, and that Institute officials had met Treasury aide Daniel Kowalski to shape the programme's rules ([InfluenceWatch](https://www.influencewatch.org/non-profit/milken-institute/)). A spokesman for Milken denied the Institute had any knowledge of Milken's personal holding, while an Institute spokesman insisted its support for the programme was motivated solely by concern for the poor ([InfluenceWatch](https://www.influencewatch.org/non-profit/milken-institute/)).

Congress picked up the story. Representative Bill Pascrell Jr. of New Jersey, a member of the House Ways and Means Committee, wrote to Mnuchin about "alleged rampant corruption" at the Treasury Department, asking whether the Secretary saw his job as protecting "the entirety of the American people or a handful of plutocrats and personal friends" ([The Fiscal Times](https://www.thefiscaltimes.com/2019/10/29/Mnuchin-Pressed-Alleged-Rampant-Corruption-Treasury-Department)). A Treasury spokesperson called the *Times* story "highly inaccurate and deeply misleading," Mnuchin denied having knowledge of Milken's investments in the zone in question, and Milken said he "played no role in any recommendations to any government officials regarding changes in opportunity zone regulations" ([The Fiscal Times](https://www.thefiscaltimes.com/2019/10/29/Mnuchin-Pressed-Alleged-Rampant-Corruption-Treasury-Department)). The underlying *Times* article was not re-read in full for this profile, so the programme specifics above rest on the two secondary summaries cited here (see open items).

The episode fits a pattern noted as early as 2000: the Institute's work sometimes touches subjects in which its founder has a clear personal interest, from junk bonds and savings-and-loan reform to securities litigation — raising questions, in one contemporary critic's words, about "how independent the think tank really is" ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424)). The Institute has consistently denied that its research serves Milken's interests, asking that its work be judged on its merits ([The American Prospect](https://www.thefreelibrary.com/THE+RESURRECTION+OF+MICHAEL+MILKEN-a060269424)).
