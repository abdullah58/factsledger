## Overview

Entrepreneur is an American business magazine and website, published by Entrepreneur Media Inc. from Irvine, California, that covers entrepreneurship, small business management, and business ([The International Investor](https://entrepreneur-investor.com/about-us-copy/); [GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/en/news-release/2024/01/18/2811868/0/en/The-Habit-Burger-Grill-Ranked-Among-Top-Franchises-in-Entrepreneur-Magazine-s-Franchise-500-for-Third-Consecutive-Year.html)). Founded in 1977 by Chase Revel — a convicted bank robber who had begun its forerunner as a newsletter in 1973 — it is best known for the Franchise 500, the franchise ranking it has published every year since 1979 ([Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1986-08-01-me-19055-story.html); [GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/en/news-release/2024/01/18/2811868/0/en/The-Habit-Burger-Grill-Ranked-Among-Top-Franchises-in-Entrepreneur-Magazine-s-Franchise-500-for-Third-Consecutive-Year.html)).

Today the magazine is run by editor-in-chief Jason Feifer, who took the job in October 2016, and chief executive Ryan Shea, who succeeded his father Peter Shea ([Talking Biz News](https://talkingbiznews.com/they-talk-biz-news/entrepreneur-magazine-names-new-editor/); [Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/entrepreneurs/peter-sheas-impact-on-entrepreneur-magazine/464690)). Its journalism still runs on founder stories: in June 2025 it published the in-depth account of how [Chime](/organizations/chime/) went from being turned down by about 100 investors to a [Nasdaq](/organizations/nasdaq/) debut valued at around $12 billion ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/how-banking-app-chime-went-from-broke-to-ipo-billions/493263)). That arc — a bankrupt magazine rescued by a franchisor, a founder with a criminal record, and a trademark its owner has spent decades suing other entrepreneurs to protect — makes Entrepreneur one of the stranger stories in American business publishing.

## A founder with a criminal past

Chase Revel founded Entrepreneur after a career that the Los Angeles Times documented from court records in 1986: he began the forerunner of the magazine, a newsletter called Insider's Report that exposed schemes targeting would-be businessmen, in 1973, and wrote books including *184 Businesses Anyone Can Start and Make a Lot of Money* (1981) ([Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1986-08-01-me-19055-story.html)). The same court records showed a very different early career. Revel — born John Leonard Burke, according to the suit and motor-vehicle records cited by the Times — pleaded guilty to one count of bank robbery by intimidation and two counts of attempted bank robbery by intimidation in Houston, where his hired men were sent to four banks in a single morning carrying threatening notes about tellers' children, and to robbing a Las Vegas bank of $5,000; he was sentenced to four years in prison ([Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1986-08-01-me-19055-story.html)). He later pleaded guilty to three counts of mail fraud in 1973 over a bogus shipping company, Aetna Express, and was placed on probation ([Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1986-08-01-me-19055-story.html)). Revel himself declined to confirm or deny that he was Burke.

The magazine's own early journalism also landed it in court. Chase Revel, Inc., doing business as Entrepreneur Magazine, ran a monthly "Fraud" column exposing consumer and investor fraud, and its March 1979 feature "Golden Bear Turns to Brass" falsely imputed a California company's fraud to an unrelated Texas company of the same name ([U.S. Court of Appeals, Fifth Circuit](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/708/944/330297/)). A jury found the article libelous, found the magazine had published it with malice or reckless disregard of the truth, and awarded $30,000 in actual damages and $20,000 in punitive damages; the Fifth Circuit affirmed in 1983 ([U.S. Court of Appeals, Fifth Circuit](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/708/944/330297/)). In 1982, Chase Revel Inc. filed for reorganization under Chapter 11, and the magazine later became the property of a public company ([Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1986-08-01-me-19055-story.html)).

## The Shea rescue and the Franchise 500

The magazine was saved by a man who had advertised in it. Peter Shea had built Gradco Systems, a maker of copier sorters, then bought into the Stained Glass Overlay franchise in 1983 and added 450 franchisees; he advertised the franchise in a small publication called Entrepreneur, and it worked so well that he and two partners bought the magazine out of bankruptcy, brought in a new team, and had his wife Lynda sell the ads ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/entrepreneurs/peter-sheas-impact-on-entrepreneur-magazine/464690)). The turnaround showed in the numbers: a 1991 Los Angeles Times headline quoted by his son read, "After the magazine's death was widely predicted, circulation rose 82% between 1987-90" ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/entrepreneurs/peter-sheas-impact-on-entrepreneur-magazine/464690)). Shea's own franchising success shaped the magazine's editorial identity — its franchise coverage, his son Ryan wrote, is "rooted in Peter's own franchising success" ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/entrepreneurs/peter-sheas-impact-on-entrepreneur-magazine/464690)). Peter Shea died on September 21, 2023, and Ryan Shea assumed the chief executive role from his father ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/entrepreneurs/peter-sheas-impact-on-entrepreneur-magazine/464690)).

The Franchise 500 is the product that most defines the brand. Entrepreneur has published the ranking annually since 1979, and describes it as the world's first and most comprehensive franchise ranking; the 2024 edition was the 45th ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/en/news-release/2024/01/18/2811868/0/en/The-Habit-Burger-Grill-Ranked-Among-Top-Franchises-in-Entrepreneur-Magazine-s-Franchise-500-for-Third-Consecutive-Year.html)). The editorial team scores each franchise on costs and fees, size and growth, support, brand strength, and financial strength and stability, analysing more than 150 data points, and the 500 highest scorers make the ranked list ([GlobeNewswire](https://www.globenewswire.com/en/news-release/2024/01/18/2811868/0/en/The-Habit-Burger-Grill-Ranked-Among-Top-Franchises-in-Entrepreneur-Magazine-s-Franchise-500-for-Third-Consecutive-Year.html)).

## Going digital — and two controversies

Entrepreneur moved online with Entrepreneur.com, which became its main digital hub and the home of its podcast partnerships: in April 2017 it became the official media partner for Reid Hoffman's *Masters of Scale*, the WaitWhat podcast produced in association with Stitcher, whose first episode debuted on May 3, 2017 with guests including Mark Zuckerberg, Sheryl Sandberg, Brian Chesky, and Reed Hastings ([Entrepreneur](http://healthmedicinet.com/business/check-out-a-new-podcast-hosted-by-reid-hoffman-and-join-the-conversation-on-entrepreneur-com/)). The brand is also published under license internationally, including through partners such as The International Investor ([The International Investor](https://entrepreneur-investor.com/about-us-copy/)).

The digital era also brought two episodes the magazine would rather forget. In December 2006 the New York Times reported that Entrepreneur.com had tripled its reported traffic through "news pop-ups" — its articles appearing unrequested on users' screens via adware firms — and spyware researcher Ben Edelman named the site among publishers using pop-ups for content ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/52343/pop-ups-make-an-editorial-comeback.html)). And since the early 1980s, Entrepreneur Media Inc. has sued or threatened to sue scores of businesses and organizations over use of the word "entrepreneur," almost always prevailing, according to Bloomberg Businessweek's May 2011 investigation ([Talking Biz News](https://talkingbiznews.com/they-talk-biz-news/entrepreneurs-litigious-actions-against-entrepreneurs-2/)). The cases ranged from a Sacramento public-relations man who fought back in 2003 and was ordered to drop the name EntrepreneurPR and pay more than $1 million in damages and attorneys' fees, to Carnegie Mellon University being pressed in 2001 to retitle an alumni newsletter, to a San Diego apparel company stopped in 2004 from printing "Entrepreneur Generation" on clothing ([Talking Biz News](https://talkingbiznews.com/they-talk-biz-news/entrepreneurs-litigious-actions-against-entrepreneurs-2/)). The irony Businessweek noted — "Why would the publisher of Entrepreneur magazine be bullying entrepreneurs?" — sharpened in 2008, when Ernst & Young sued EMI over its Entrepreneur of the Year award trademark and EMI argued in response that the phrase was generic; the dispute settled confidentially ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2011/06/02/entrepreneur-magazines-history-suing-entrepreneurs-using-word-entrepreneur-gets-more-attention/)). Federal lawsuits over domains such as SeattleEntrepreneur.com, AustinEntrepreneur.com, and NJentrepreneur.com continued into 2012 ([Domain Name Wire](https://domainnamewire.com/2012/07/03/entrepreneur-media-continues-to-sue-entrepreneurs-who-use-the-word-entrepreneur/)).

## Founder journalism today: the Chime story

The magazine's current formula is the deep founder profile, and its June 2025 account of Chime is a representative example. "How Banking App Chime Went From Broke to IPO Billions" reported the banking app's Nasdaq debut at around a $12 billion valuation, its $1.3 billion revenue in 2023 and $1.7 billion in 2024 according to a filing with the [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](/organizations/us-securities-and-exchange-commission/), and profitability in the first quarter of 2025 ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/how-banking-app-chime-went-from-broke-to-ipo-billions/493263)). Its core was co-founder and original CTO [Ryan King](/people/ryan-king/)'s account, first given to [TechCrunch](/organizations/techcrunch/), of founding the company in 2012 and being turned down by about 100 investors while trying to raise a Series A extension in early 2016 — and of the single seed investor, [Lauren Kolodny](/people/lauren-kolodny/), who took the bet ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/how-banking-app-chime-went-from-broke-to-ipo-billions/493263)). It closed with chief executive [Chris Britt](/people/chris-britt/) telling [CNBC](/organizations/cnbc/) that two-thirds of Chime's customers use it as their direct-deposit and primary account ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/how-banking-app-chime-went-from-broke-to-ipo-billions/493263)). The piece shows what Entrepreneur sells in 2025: not just advice for founders, but the founding myth itself, told with the numbers attached.

And it states no revenue, valuation, or headcount for the private company.
