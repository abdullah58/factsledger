Bloomberg Law occupies an unusual double role in the American legal world: it both **honors** the profession's pro-bono work and **reports on** the profession's fights. In November 2022, it named its inaugural class of Pro Bono Innovators — fourteen law firms and one nonprofit celebrated for creative pro-bono service ([Introducing Our 2022 Pro Bono Innovators](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/introducing-our-2022-pro-bono-innovators)). Two of the honorees, the MacArthur Justice Center and Jenner & Block, would soon sit at the center of the most dramatic confrontation between the [White House](https://factsledger.com/organizations/the-white-house) and the legal profession in modern memory. The platform that honored them would also chronicle that confrontation in its own newsroom. No other profile of Bloomberg Law has connected these threads; this one starts with them.

## What Bloomberg Law is

Bloomberg Law is a subscription-based legal research platform for attorneys, law students, and other legal professionals, introduced by Bloomberg L.P. and officially launched in December 2009 ([LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2012/04/bloomberg-law-now-fully-integrates-bna-content.html)). From the start, its stated ambition was blunt: compete against Westlaw and LexisNexis on their own turf. Legal-tech journalist Bob Ambrogi, who tracked the platform from birth, credited it for "getting into the game with swagger" — loading up on primary legal content, building its own editorial enhancements, and developing its own citator to rival Shepard's and KeyCite — while also calling it "a luxury yacht only partially constructed" ([LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2012/04/bloomberg-law-now-fully-integrates-bna-content.html)).

Two things set the platform apart from its launch. First, its pricing: Bloomberg Law was and remains a flat-rate, all-access platform — one subscription price, no metered per-search fees of the kind Westlaw and LexisNexis were known for ([LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2012/04/bloomberg-law-now-fully-integrates-bna-content.html)). Second, its parentage: the platform sits on top of Bloomberg's news and financial network, so legal research comes bundled with business and current-awareness content that the legacy research platforms could not match ([LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2012/04/bloomberg-law-now-fully-integrates-bna-content.html)). It is part of Bloomberg Industry Group, an affiliate of Bloomberg L.P., alongside Bloomberg Tax and Bloomberg Government ([Bloomberg Industry Group](https://www.bloombergindustry.com/press-releases/bloomberg-industry-group-regology/)).

## Building the platform: BNA and beyond

Bloomberg Law's biggest content leap came from the 2011 acquisition of BNA, the century-old publisher of specialized legal and regulatory materials. Bloomberg announced the deal in August 2011, and by April 2012 all BNA content was fully integrated into the platform — at no extra cost to subscribers, consistent with the flat-rate model ([LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2012/04/bloomberg-law-now-fully-integrates-bna-content.html)). The integration pulled in more than 120 current-awareness law reports (including the *Daily Labor Report*, the *Daily Tax Report*, and *United States Law Week*), BNA's professional books and treatises such as *Harmon on Patents*, and its practitioner-written portfolio series ([LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2012/04/bloomberg-law-now-fully-integrates-bna-content.html)). BNA's CEO Greg McCaffery told Ambrogi his company had "always [lacked] a good legal research platform on which to showcase our content," and the BNA editorial team in Arlington, Virginia, joined Bloomberg's legal division ([LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2012/04/bloomberg-law-now-fully-integrates-bna-content.html)).

The platform kept adding functionality and marquee clients. The American Association of Law Libraries gave Bloomberg Law its 2012 New Product Award, and around the same time the firm DLA Piper contracted to integrate the service across its 1,400 lawyers ([LawSites](https://www.lawnext.com/2012/04/bloomberg-law-now-fully-integrates-bna-content.html)). In June 2026, Bloomberg Industry Group acquired Regology, an AI-powered global regulatory intelligence platform, to fold AI-driven regulatory change management into Bloomberg Law, Bloomberg Tax, and Bloomberg Government — Regology's Smart Law Library monitors U.S. federal and state regulations plus rules in more than 135 countries ([Bloomberg Industry Group](https://www.bloombergindustry.com/press-releases/bloomberg-industry-group-regology/)).

## The Pro Bono Innovators program

Bloomberg Law's awards program for pro-bono work began with nominations solicited from across the legal profession. Editors evaluated nominees on the innovativeness of their approaches to client service, their pro-bono track records, and the breadth and impact of their matters ([Introducing Our 2022 Pro Bono Innovators](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/introducing-our-2022-pro-bono-innovators)). The inaugural 2022 class that resulted included firms honored for work such as Skadden's unanimous Supreme Court win in *Caniglia v. Strom*, Hunton Andrews Kurth's Federal Circuit veterans'-benefits victory potentially affecting up to 1.7 million veterans, and Latham & Watkins's 157-page investigative report on the police killing of Elijah McClain ([Introducing Our 2022 Pro Bono Innovators](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/introducing-our-2022-pro-bono-innovators)).

The single nonprofit in the inaugural class was the MacArthur Justice Center, honored for a Supreme Court and Appellate Program strategy that joined appeals midstream — coming in on appeal to represent clients who could not afford counsel below, rather than growing cases from trial-court litigation ([Pro Bono Innovators 2022 Honoree: MacArthur Justice Center](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/pro-bono-innovators-2022-honoree-macarthur-justice-center)). The work Bloomberg Law cited included the Supreme Court case *McCoy v. Alamu* and the Tenth Circuit case *Ashaheed v. Currington*, both chipping away at qualified immunity, plus *Thompson v. Clark*, where the Center argued — and the Supreme Court agreed — that the end of a criminal proceeding in anything but conviction or guilty plea satisfies the "favorable termination" standard for a Fourth Amendment claim ([Pro Bono Innovators 2022 Honoree: MacArthur Justice Center](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/pro-bono-innovators-2022-honoree-macarthur-justice-center)). The honoree Q&A identifies the lead appellate lawyers in the three cases, naming **Amir Ali**, then the Center's executive director, as the lead in *Thompson* ([Pro Bono Innovators 2022 Honoree: MacArthur Justice Center](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/pro-bono-innovators-2022-honoree-macarthur-justice-center)). Ali's biography at the judicial-confirmation advocacy site CourtsMatter lists "Bloomberg Law's Inaugural Pro Bono Innovators Award" among his personal awards ([CourtsMatter](https://courtsmatter.org/nominees/amir-ali/)).

Ali's path runs straight through the other honoree in this story. He began his legal career at **Jenner & Block**, where in his fifth year he was chosen to argue *Welch v. United States* before the Supreme Court — and won 7–1 for his client ([CourtsMatter](https://courtsmatter.org/nominees/amir-ali/)). He went on to lead the MacArthur Justice Center's Supreme Court and Appellate Program, became its president and executive director, and was later confirmed as a federal judge for the District of Columbia ([CourtsMatter](https://courtsmatter.org/nominees/amir-ali/)).

## The 2025 arc: honorees at the center of the EO war

In 2025, President Trump issued a series of executive orders targeting law firms he accused of opposing his interests — stripping lawyers of security clearances, barring firm personnel from federal buildings, and directing agencies to review the firms' clients' contracts for possible termination ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/states-of-play/trumps-order-against-perkins-coie-struck-down-by-judge)). Bloomberg Law's newsroom covered the fallout closely: when Judge Beryl Howell struck down Trump's March 6 order against Perkins Coie, Bloomberg Law reported it as "another blow against the White House's efforts to punish law firms for taking up causes against his interests" ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/states-of-play/trumps-order-against-perkins-coie-struck-down-by-judge)).

Two of the firms and people in Bloomberg Law's 2022 honoree class then took starring roles. In March 2025, Judge Ali — the same lawyer Bloomberg Law had honored for *Thompson v. Clark* — ordered the Trump administration to release nearly $2 billion in USAID funds frozen by a January 20 executive order, ruling that the president had exceeded his constitutional authority by withholding money Congress had appropriated; the Supreme Court declined, 5–4, to halt Ali's order ([The Economic Times](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/international/us/federal-judge-orders-trump-administration-to-pay-2b-in-withheld-usaid-funds-supreme-court-denies-appeal-citing-constitutional-overreach-and-legal-violations/articleshow/118894444.cms)).

And Jenner & Block — Ali's former firm and a fellow 2022 Pro Bono Innovator — sued over the executive order targeting it and won. In May 2025, U.S. District Judge John Bates, a Republican appointee, struck the order down, writing that it "makes no bones about why it chose its target: it picked Jenner because of the causes Jenner champions, the clients Jenner represents and a lawyer Jenner once employed" ([The Global Legal Post](https://www.globallegalpost.com/news/uniquely-harmful-judge-overturns-executive-order-against-jenner-block-2131697657)). Bates was especially critical of the order's focus on the firm's pro-bono work, calling it "uniquely harmful": "When law firms volunteer to represent vulnerable individuals and groups without pay, they embody the best of the profession" ([The Global Legal Post](https://www.globallegalpost.com/news/uniquely-harmful-judge-overturns-executive-order-against-jenner-block-2131697657)).

That is the detail that makes the Bloomberg Law story cohere. The platform built an awards program to celebrate pro-bono lawyering; three years later, a judge called the White House's targeting of a pro-bono practice "uniquely harmful" — and both the judge making the earlier pro-bono case (Ali, via the USAID fight) and the firm in the dock (Jenner & Block) were names on Bloomberg Law's own 2022 honor roll, while Bloomberg Law's reporters documented it all.

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim that Bloomberg Law's 2022 awards caused or predicted the 2025 litigation; the overlap is a retrospective observation, not a causal argument. It does not rank Bloomberg Law against Westlaw or LexisNexis by subscriber count, revenue, or market share — those figures were not verified. It does not state the exact date or vote margin of Amir Ali's judicial confirmation, nor the outcomes of appeals still moving through the courts in 2026. And where sources frame the 2022 honor differently — Bloomberg Law's list honors the MacArthur Justice Center as an organization, while CourtsMatter lists the award among Ali's personal honors — this profile reports both framings as stated rather than reconciling them.
