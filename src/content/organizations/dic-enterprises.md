Every search result about DIC Entertainment lists its cartoons — *Inspector Gadget*, *The Real Ghostbusters*, *Sailor Moon*'s English seasons — as if the company were only ever a cartoon factory. But the numbers that actually made DIC valuable tell a different story: the studio's defining business achievement was taking a doll that had been dormant for 15 years and turning it into one of the biggest licensing comebacks in children's entertainment, with more than $5 billion in retail sales of licensed product since 2002 across Strawberry Shortcake and Care Bears combined. That figure comes not from a fan wiki but from the trade press covering DIC's 2008 sale to Cookie Jar — and nobody on the first page of the SERP connects the dots.

The relaunch began in 2002, when Los Angeles-based DIC partnered with American Greetings to develop new entertainment and handle licensing for Strawberry Shortcake. The property's history made it an unlikely bet: the original redheaded rag doll had sold 25 million units between 1981 and 1986 and generated US$1.2 billion in books, videos, records, and other licensed items over five years — all without a TV show, save six specials from the early 1980s. Then the brand sat dormant for 15 years. DIC's strategy, as reported by Kidscreen in March 2002, centered on four new holiday-themed animated specials released quarterly on video, each backed by a large promotional program including a QSR (quick-service restaurant) tie-in, with a deal in principle already in place for a U.S. broadcaster to air them that fall.

The plan worked beyond what the industry expected. By the time Cookie Jar Group bought DIC in 2008, WorldScreen reported that Strawberry Shortcake alone had generated more than $3 billion at retail since DIC relaunched it in 2004 — and License Global put the combined Strawberry Shortcake/Care Bears retail figure above $5 billion since the 2002 re-introduction. The deal terms reveal what Cookie Jar was actually buying: roughly $31.5 million for all of DIC's equity in a transaction valued at approximately $87.6 million, and alongside the shows, DIC's relationships with consumer brands such as McDonald's, General Mills, and Wal-Mart. American Greetings received a 10-year exclusive inbound licensing agreement for social expression products on all three properties. Cookie Jar president and COO Toper Taylor said the acquisition "expands our merchandising and licensing capabilities," while Cookie Jar CEO Michael Hirsh added a personal note: "I first had the pleasure of working with Andy Heyward on the original *Inspector Gadget* series." The merger was announced on June 20, 2008, creating a combined library of approximately 6,000 half-hours.

That licensing turnaround was only the last act of a company that had reinvented itself several times over. DIC began in 1971 as DIC Audiovisuel, founded in Paris by Frenchman Jean Chalopin as a subsidiary of Radio-Television Luxembourg (RTL); "DIC" was originally an acronym for Diffusion, Information et Communication. The American arm, DIC Enterprises, was established in 1982 in Burbank, California, by Andy Heyward, Robby London, and Michael Maliani — set up to adapt DIC productions into English for American audiences.

What followed reads like a case study in kids'-media private equity. According to a Kartoon Studios SEC proxy statement, Heyward co-founded DIC Animation City in 1983 and served as its CEO until selling the company to Capital Cities/ABC in 1993. When The Walt Disney Company bought Capital Cities/ABC in 1995, DIC went along with it — and Heyward kept running the company under Disney until 2000, when he bought it back with assistance from Bain Capital, becoming chairman and CEO of DIC Entertainment Corporation. He later took the company public on London's Alternative Investment Market (AIM) before selling it to Cookie Jar in 2008. Few people in children's television can claim to have owned the same studio three separate times in three different corporate forms.

The shows themselves were formidable: the proxy statement credits Heyward with producing more than 5,000 half-hours of children's entertainment, including *Inspector Gadget*, *The Real Ghostbusters*, *Strawberry Shortcake*, *Care Bears*, *Alvin and the Chipmunks*, *Hello Kitty's Furry Tale Theater*, *The Super Mario Brothers Super Show*, *The Adventures of Sonic the Hedgehog*, *Sabrina the Animated Series*, and *Captain Planet and the Planeteers*. The company also licensed anime such as the first two English seasons of *Sailor Moon*, and while under Disney it produced live-action films.

One name in DIC's alumni file rarely gets mentioned alongside the cartoons: [Chuck Lorre](/people/chuck-lorre). Before he became the most successful comedy writer-producer of the 2000s, Lorre got his start working for DIC Enterprises, with animation credits on *Pole Position* (1984) and *Heathcliff: The Movie* (1986). His first live-action writing job, on *Charles in Charge* in 1987, came immediately after — the bridge from DIC's cartoon floor to *Roseanne* (1990–92) and eventually *Two and a Half Men* and *The Big Bang Theory*.

The 2008 deal also carried a one-third interest in KidsCo, the international children's channel DIC had built with partners — the merger announcement noted it as part of the package. After the merger, DIC was folded into Cookie Jar's operations entirely; Cookie Jar was itself acquired by DHX Media in 2012 (now WildBrain), which holds most of the DIC library today.

So the full picture of DIC isn't just a logo that flashed before Saturday-morning cartoons. It's a French commercial-production house that became an American cartoon studio, that became a Disney subsidiary, that became a Bain-backed buyout, that became a London-listed public company — and that finally proved its worth not with a new cartoon but by resurrecting a dead doll brand into billions of dollars in retail. The cartoons were the visible product; the turnarounds were the business.
