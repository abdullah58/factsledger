## Overview

**AARP Services Inc. (ASI) is the for-profit, taxable subsidiary of [AARP](/organizations/aarp/), the senior advocacy organization** — a Delaware-incorporated company whose board is appointed by AARP, Inc.'s board. It negotiates and manages the brand-licensing deals — insurance, travel, financial products — that turn the AARP name into royalties: $1.2 billion in 2024 against $289 million in member dues. ([Grant Thornton LLP (audited AARP financials)](https://www.presentationnation.com/api/assets/companies/aarp/presentations/aarp-consolidated-financial-statements-2024-annual-report-2024/original.pdf))

ASI is the commercial side of the organization most people only know as a membership club. Its board is appointed by AARP, Inc.'s board, and it is run as a taxable business: it manages provider relationships, performs quality-control oversight to make sure licensees use AARP's trademarks and service marks properly, develops new products, and sells advertising and sponsorships across AARP's print and online publications, AARP.org, and AARP Broadcast. A 2007 Stanford GSB case study described ASI's operation as "one of the largest social enterprises in the country." ([Stanford Graduate School of Business](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/case-studies/aarp-aarp-services-multi-sector-approach-social-change))

## Why a nonprofit owns a for-profit company

ASI exists because AARP's commercial success kept colliding with its nonprofit status. A 2007 Stanford GSB case study recorded that ASI, the "wholly owned, taxable (earned income activities) subsidiary," managed relationships with AARP-endorsed businesses generating over $500 million in royalties from health insurance, life insurance, mutual funds, and other products — calling the operation "one of the largest social enterprises in the country." ([Stanford Graduate School of Business](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/case-studies/aarp-aarp-services-multi-sector-approach-social-change)) The case study's framing is the honest one: AARP is not only an advocacy group but a licensing business, and ASI is the vehicle built to hold it.

That taxable container is what lets the nonprofit parent keep its distance from the commerce it profits from. AARP, Inc. owns ASI outright and appoints its board, but the subsidiary transacts as a business — negotiating deals, overseeing licensees, developing products — while the parent advocates. That separation is what keeps ASI's commercial income from endangering the parent's tax-exempt status under [United States](/places/united-states/) tax law.

## The 1999 IRS settlement behind ASI

In a 2018 federal class-action complaint, plaintiffs alleged that ASI was created in 1999 under a settlement with the Internal Revenue Service, after the IRS investigated the large income AARP was earning from its "endorsement" deals — and that related settlements with the U.S. Postal Service and the [District of Columbia](/places/district-of-columbia/) tax authorities addressed failures to pay unrelated business income tax on commercial activity, including mailing health insurance solicitations at nonprofit rates. ([U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania (complaint)](https://truthinadvertising.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Christoph-v-AARP-complaint.pdf)) Those are the plaintiffs' allegations, not court findings; what is independently established by AARP's audited accounts is the structural fact that emerged: a Delaware-incorporated, wholly-owned taxable subsidiary whose role is to be the business entity the nonprofit parent cannot legally be.

The dating across sources is slightly uneven — the audited financials say ASI was incorporated in Delaware in 1998, while AARP's own boilerplate and the complaint describe its founding/creation in 1999 — most plausibly incorporation followed by activation after the settlement. Either way, ASI's origin is inseparable from the moment AARP's endorsement business needed a taxable home of its own.

## What ASI manages: the partner portfolio

ASI's day-to-day work is partner management at scale. It sits between AARP, Inc. — which owns the trademarks — and the independent companies that sell AARP-branded products and services to members. It selects and oversees those providers, performs quality-control checks on how licensees use the AARP name, develops new product lines, and even sells advertising and sponsorships across AARP's own media properties, collecting fees from both AARP, Inc. and outside clients. ([Grant Thornton LLP (audited AARP financials)](https://www.presentationnation.com/api/assets/companies/aarp/presentations/aarp-consolidated-financial-statements-2024-annual-report-2024/original.pdf)) Its announced provider relationships span health, financial, travel, home, and telecom products:

| Category | Example providers (all via ASI announcements) |
|---|---|
| Health insurance | [UnitedHealthcare](/organizations/unitedhealthcare/) (Medicare supplemental); Soundly hearing care |
| Financial | Credit cards; auto, home, and life insurance; annuities |
| Travel & leisure | All My Sons Moving & Storage; Shell Fuel Rewards; Laithwaites Wine |
| Home services | American Home Shield home warranties |
| Telecom | SpeakEasy / Consumer Cellular wireless |

The products are sold by third parties, not by AARP or its affiliates: providers pay a royalty fee to AARP for the use of its intellectual property, and those fees are used for AARP's general purposes. ([PR Newswire (via Webull)](https://www.webull.com/news/14048262285132800)) ASI is the company in the middle — negotiating the relationships, policing the brand, and building the next generation of them.

## The royalty engine and the UnitedHealth deal

The defining deal remains the one with [UnitedHealth Group](/organizations/unitedhealth-group/). The 2018 complaint described the AARP Medigap relationship as ASI's signature "lucrative contract," with the carriers paying a percentage of premiums for the use of AARP's intellectual property — a payment the defendants call a royalty and the plaintiffs alleged was really an unlicensed 4.95% insurance-producer commission on each policy sold or renewed. ([U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania (complaint)](https://truthinadvertising.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Christoph-v-AARP-complaint.pdf))

The numbers explain why the arrangement is both valuable and contested. In 2024 AARP reported $1.204 billion in royalties against $288.8 million in membership dues — licensing revenue more than four times what members pay, which means ASI's licensing operation, not the membership, is the organization's true economic engine. ([Grant Thornton LLP (audited AARP financials)](https://www.presentationnation.com/api/assets/companies/aarp/presentations/aarp-consolidated-financial-statements-2024-annual-report-2024/original.pdf))

## ASI is not AARP, and not the Foundation

ASI is routinely confused with two other AARP entities, and each distinction matters. First, ASI is not AARP itself: AARP, Inc. is the nonprofit advocacy organization; ASI is the taxable Delaware subsidiary that runs the commerce. Second, ASI is not the [AARP Foundation](/organizations/aarp-foundation/): the Foundation is a 501(c)(3) charity incorporated in 1961 in the District of Columbia that works on poverty among older adults — tax-exempt, mission-driven, and legally separate from the for-profit licensing arm. ([Grant Thornton LLP (audited AARP financials)](https://www.presentationnation.com/api/assets/companies/aarp/presentations/aarp-consolidated-financial-statements-2024-annual-report-2024/original.pdf)) When AARP speaks as an advocate or runs anti-poverty programs, that is the nonprofit and the Foundation; when the AARP name appears on an insurance card, ASI is the entity that put it there.

## ASI is not the AARP Insurance Plan either

The third confusion is the most consequential. The [AARP Insurance Plan](/organizations/aarp-insurance-plan/) — also called the AARP Health Trust — is a grantor trust established in 1958 that collects, invests, and remits insurance premiums and routes the royalty payments from carriers to AARP, Inc. In July 2024 the Plan's trust agreement was restated after a royalty-agreement restructuring, and it is administered by trustees appointed by AARP's board together with a participating group health insurance provider. ([Grant Thornton LLP (audited AARP financials)](https://www.presentationnation.com/api/assets/companies/aarp/presentations/aarp-consolidated-financial-statements-2024-annual-report-2024/original.pdf)) In short: the Plan is the plumbing the money flows through, while ASI is the company that negotiates and polices the deals. Knowing which entity does what — advocate, charity, trust, or licensing company — is the difference between understanding AARP and being misled by it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is AARP Services a nonprofit?**
No. It is a wholly-owned taxable subsidiary of AARP, Inc., incorporated in Delaware, and its board is appointed by AARP, Inc.'s board. It pays taxes on its earnings, unlike its nonprofit parent and the AARP Foundation. ([Grant Thornton LLP (audited AARP financials)](https://www.presentationnation.com/api/assets/companies/aarp/presentations/aarp-consolidated-financial-statements-2024-annual-report-2024/original.pdf))

**Does AARP Services sell insurance?**
It does not underwrite insurance. It manages the provider relationships for, and performs quality-control oversight of, the products and services that carry the AARP name and are offered by independent providers — including Medicare supplemental insurance, auto, home, and life insurance — plus travel, financial, and life-event services. ([AARP Services (via EIN Presswire)](https://foodsafety.einnews.com/pr_news/949919125/all-my-sons-offers-aarp-members-exclusive-discounts-and-vip-service))

**Who runs AARP Services?**
Kevin Tator was named Chief Executive Officer in a September 2026 release; John Larew held the President and CEO title in an earlier release. The exact date of the transition could not be verified from a fully-read source. ([PR Newswire (via Morningstar)](https://www.morningstar.com/news/pr-newswire/20260923ny52630/aarp-adds-new-and-enhanced-member-benefits-to-support-everyday-living))

**Where does AARP's royalty money come from?**
From licensees — independent companies that pay a fee to use AARP's intellectual property on member products. AARP's own disclosures state these fees "are used for the general purposes of AARP," and in 2024 the organization reported $1.204 billion in royalties against $288.8 million in member dues. ([PR Newswire (via Webull)](https://www.webull.com/news/14048262285132800); [Grant Thornton LLP (audited AARP financials)](https://www.presentationnation.com/api/assets/companies/aarp/presentations/aarp-consolidated-financial-statements-2024-annual-report-2024/original.pdf))

**Why is the UnitedHealth relationship controversial?**
A 2018 federal class action alleged that payments UnitedHealth makes for the use of AARP's intellectual property — which the defendants call royalties — are really a 4.95% commission on each AARP-branded Medigap policy sold or renewed, charged on top of premiums and collected from an unlicensed entity. Those are plaintiffs' allegations; the case's outcome was not verified. ([U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania (complaint)](https://truthinadvertising.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Christoph-v-AARP-complaint.pdf))
