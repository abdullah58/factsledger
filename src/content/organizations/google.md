## Overview

In February 2023, while Microsoft's multibillion-dollar bet on OpenAI was resetting the AI industry, Google quietly took a stake in the other major AI lab: the [Financial Times](https://factsledger.com/organizations/financial-times/) confirmed that Google had invested around $300 million in [Anthropic](/organizations/anthropic/), worth roughly 10% of the company, while Anthropic publicly announced that it had chosen Google Cloud as its preferred cloud provider ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/artificial-intelligence-2/2023/googles-new-focus-on-ai-includes-300m-anthropic-investment/)). Eight months later the bet grew: an Anthropic spokesperson confirmed to Reuters that Google had agreed to invest up to $2 billion more — $500 million upfront plus another $1.5 billion over time — in a deal framed explicitly as an effort to keep pace with Microsoft ([Reuters](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/business/google-agrees-to-invest-up-to-2-billion-in-openai-rival-anthropic-11615901.html)). Anthropic, co-founded by former OpenAI executives and siblings [Dario Amodei](/people/dario-amodei/) and [Daniela Amodei](/people/daniela-amodei/), develops the Claude models; the two deals made Google one of the biggest financial backers of the company racing OpenAI ([Reuters](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/business/google-agrees-to-invest-up-to-2-billion-in-openai-rival-anthropic-11615901.html)).

Less than a year after that, a federal judge gave Google a very different kind of headline. On August 5, 2024, U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta ruled that Google had violated antitrust law to build and preserve an illegal monopoly over internet search ([Hindustan Times](https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/google-built-illegal-monopoly-to-become-worlds-default-search-engine-us-court-in-landmark-antitrust-case-101722909680744.html)). Mehta found that Google controls about 90% of online search and 95% of smartphone search, writing that "Google is a monopolist, and it has acted as one to maintain its monopoly." Google said it would appeal; U.S. Attorney General Merrick Garland called the decision a historic win for competition, and a second trial to determine remedies — with a possible breakup of [Alphabet](https://factsledger.com/organizations/alphabet/) on the table — was set to follow ([Hindustan Times](https://www.hindustantimes.com/world-news/google-built-illegal-monopoly-to-become-worlds-default-search-engine-us-court-in-landmark-antitrust-case-101722909680744.html)).

## A Stanford project called BackRub

The company behind both the Anthropic bets and the antitrust ruling began as a graduate-student project. In 1998, Larry Page and Sergey Brin founded Google, formalizing the work they had started on a search engine called BackRub built in a Stanford dorm room ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). The name was a play on "googol," the term for the number 1 followed by 100 zeros — an intended nod to the vast number of web pages; an early investor's misspelling stuck ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). Backing from Sun Microsystems co-founder Andy Bechtolsheim helped the pair raise about $1 million and open a first office in a Menlo Park garage ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)).

## Scale, auctions, and acquisitions

Google grew at a pace that made its name a verb. In 1999 it raised $25 million from two venture-capital firms, and in 2000 Yahoo selected it as the search engine for its site — the distribution deal that put Google in front of the mainstream web ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). The numbers followed: from hundreds of thousands of queries a day early on, Google reached 200 million searches a day by 2004 and roughly 3 billion a day by the end of 2011 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). Behind that scale sat its infrastructure work — the Google File System, Bigtable, and MapReduce — which let it distribute computing jobs across vast machine fleets in ways rivals could not match ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)).

The 2004 IPO was deliberately unconventional: a Dutch auction designed to give ordinary investors a fairer shot, raising $1.66 billion ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). Leadership had already professionalized — Eric Schmidt joined as CEO in 2001, forming a "triumvirate" with the founders, before Page returned to the top job in 2011 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). Google joined the S&P 500 in 2006, and acquisitions built out its advertising and product empire: AdSense ($102 million, 2003), dMarc ($102 million, 2006), a $900 million advertising agreement with MySpace, DoubleClick ($3.1 billion, 2007), and AdMob ($750 million) ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). In 2012 it bought Motorola Mobility ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). By the late 2000s the company held more than 70% of the search market and offered more than 50 products ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)).

## G is for Google, A is for Alphabet

In August 2015, Page announced the company's biggest corporate change since the IPO: Google would become a subsidiary of a new holding company called Alphabet, a "bit slimmed down" Google keeping Search, Maps, Android, YouTube, and the advertising business while the far-afield bets — Calico, Life Sciences, Nest, Fiber, Google Ventures, Google Capital, and Google X — moved under the Alphabet umbrella ([BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/google-s-new-parent-company-b-alphabet-b-and-what-it-means-for-b-life-sciences-b-calico-and-healthcare)). Page became Alphabet's CEO with Brin as president, and Google's former senior vice president of products, [Sundar Pichai](/people/sundar-pichai/), stepped up to run Google ([BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/google-s-new-parent-company-b-alphabet-b-and-what-it-means-for-b-life-sciences-b-calico-and-healthcare)). All Google shares converted into Alphabet shares, continuing to trade on the Nasdaq as GOOGL and GOOG ([BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/google-s-new-parent-company-b-alphabet-b-and-what-it-means-for-b-life-sciences-b-calico-and-healthcare)).

The business model underneath never changed much: advertising funds the machine. In 2023, ad revenue of $175 billion accounted for 57% of Google's revenue, per Alphabet's reporting as cited by Britannica ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)). The company remains headquartered in Mountain View, California ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Google-Inc)).

## The AI-race hedge

The Anthropic investments were Google's answer to an AI race it had helped start but was at risk of losing: Microsoft's OpenAI partnership had reset the board, and Google's own deep-learning research team, [Google Brain](/organizations/google-brain/), was absorbed into DeepMind in April 2023 to form Google DeepMind ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/artificial-intelligence-2/2023/googles-new-focus-on-ai-includes-300m-anthropic-investment/), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/04/20/google-consolidates-ai-research-divisions-into-google-deepmind/)). Pichai had told investors on Google's earnings call that AI was reaching an "inflection point" and described it as a powerful enabler for businesses ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/artificial-intelligence-2/2023/googles-new-focus-on-ai-includes-300m-anthropic-investment/)). Buying a large stake in the leading OpenAI rival was the hedge.

It cites the "G is for Google" announcement through contemporary coverage, not through the original blog post, which was not located. And it does not present Google's current product lineup, headcount, or market capitalization, which change constantly and were not verified at a fixed point for this profile.
