## Overview

8chan was an imageboard website of user-created message boards, created in 2013 by computer programmer Fredrick Brennan as a less-moderated alternative to 4chan, where users could post with minimal oversight ([NPR](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-08-05/uniquely-lawless-security-firm-drops-8chan-website-following-el-paso-shooting)). Its home page called it "the darkest reaches of the Internet," and by 2019 it drew about 15 million visitors a month ([NPR](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-08-05/uniquely-lawless-security-firm-drops-8chan-website-following-el-paso-shooting)). In 2019 the site became globally notorious when the perpetrators of three mass shootings — in Christchurch, [New Zealand](/places/new-zealand/); Poway, [California](/places/california/); and El Paso, Texas — each posted a manifesto or final message there before attacking ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/terminating-service-for-8chan/)). [Cloudflare](/organizations/cloudflare/) terminated 8chan as a customer effective midnight on August 5, 2019, calling it "a cesspool of hate," and the site returned three months later under the new name 8kun ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/terminating-service-for-8chan/); [Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)).

## Origins and growth

Brennan created 8chan after concluding that 4chan, the imageboard it spun off from, had become too heavily moderated; the new site pitched itself as a place where uncensored speech could thrive ([NPR](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-08-05/uniquely-lawless-security-firm-drops-8chan-website-following-el-paso-shooting)). Its home page banner read "Welcome to 8chan, the Darkest Reaches of the Internet," alongside the slogan "Embrace infamy," and despite a disclaimer that content violating U.S. law would be deleted, the site quickly became a free-for-all permitting racist, sexist, and homophobic content and graphic imagery, particularly on its /pol/ ("politically incorrect") board ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)). Growth was rapid: according to Wired, cited by CREST, user migration took the site from roughly 10 posts a day in 2013 to around 5,000 posts an hour by the end of 2014 ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)). Brennan later walked away from the site, saying some of its content had become toxic, and by 2019 it was run by father-and-son Jim and Ron Watkins; Brennan publicly criticized the Watkinses and thanked Cloudflare for ending its ties to the site ([NPR](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-08-05/uniquely-lawless-security-firm-drops-8chan-website-following-el-paso-shooting)). The site also became closely associated with the QAnon conspiracy theory, whose followers were among its user base, and with "incel" communities ([NPR](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-08-05/uniquely-lawless-security-firm-drops-8chan-website-following-el-paso-shooting); CREST notes claims that Jim Watkins himself was behind Q's posts, while stressing there is "no hard evidence" for that ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)).

## The 2019 shootings and the Cloudflare termination

In 2019, 8chan moved from an obscure corner of chan culture to global headlines in the space of five months, as three attackers used it to publish their messages before killing. The table below gives the tolls as independently verified — not as first reported.

| Attack | Date | Killed | Wounded | 8chan link |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Christchurch mosque shootings](/events/christchurch-mosque-shootings/) | March 15, 2019 | 51 | — | Attacker posted a final message and a link to his manifesto and a livestream |
| Poway synagogue shooting | April 27, 2019 | 1 | 3 | Attacker posted a manifesto link to /pol/ |
| [El Paso Walmart shooting](/events/el-paso-shooting-2019/) | August 3, 2019 | 23 | 22 | Attacker posted a manifesto minutes before the attack |
| Dayton, Ohio shooting | August 4, 2019 | 9 | 27 | **None — listed only because it is often bundled with El Paso** |

In Christchurch, the attacker posted his final message to 8chan, addressing the "lads" and warning of an imminent attack, and linked to a Facebook livestream and his manifesto, "The Great Replacement" ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)). In Poway, California, the attacker posted to the site's /pol/ board and linked his manifesto, describing the Christchurch attack as a "catalyst" ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)); the FBI records one person killed and three wounded there ([Federal Bureau of Investigation / ALERRT Center](https://www.justice.gov/usao-mdpa/page/file/1272096/download)). In El Paso, the gunman posted a four-page manifesto to 8chan minutes before opening fire at the Cielo Vista Walmart, describing the attack as "a response to the Hispanic invasion of Texas" and citing the Christchurch manifesto as a primary influence ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)). The final toll in El Paso was 23 killed and 22 injured: Patrick Wood Crusius pleaded guilty to 90 federal counts and was sentenced to 90 consecutive life sentences ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/texas-man-sentenced-90-consecutive-life-sentences-2019-mass-shooting-walmart-el-paso-texas)). Note that Cloudflare's own August 5, 2019 post says the El Paso attack killed "20 people" — the toll known that day; the figure later rose to 23 ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/texas-man-sentenced-90-consecutive-life-sentences-2019-mass-shooting-walmart-el-paso-texas)). The [Dayton shooting](/events/dayton-shooting-2019/) the next day, which killed 9 and wounded 27, is often mentioned in the same breath but had no connection to 8chan: Cloudflare's post names Dayton only as a same-weekend tragedy, and attributes the 8chan link to El Paso alone ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/terminating-service-for-8chan/); [Federal Bureau of Investigation / ALERRT Center](https://www.justice.gov/usao-mdpa/page/file/1272096/download)).

Cloudflare's decision was not immediate. On August 3, the day of the El Paso shooting, chief executive [Matthew Prince](/people/matthew-prince/) initially defended the company's support for the site; by the next day, under press attention, he had reversed course — telling The Guardian he wanted to "kick 8chan off our network" while telling The New York Times he hesitated because termination could make it harder for law enforcement to access the site ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/08/04/cloudflare-will-stop-service-to-8chan-which-ceo-matthew-prince-describes-as-a-cesspool-of-hate/)). Hours earlier, Cloudflare's general counsel had told BuzzFeed News that cutting the site off would set a dangerous precedent ([NPR](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-08-05/uniquely-lawless-security-firm-drops-8chan-website-following-el-paso-shooting)). In the early hours of August 5, Prince posted that "8chan has repeatedly proven itself to be a cesspool of hate" and that the company had "just sent notice that we are terminating 8chan as a customer effective at midnight tonight Pacific Time," adding: "The rationale is simple: they have proven themselves to be lawless and that lawlessness has caused multiple tragic deaths" ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/terminating-service-for-8chan/)). The site was online only sporadically that morning and was offline by 2:45 p.m. ET ([NPR](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-08-05/uniquely-lawless-security-firm-drops-8chan-website-following-el-paso-shooting)). Prince wrote that Cloudflare drew the line at "platforms that have demonstrated they directly inspire tragic events and are lawless by design," while conceding the action "will almost certainly not even remove 8chan from the Internet" ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/terminating-service-for-8chan/)). Brennan, the site's creator, publicly thanked Cloudflare and said the site should be shut down ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/08/04/cloudflare-will-stop-service-to-8chan-which-ceo-matthew-prince-describes-as-a-cesspool-of-hate/)). Cloudflare had previously terminated the [Daily Stormer](/organizations/daily-stormer/) in August 2017 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/08/04/cloudflare-will-stop-service-to-8chan-which-ceo-matthew-prince-describes-as-a-cesspool-of-hate/)).

## 8kun and the aftermath

Cloudflare's prediction proved accurate: 8chan turned to BitMitigate, a security firm that described itself as a non-discriminatory free-speech provider, and switched its domain registration from Tucows to Epik ([NPR](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-08-05/uniquely-lawless-security-firm-drops-8chan-website-following-el-paso-shooting)). In September 2019, owner Jim Watkins testified before the U.S. House Committee on Homeland Security, defending the site as a bastion of the First Amendment ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)). The site returned to the clearnet in early November 2019 as 8kun, still owned by Watkins, but it was not a like-for-like replacement: the infamous /pol/ board was gone, replaced by /pnd/ ("Politics, News, Debate"), new boards required administrator approval instead of free creation, and the home page motto softened to "Speak freely — speak legally" ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)). CREST's December 2020 assessment found the user base largely rejected the successor as a poor substitute, with many users moving to Discord and Telegram instead, and observed no further use of 8kun to post final messages or manifestos before far-right attacks — though past attackers continued to be glorified there ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)). Even 8kun proved hard to keep online: in October 2020 it briefly went offline after the Oregon-based cybersecurity company CNServers revoked its DDoS protection, returning almost immediately after switching to the Russian company ddos-guard.net ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What was 8chan?**
An imageboard website of user-created message boards, created in 2013 by Fredrick Brennan as a less-moderated alternative to 4chan, whose home page called it "the darkest reaches of the Internet" ([NPR](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-08-05/uniquely-lawless-security-firm-drops-8chan-website-following-el-paso-shooting)).

**Why did Cloudflare terminate 8chan?**
After the El Paso gunman posted a manifesto to the site minutes before killing 23 people — the third 2019 shooting linked to 8chan after Christchurch and Poway — Cloudflare said the site had "proven themselves to be lawless and that lawlessness has caused multiple tragic deaths" ([Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/terminating-service-for-8chan/)).

**How many people were killed in the El Paso shooting?**
23 killed and 22 injured, according to the U.S. Department of Justice's sentencing release; early reports, including Cloudflare's August 5, 2019 post, said 20, the toll known on the day ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/texas-man-sentenced-90-consecutive-life-sentences-2019-mass-shooting-walmart-el-paso-texas)).

**Was the Dayton shooting connected to 8chan?**
No. The Dayton shooting of August 4, 2019 killed 9 and wounded 27, but no source connects the Dayton shooter to 8chan; Cloudflare's post mentions Dayton only as a same-weekend tragedy ([Federal Bureau of Investigation / ALERRT Center](https://www.justice.gov/usao-mdpa/page/file/1272096/download); [Cloudflare Blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/terminating-service-for-8chan/)).

**What happened to 8chan after 2019?**
It returned in November 2019 as 8kun under the same owner, Jim Watkins, but without the /pol/ board and with boards requiring administrator approval; researchers found much of the user base rejected it and moved elsewhere ([Centre for Research and Evidence on Security Threats (CREST)](https://www.crestresearch.ac.uk/download/3417/after_8chan.pdf)).
