## Overview

The Taliban is a Sunni Islamist political and militant organization that has governed Afghanistan since 2021, having previously ruled the country from 1996 to 2001 ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). It emerged in 1994 as a faction in the Afghan civil war, took Kandahar that November and seized the capital, Kabul, in September 1996, establishing a government that enforced a strict interpretation of Islamic law. Overthrown by a US-led invasion in 2001 after harbouring al-Qaeda, it fought a twenty-year insurgency and retook power on 15 August 2021 as US and international forces withdrew ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). The group remains designated by the [United States](/places/united-states/) as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist under Executive Order 13224, and Russia became the first country to formally recognise its government, on 3 July 2025 ([US Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/8126); [IISS](https://www.iiss.org/online-analysis/online-analysis/2025/08/will-russias-diplomatic-recognition-of-the-afghan-taliban-government-have-a-domino-effect/)).

## Origins and first rule, 1994–2001

The Taliban formed in 1994, comprised mostly of students and led by Mullah Mohammed Omar; "Taliban" is the Pashto plural of *talib*, meaning student, and most members are Pashtun, Afghanistan's largest ethnic group ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). The group took control of Kandahar in November 1994 and Kabul fell to it in September 1996 ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). From 1996 to 2001 it imposed strict Islamic laws: women had to wear head-to-toe coverings, were not allowed to attend school or work outside the home, and were forbidden to travel alone, while television, music, and non-Islamic holidays were banned ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). In 1997 the Taliban renamed the country the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan; only three countries officially recognised it — Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)).

The first rule ended after the 11 September 2001 attacks. The Taliban had forged a relationship with al-Qaeda leader Osama bin Laden, who moved his base of operations to Kandahar, and after the attacks US and allied forces began the invasion of Afghanistan on 7 October 2001 ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). The Taliban lost its last major stronghold as Kandahar fell in December 2001, and Hamid Karzai was chosen as interim leader ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). Mullah Omar led the movement from the mid-1990s until his death; the Afghan government announced in July 2015 that he had died in April 2013 in Pakistan ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)).

## Insurgency and the Doha agreement

Driven from power, the Taliban regrouped and mounted an insurgency against the US-backed Islamic Republic of Afghanistan and coalition forces that lasted two decades ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). Leadership passed to Mawlawi Haibatullah Akhundzada in May 2016 after Mullah Akhtar Mohammad Mansour was killed in a US air strike in Pakistan; Akhundzada, a senior religious cleric from the movement's founding generation, remains supreme leader ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). The group opened an official political office in Doha, Qatar, in June 2013, and after more than a year of negotiations the United States and the Taliban signed an agreement on 29 February 2020: the US committed to withdraw all international military forces and contractors by May 2021, in return for Taliban commitments to take unspecified action to prevent other groups, including al-Qaeda, from using Afghan soil to threaten the US and its allies ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). By 2020 the Taliban already controlled or contested about half the country, and US officials contended the group was not fulfilling its commitments even as US forces drew down to 2,500 in January 2021 ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)).

## Return to power in 2021

In 2021 President Joseph Biden announced the US would withdraw its troops, several months later than the date agreed in the US–Taliban accord, and the Taliban began a sweeping advance that captured wide swaths of rural Afghanistan ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). The advance was secured through both combat and negotiation: in many areas the Taliban secured the surrender or departure of government forces, sometimes with payments or through the mediation of local elders ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). The Taliban captured their first provincial capital on 6 August 2021, and within a week they were nearing Kabul, which they entered on 15 August 2021; President Ashraf Ghani fled the country the same day ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). In the last two weeks of August, US forces oversaw the evacuation of over 120,000 people from Kabul's airport before departing on 30 August 2021 ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)).

On 7 September 2021 the Taliban announced a caretaker government dominated by Taliban loyalists, again calling itself the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). Haibatullah Akhundzada holds supreme power as the group's emir, having led it since the 2016 killing of his predecessor in a US drone strike, and has made few public appearances ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). The takeover brought intersecting humanitarian and economic crises, driven by drought, the pandemic, Afghanistan's weak economic base, the cutoff of international development assistance, US sanctions on the Taliban, and the US hold on Afghan central bank assets ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)). In February 2022 President Biden signed an executive order covering USD 7 billion in frozen Afghan central bank assets; the Taliban claimed the funds, but the United States declined access ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)).

The renewed rule has been detrimental for the status of women and girls: on 23 March 2022 the Taliban prevented girls above the 6th grade from returning to school, on 20 December 2022 it suspended university education for all female students, and on 24 December 2022 it ordered NGOs to stop their female employees from coming to work ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). A [United Nations](/organizations/united-nations/) report released in June 2023 said the Taliban had committed "egregious systematic violations of women's rights" since retaking control ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)).

## Sanctions, recognition, and the compliance picture

The sanctions position is precise and often misstated, so it is worth stating exactly. The Taliban remains designated as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist (SDGT) under Executive Order 13224 — it is not a US-designated Foreign Terrorist Organization ([US Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/8126)). The Haqqani Network, by contrast, carries a dual designation: SDGT under E.O. 13224 and Foreign Terrorist Organization under section 219 of the Immigration and Nationality Act ([US Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/8126)). The US has not lifted sanctions on the Taliban and the Haqqani Network, and the designations do not prohibit US persons from exporting goods or services to Afghanistan, provided the transactions do not involve sanctioned individuals or entities ([US Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/8126)).

The [US Department of the Treasury](/organizations/us-department-of-the-treasury/)'s [Office of Foreign Assets Control](/organizations/office-of-foreign-assets-control/) has issued General Licenses 14 through 20 covering humanitarian assistance, agricultural commodities, medicine and medical devices, and personal remittances — and GL 20 authorises transactions involving Afghanistan's governing institutions — but none of them authorises transfers to the Taliban, the Haqqani Network, or any entity in which they own a 50 percent or greater interest ([US Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/8126)). That distinction — commerce with Afghanistan is permitted, transfers to the Taliban are not — is the core compliance fact for anyone dealing with the country.

On recognition, the United States has not recognised the Taliban as the government of Afghanistan ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). On 3 July 2025 Russia became the first country to formally recognise the Taliban regime as the legitimate government of Afghanistan, nearly four years after its return to power, after the Taliban's acting foreign minister received credentials from Russia's ambassador in Kabul ([IISS](https://www.iiss.org/online-analysis/online-analysis/2025/08/will-russias-diplomatic-recognition-of-the-afghan-taliban-government-have-a-domino-effect/)). Russia's Supreme Court had suspended its ban on the Taliban, which it had listed as a terrorist organisation for over 20 years, in April 2025 ([IISS](https://www.iiss.org/online-analysis/online-analysis/2025/08/will-russias-diplomatic-recognition-of-the-afghan-taliban-government-have-a-domino-effect/)). Western countries had worked to build an informal consensus that recognition would depend on conditions including the rights of women and girls, a more inclusive government, and mitigating terrorist threats from Afghan soil; Russia's move fractured that consensus, though IISS judged a global domino effect unlikely ([IISS](https://www.iiss.org/online-analysis/online-analysis/2025/08/will-russias-diplomatic-recognition-of-the-afghan-taliban-government-have-a-domino-effect/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the Taliban?**
The Taliban is a Sunni Islamist political and militant organization that has governed Afghanistan since 2021, after previously ruling from 1996 to 2001. It formed in 1994 under Mullah Mohammed Omar and retook power in August 2021 as US and international forces withdrew ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)).

**Is the Taliban a designated terrorist organization in the United States?**
The Taliban is designated a Specially Designated Global Terrorist under Executive Order 13224, but it is not a US Foreign Terrorist Organization. The allied Haqqani Network holds both designations ([US Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/8126)).

**Who leads the Taliban?**
Mawlawi Haibatullah Akhundzada has been supreme leader since 2016. He holds supreme power as the group's emir and has made few public appearances ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R45122/R45122.49.pdf)).

**Has any country recognised the Taliban government?**
Russia became the first country to formally recognise the Taliban government, on 3 July 2025. The United States has not recognised it ([IISS](https://www.iiss.org/online-analysis/online-analysis/2025/08/will-russias-diplomatic-recognition-of-the-afghan-taliban-government-have-a-domino-effect/); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/world/taliban-fast-facts?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)).

**Do US sanctions block humanitarian aid to Afghanistan?**
No. OFAC General Licenses 14–20 authorise humanitarian assistance, food and medicine exports, and personal remittances, but they do not authorise transfers to the Taliban, the Haqqani Network, or entities they majority-own ([US Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/8126)).

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not call the Taliban a Foreign Terrorist Organization under US law — that designation applies to the Haqqani Network, not the Taliban itself.
- It does not give a current force-strength figure; CNN notes the exact number of Taliban forces is unknown.
- It does not describe the internal workings of the Taliban government, its ministries, or its revenue sources — those were not verified from a fully-read source this pass.
- It does not predict whether other countries will follow Russia in recognising the government; IISS judged a global domino effect unlikely, and the profile reports that assessment rather than adopting it.
