## Overview

Every quick account of the House Select Committee on January 6 ends in the same place: the December 2022 criminal referrals against [Donald Trump](https://factsledger.com/people/donald-trump/), on four statutes, in what the [Associated Press](https://factsledger.com/organizations/associated-press/) called one of the most exhaustive congressional probes in memory.[^1] But the referrals are the headline, not the full story. The panel also voted to send four *sitting* Republican House members — including the party's leader — to the House Ethics Committee for defying its subpoenas. It debated a fifth charge, seditious conspiracy, and could not agree on it. And its final report recommended barring Trump from federal or state office under the 14th Amendment.[^1][^2]

Those three under-told facts are worth leading with, because the quick results that describe the committee as "the panel that referred Trump for prosecution" leave out what made its referrals unusual even by congressional standards: a panel referring members of its own chamber to ethics investigators, and failing — on the record — to reach consensus on the most serious charge it considered.[^1]

## How the panel got its shape

The Select Committee to Investigate the January 6th Attack on the United States Capitol was not Congress's first choice for investigating the attack. Senate Republicans blocked the formation of a bipartisan, independent commission; only after that effort failed did the Democratic-controlled House form an investigative committee of its own, in the summer of 2021.[^1]

House Republican leader Kevin McCarthy of California decided not to participate after House Speaker Nancy Pelosi rejected two of his appointments. That boycott opened the door to what the AP called "an unusually unified panel in the divided Congress": seven Democrats joined by two Republicans, Liz Cheney of Wyoming — the panel's vice chair — and Adam Kinzinger of Illinois.[^1] The committee launched in July 2021, with Bennie Thompson of Mississippi as chairman.[^1][^2]

## Eighteen months of investigation

Over its lifespan the committee conducted more than 1,000 interviews, held 10 public hearings, and collected more than a million documents.[^1] The Hindu's explainer, published after the report's release, described the haul as "millions of documents" gathered across an 18-month probe with 10 public hearings.[^2]

The panel aired some of its evidence for the first time at its final meeting, including a recent interview with longtime Trump aide [Hope Hicks](https://factsledger.com/people/hope-hicks/). Describing a conversation with Trump around that time, Hicks told the committee he had said that no one would care about his legacy if he lost the election — "the only thing that matters is winning."[^1]

## The final meeting: four statutes, and the fifth that failed

On December 19, 2022, at its final public meeting, the committee alleged violations of four criminal statutes by Trump — in both the run-up to the riot and during the insurrection itself — and recommended him for prosecution to the [Justice Department](https://factsledger.com/organizations/us-department-of-justice/): aiding an insurrection, obstructing an official proceeding, conspiracy to defraud the United States, and conspiracy to make a false statement.[^1]

The committee's summary tied each charge to the record it had built. On aiding an insurrection, it said Trump "was directly responsible for summoning what became a violent mob" and refused repeated entreaties from his aides to condemn the rioters or encourage them to leave. On obstruction, it cited his relentless badgering of Vice President Mike Pence and others to prevent certification of the election results on January 6. The conspiracy-to-defraud charge rested on his repeated lies about the election and his efforts to undo the result; the false-statement charge on the scheme to put forward slates of fake electors in battleground states won by Joe Biden.[^1]

What the panel did *not* recommend is nearly as telling. Among the charges contemplated but not approved was seditious conspiracy — the same allegation Justice Department prosecutors had used against a subset of rioters from far-right groups like the Oath Keepers and Proud Boys. "Something that the committee didn't come to agreement on," Thompson said after the hearing.[^1]

The committee also voted to refer conservative lawyer John Eastman, who devised the legal maneuvers aimed at keeping Trump in power, for prosecution on two of the same statutes: conspiracy to defraud the United States and obstructing an official proceeding.[^1] Thompson called the referrals a potential "roadmap to justice," and noted that while they are mostly symbolic — the Justice Department decides whether to prosecute — "if we are to survive as a nation of laws and democracy, this can never happen again."[^1]

## The referrals nobody leads with

The same Monday vote produced referrals that most summaries skip: the panel referred McCarthy and Representatives Jim Jordan of Ohio, Scott Perry of Pennsylvania, and Andy Biggs of Arizona to the House Ethics Committee for ignoring the panel's subpoenas. All four had been in touch with Trump or the White House in the weeks leading up to the attack, and McCarthy had acknowledged he spoke with Trump on January 6 itself.[^1]

A criminal referral from a congressional committee has no real legal standing, the AP noted, but the Ethics Committee referrals were a forceful statement of a different kind — a panel recommending scrutiny of four members of its own chamber for non-compliance.[^1] The committee members said the final vote on the report itself was 9-0.[^1]

## The report: 814 pages, 17 findings, one recommendation about office-holding

The 154-page executive summary went public as the final meeting ended; the full 814-page report, in eight chapters, followed on Thursday night, December 22, 2022.[^1][^2] Its central conclusion, as The Hindu summarized it: "The central cause of January 6th was one man, former President Donald Trump… None of the events of January 6th would have happened without him."[^2] The report enlisted 17 key findings, beginning with the charge that Trump, from election night onward, "purposely disseminated false allegations of fraud" despite having no evidence — claims that "provoked his supporters to violence on January 6."[^2]

In the chapters on the day of the violence, the report described a December 2020 tweet in which Trump "summoned tens of thousands of supporters to Washington for January 6th" and a segment titled "187 minutes of dereliction" detailing how he refused for hours to tell his supporters to stop, watching the riot on television until finally persuaded to record a video statement asking them to go home.[^2] The panel's framing of what had changed: "If we lacked the imagination to suppose that a President would incite an attack on his own Government, urging his supporters to 'fight like hell,' we lack that insight no more." The report's closing counsel: "The best defense against that danger will not come from law enforcement, but from an informed and active citizenry."[^3]

The report's most consequential recommendation was not criminal at all. It recommended that, using the 14th Amendment, Trump be barred from ever holding federal or state office again.[^2] The referrals themselves, The Hindu noted, were "eventually symbolic" — they do not bind the Justice Department, and the panel has "no constitutional path" to bar a candidacy on its own.[^2]

## Afterlife

The panel dissolved on January 3, 2023, with the arrival of the new Republican-led House.[^1] Its work continued to surface in criminal proceedings: lawmakers had openly aimed their findings at an "audience of one," Attorney General Merrick Garland, who had appointed special counsel Jack Smith to oversee the insurrection and classified-documents probes.[^1]

When the House Republican majority took over, the committee's website and its trove of materials disappeared from the House's servers — but Chairman Thompson posted the Select Committee's materials, including witness-testimony transcripts, on his own website, according to the Erie County Democrats' account of the release.[^4]

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not recite all 17 of the report's findings verbatim — their exact enumeration appears only in snippet-level sources, and the profile summarizes the report's structure and conclusions instead. It does not assert that the committee site's removal was a partisan act; the eriecountydems account of Thompson re-posting the materials is attributed as that outlet's account. It does not predict or describe any DOJ action on the referrals, which the AP itself described as mostly symbolic. And it does not take a position on the disputed 2020-election claims at the center of the report — the findings are presented as the committee's, which is what the fully read sources support.
