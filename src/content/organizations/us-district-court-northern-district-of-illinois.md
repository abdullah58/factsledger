## Overview

The United States District Court for the Northern District of Illinois — cited as N.D. Ill. — is the federal trial court for the northern counties of Illinois, seated principally in [Chicago](/places/chicago/) ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/history/courts/us-district-courts-districts-illinois-judicial-district-organization-1855-present)). Congress created it on February 13, 1855, by splitting the single District of Illinois in two, and its decisions are appealed to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/sites/default/files/trials/chicago7.pdf)). Its docket has carried some of the most famous federal trials in American history, from Al Capone in 1931 to the Chicago Seven in 1969.

The court covers the Chicago metropolitan area and the northern tier of the state. Under current law, Illinois is divided into three judicial districts — Northern, Central and Southern — and the Northern District itself is split into two divisions ([28 U.S.C. § 93](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2000&num=0&req=granuleid:USC-2000-title28-section93)). The Eastern Division, sitting in Chicago and Wheaton, covers Cook, Du Page, Grundy, Kane, Kendall, Lake, La Salle and Will counties; the Western Division, sitting in Freeport and Rockford, covers Boone, Carroll, De Kalb, Jo Daviess, Lee, McHenry, Ogle, Stephenson, Whiteside and Winnebago counties ([28 U.S.C. § 93](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2000&num=0&req=granuleid:USC-2000-title28-section93)). As a court of the [United States](/places/united-states/) federal judiciary, its docket spans civil and criminal cases alike.

## From one Illinois district to three

Illinois began as a single federal judicial district. Congress created it on March 3, 1819, and its first judge, Nathaniel Pope, served from 1819 to 1850, followed by Thomas Drummond from 1850 to 1855 ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/5921)). The Act of February 13, 1855 (10 Stat. 606) then divided the state into Northern and Southern districts, and Drummond's judgeship transferred to the new Northern District, where he served until 1869 ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/5921)).

The map kept being redrawn as the state grew. On March 3, 1905, Congress carved the Eastern District of Illinois out of counties taken from both the Northern and Southern districts, shrinking the Northern District to 18 northern counties and authorizing its second judgeship ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/history/courts/us-district-courts-districts-illinois-judicial-district-organization-1855-present)). The bench then grew in step with the Chicago region: further seats were added in 1922, 1931, 1938, 1940, 1950, 1961, 1966, 1970, 1978 and 1984, including five seats in a single 1984 act ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/5921)). Kenesaw M. Landis took the 1905 second seat and served until 1922 ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/5921)). On October 7, 1978, Congress reorganized Illinois into its present three districts — Central, Northern and Southern — abolishing the Eastern District as a separate district ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/history/courts/us-district-courts-districts-illinois-judicial-district-organization-1855-present)).

## How the court is organized

The court runs on a random-assignment system that is older than most of its judges' careers. It has used random assignment for more than 50 years, aiming at "an equitable distribution of cases, both in quantity and kind, among the judges" and at making sure no one can manipulate the system to choose a judge in advance ([U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois](https://www.ilnd.uscourts.gov/_assets/_documents/_forms/_clerksoffice/rules/pdf-orders/General%20Order%2014-0017%20-%20Local%20Rule%2040.1.pdf)). Cases are grouped into categories of roughly equal work and assigned by computer from a deck kept for each category; Eastern Division case numbers start with 1 each year, Western Division numbers with 50,001 ([U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois](https://www.ilnd.uscourts.gov/_assets/_documents/_forms/_clerksoffice/rules/pdf-orders/General%20Order%2014-0017%20-%20Local%20Rule%2040.1.pdf)).

As of the court's 2014 General Order, 22 district judgeships and 10 magistrate judgeships were authorized, with one of each assigned to the Western Division and the rest to the Eastern Division ([U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois](https://www.ilnd.uscourts.gov/_assets/_documents/_forms/_clerksoffice/rules/pdf-orders/General%20Order%2014-0017%20-%20Local%20Rule%2040.1.pdf)). A later seat complicates that count: a 1990 statute authorized an additional judge, filled by Jeffrey Cummings in 2023 after Judge Robert M. Dow Jr. became counselor to the Chief Justice in 2022 ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/5921)). The chief judge is Virginia M. Kendall, on the court since 2006 and chief judge since 2024, previously an Assistant U.S. Attorney in the district from 1995 to 2005 ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/1392326)).

The court's home is the Everett McKinley Dirksen U.S. Courthouse in Chicago. Its predecessor, designed by Henry Ives Cobb and completed in 1905, housed the Northern District until its demolition in April 1965 and saw "some of the nation's most sensational trials, including the trials of Al Capone and James Hoffa" ([U.S. Bankruptcy Court, Northern District of Illinois](https://www.ilnb.uscourts.gov/brief-history-everett-mckinley-dirksen-us-courthouse)). The replacement, designed by Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, was completed in 1964 and rededicated in 1970 in memory of Senator Everett McKinley Dirksen, who represented Illinois in the U.S. Senate from 1950 to 1969 ([U.S. Bankruptcy Court, Northern District of Illinois](https://www.ilnb.uscourts.gov/brief-history-everett-mckinley-dirksen-us-courthouse)).

## The trials that defined the court

**Al Capone, 1931.** The federal government could reach Capone only through Prohibition violations and income-tax evasion: he kept no bank account, owned no property in his own name and paid cash for everything, so the Internal Revenue Service built its case from his spending ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/law/law-magazines/al-capone-trial-1931)). The grand jury returned three indictments — failure to pay 1924 taxes, 22 counts covering 1925–1929, and a Volstead Act conspiracy indictment that was never pursued — and Capone first pleaded guilty under a negotiated 2½-year deal, then changed his plea to not guilty after Judge James H. Wilkerson told him, "It is time for somebody to impress upon the defendant that it is utterly impossible to bargain with a Federal court" ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/law/law-magazines/al-capone-trial-1931)). Tried October 6–24, 1931, he was found not guilty on the 1924 counts but guilty on five counts, and sentenced to 11 years in prison and a $50,000 fine — then the strongest penalties ever imposed on a tax evader ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/law/law-magazines/al-capone-trial-1931)).

**The Chicago Seven, 1969–1970.** The trial grew out of the protests at the 1968 Democratic National Convention, where about 10,000 demonstrators came to Chicago and police and protesters clashed violently, notably in Grant Park on August 28 ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/sites/default/files/trials/chicago7.pdf)). On September 9, 1968, Chief Judge William J. Campbell convened a grand jury, and on March 20, 1969, it indicted eight demonstrators and eight policemen; the demonstrators were charged with conspiring to use interstate commerce with intent to incite a riot ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/sites/default/files/trials/chicago7.pdf)). Judge Julius Hoffman was randomly selected to preside after Campbell recused himself, and Hoffman declared a mistrial for Bobby Seale and severed his case — leaving the seven defendants who gave the trial its name ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/sites/default/files/trials/chicago7.pdf)). The nearly five-month trial ended on February 19, when the jury acquitted all seven of conspiracy and acquitted John Froines and Lee Weiner on all charges, while convicting the other five of intent to incite a riot; Hoffman also convicted the defendants and their two lawyers of contempt, but the Seventh Circuit reversed most of the contempt convictions and reversed the five riot convictions, remanding them for retrial at the government's discretion ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/sites/default/files/trials/chicago7.pdf)).

**Rod Blagojevich, 2010–2011.** The court's modern public-corruption docket peaked with the prosecution of former Illinois Governor Rod Blagojevich, part of Operation Board Games, an investigation begun in 2003 that produced convictions against 15 defendants, announced by the [U.S. Department of Justice](/organizations/us-department-of-justice/) ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://WWW.JUSTICE.GOV/archive/usao/iln/chicago/2011/pr1207_01.pdf)). Blagojevich went to trial in the summer of 2010 and was convicted of lying to FBI agents, with the jury deadlocked on the remaining counts; retried in spring 2011, he was convicted on 17 further counts, including 10 counts of wire fraud and two counts of attempted extortion ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://WWW.JUSTICE.GOV/archive/usao/iln/chicago/2011/pr1207_01.pdf)). On December 7, 2011, Judge James Zagel sentenced him to 14 years in federal prison — the longest sentence ever imposed on a former governor in the Northern District of Illinois — plus a $20,000 fine and two years' supervised release ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://WWW.JUSTICE.GOV/archive/usao/iln/chicago/2011/pr1207_01.pdf)). The counts included his 2008 effort to trade a U.S. Senate appointment for $1.5 million in campaign contributions ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://WWW.JUSTICE.GOV/archive/usao/iln/chicago/2011/pr1207_01.pdf)).

**FTC v. Avant, 2019–2022.** The court's consumer-protection docket reached the FactsLedger profile of [Avant](/organizations/avant/) when the [Federal Trade Commission](/organizations/federal-trade-commission/) sued the Chicago online lender in this court, alleging it falsely advertised that it accepted credit- and debit-card payments while rejecting them, withdrew money from consumers' accounts without authorization — one consumer's monthly payment was debited 11 times in a single day — and refused refunds despite hundreds of complaints ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/04/online-lending-company-agrees-settle-ftc-charges-it-engaged-deceptive-unfair-loan-servicing)). The settlement order, filed in the Northern District of Illinois on April 15, 2019, imposed a $3.85 million judgment to be returned to harmed consumers and barred Avant from unauthorized payments and from collecting by remotely created check ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/04/online-lending-company-agrees-settle-ftc-charges-it-engaged-deceptive-unfair-loan-servicing)). In January 2022 the FTC mailed more than $3.7 million in refund checks to 17,367 consumers, averaging $216 each ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/01/ftc-returns-more-37-million-consumers-harmed-online-lender-avant)).

## FAQ

**When was the Northern District of Illinois created?** Congress created it on February 13, 1855, by splitting the single District of Illinois into Northern and Southern districts under 10 Stat. 606 ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/history/courts/us-district-courts-districts-illinois-judicial-district-organization-1855-present)).

**Which counties does the Northern District of Illinois cover?** The Eastern Division covers Cook, Du Page, Grundy, Kane, Kendall, Lake, La Salle and Will counties; the Western Division covers Boone, Carroll, De Kalb, Jo Daviess, Lee, McHenry, Ogle, Stephenson, Whiteside and Winnebago counties ([28 U.S.C. § 93](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2000&num=0&req=granuleid:USC-2000-title28-section93)).

**Where do appeals from the Northern District of Illinois go?** To the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, as the Chicago Seven appeal shows: the Seventh Circuit reversed the riot convictions and most of the contempt convictions from that trial ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/sites/default/files/trials/chicago7.pdf)).

**What happened in the FTC's Avant case in this court?** The FTC's settlement with Avant, LLC was filed in the Northern District of Illinois on April 15, 2019, imposing a $3.85 million judgment over deceptive and unfair loan servicing ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/04/online-lending-company-agrees-settle-ftc-charges-it-engaged-deceptive-unfair-loan-servicing)). In January 2022 the FTC mailed more than $3.7 million of that money back to 17,367 harmed consumers ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/01/ftc-returns-more-37-million-consumers-harmed-online-lender-avant)).
