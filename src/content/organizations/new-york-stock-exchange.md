## Overview

The New York Stock Exchange is the world's largest stock exchange by listed-company market capitalization — about $30.9 trillion across more than 2,000 listed companies in a March 2026 ranking, a figure that moves with markets ([IG Group](https://www.ig.com/uk/trading-strategies/what-are-the-largest-stock-exchanges-in-the-world--180905)). Founded on May 17, 1792, when 24 brokers signed the Buttonwood Agreement on lower Wall Street, it is an American stock exchange headquartered at 11 Wall Street in [New York City](/places/new-york-city/) and owned since November 2013 by Intercontinental Exchange, Inc. — which is itself listed on the NYSE under the symbol ICE ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/new-york-stock-exchange-inc); [ICE](https://ir.theice.com/press/news-details/2013/IntercontinentalExchange-Completes-Acquisition-of-NYSE-Euronext/default.aspx)). What structurally sets it apart from every rival: it still pairs electronic trading with human Designated Market Makers on a physical trading floor, a hybrid auction model its great competitor [Nasdaq](/organizations/nasdaq/) — the world's first fully electronic stock market, launched 1971 — never had ([IG Group](https://www.ig.com/uk/trading-strategies/what-are-the-largest-stock-exchanges-in-the-world--180905)).

## The hybrid auction market: how the exchange actually works

Most profiles treat the trading floor as nostalgia. It is better understood as market structure. The NYSE runs a hybrid model: electronic systems handle the bulk of order flow, but Designated Market Makers — human beings assigned to particular stocks — remain responsible for maintaining a fair and orderly market on the physical floor in New York ([IG Group](https://www.ig.com/uk/trading-strategies/what-are-the-largest-stock-exchanges-in-the-world--180905)). That human layer is the exchange's oldest differentiator: as early as 1993, NYSE leadership was defending the combination of computer automation with on-floor communication against fully electronic dealer markets, arguing that human intelligence had to meet competitiveness "at the point of sale" ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/new-york-stock-exchange-inc)). The contrast with Nasdaq is structural, not cosmetic — Nasdaq has no traditional floor and routes everything through competing electronic market makers, who may quote different prices for the same stock depending on inventory ([IG Group](https://www.ig.com/uk/trading-strategies/what-are-the-largest-stock-exchanges-in-the-world--180905); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/new-york-stock-exchange-inc)).

The listing base reflects the model: NYSE companies skew toward long-established multinationals across finance, healthcare, energy, and consumer goods — mature, often dividend-paying firms — and they are the companies tracked by the S&P 500 and the Dow Jones Industrial Average, the benchmarks for U.S. and global equity performance ([IG Group](https://www.ig.com/uk/trading-strategies/what-are-the-largest-stock-exchanges-in-the-world--180905)). The exchange itself is registered with the [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](/organizations/us-securities-and-exchange-commission/) as a national securities exchange, a status it has held since October 1, 1934 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/new-york-stock-exchange-inc)).

## From Buttonwood to ICE: two centuries in brief

The exchange took shape in the 1790s around the Tontine Coffee House at Wall and Water Streets, where merchants auctioned the government securities that had funded the Revolutionary War. On May 17, 1792, two dozen brokers signed the Buttonwood Agreement — pledging to avoid public auctions, collect minimum commissions on federal bonds, and give preference to each other in deals — and on March 8, 1817, they adopted a constitution under the name "New York Stock & Exchange Board" ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/new-york-stock-exchange-inc)). Railroads drove the 19th-century boom: the first railroad issue, the Mohawk & Hudson Railroad, listed in August 1830, and by the early 1850s more than ten rail companies traded on the Board. In 1863 the Board became the New York Stock Exchange; in 1868–69 it absorbed two competitors, made memberships salable (averaging $7,000–$8,000), grew from 533 to 1,060 members, and replaced the old "call" auction with continuous trading ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/new-york-stock-exchange-inc)).

The 20th century wrote the crises the exchange is remembered for: the September 1873 failure of Jay Cooke & Company closed its offices for ten days; October 24, 1929 ("Black Thursday") broke the market and October 29 crashed it — stocks down 11.7% on a record 16,410,000 shares, the Dow falling from 386 to 41; and October 19, 1987 ("Black Monday") dropped the Dow 508 points ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/new-york-stock-exchange-inc)). Regulation followed the 1929 crash: the NYSE registered with the SEC on October 1, 1934, elected its first full-time paid president (William McChesney Martin, Jr.) in 1938, incorporated as a not-for-profit in 1971, and abolished fixed commissions in May 1975 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/new-york-stock-exchange-inc)). In 1903 it had moved into the building it still occupies at 11 Wall Street, designed by George B. Post in classical-revival style, its facade carrying J.A. Ward's "Integrity Protecting the Works of Man" ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/new-york-stock-exchange-inc)).

## The Euronext era, the ICE takeover, and the 24-hour question

The modern corporate arc begins in 2007, when NYSE Group acquired Euronext for about $8.2 billion, forming NYSE Euronext ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/markets/intercontinentalexchange-plans-euronext-ipo-12721854)). What followed was a half-decade of blocked megamergers: in 2011 IntercontinentalExchange teamed with Nasdaq OMX on an $11 billion hostile bid for NYSE Euronext, stopped by U.S. regulators, and in 2012 NYSE Euronext's attempted combination with Deutsche Borse was vetoed by the European Commission ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/markets/intercontinentalexchange-plans-euronext-ipo-12721854)). ICE succeeded alone: on November 13, 2013, it completed the acquisition of NYSE Euronext in a stock-and-cash deal worth about $11 billion, creating an operator of 16 global exchanges and five central clearing houses, keeping both brand names, and preserving the NYSE name and the 11 Wall Street trading floor ([ICE](https://ir.theice.com/press/news-details/2013/IntercontinentalExchange-Completes-Acquisition-of-NYSE-Euronext/default.aspx)). In 2014 ICE spun Euronext back out through an IPO — shares listed in Paris, Amsterdam, and Brussels, then Lisbon — restoring its independence for the first time since 2007 ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/markets/intercontinentalexchange-plans-euronext-ipo-12721854)).

The next transformation is already filed. On April 10, 2026, the SEC approved Nasdaq's proposal to trade 23 hours a day, five days a week; NYSE Arca — the electronic exchange within the NYSE Group, not the main floor market — submitted its amended rule filing on May 12, 2026, and both venues are targeting a December 6, 2026 launch ([Harvard Law Forum](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/10/21/nyse-arca-and-nasdaq-to-launch-extended-trading-what-companies-should-consider/)). The plan adds an overnight session Sunday through Thursday, 9 p.m. to 4 a.m. ET, with a one-hour pause from 8 to 9 p.m. Monday through Thursday for maintenance and corporate actions; the traditional 9:30 a.m.–4 p.m. core session and its closing auction would continue to set the official prices ([Harvard Law Forum](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2026/10/21/nyse-arca-and-nasdaq-to-launch-extended-trading-what-companies-should-consider/)). A 233-year-old institution built on a handshake under a buttonwood tree is preparing, conditionally, to barely close.

## Frequently asked questions

**When was the New York Stock Exchange founded?**
On May 17, 1792, when 24 brokers signed the Buttonwood Agreement on lower Wall Street. The brokers adopted a constitution and the name "New York Stock & Exchange Board" on March 8, 1817, and the name became the New York Stock Exchange in 1863.

**Who owns the New York Stock Exchange?**
Intercontinental Exchange, Inc. (ICE), since November 13, 2013, when it completed the acquisition of NYSE Euronext for about $11 billion in stock and cash. ICE itself is listed on the NYSE under the symbol ICE.

**What is the difference between the NYSE and Nasdaq?**
The NYSE runs a hybrid model — electronic trading plus human Designated Market Makers on a physical floor in New York — and lists mainly established multinationals. Nasdaq, launched in 1971 as the world's first fully electronic stock market, has no trading floor and concentrates on technology, biotechnology, and high-growth companies.

**Is the NYSE really going to trade 24 hours a day?**
Nearly: Nasdaq and NYSE Arca (the NYSE Group's electronic venue) are targeting 23-hours-a-day, 5-days-a-week trading from December 6, 2026, adding an overnight session from 9 p.m. to 4 a.m. ET Sunday through Thursday. The launch is planned, not yet accomplished, and was approved conditionally.
