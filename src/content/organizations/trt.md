TRT — the Turkish Radio and Television Corporation (Turkish: Türkiye Radyo
Televizyon Kurumu) — is Turkey's national public broadcaster, founded by law
on 1 May 1964 and televising since 1968. Constitutionally described as an
impartial public enterprise, it operates roughly 18 TV channels and 17 radio
stations in 41 languages — and in March 2020 it registered with the U.S.
Justice Department as a foreign agent, following a 2019 finding that Ankara
controls its leadership, budget, and content
([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/nsd-fara/page/file/1282151/dl);
[C21Media](https://www.c21media.net/marketplace/trt/)).

## Overview

TRT was established on 1 May 1964 "to broadcast on radio and TV on behalf of
the state," though its radio lineage traces back to 6 May 1927 and its first
television transmission aired on 31 January 1968
([Anadolu Agency](https://en.haberler.com/turkish-public-global-broadcaster-trt-marks-57th-1594257/)).
The Turkish Constitution writes its autonomy and impartiality into law,
describing it as an impartial public economic enterprise
([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/nsd-fara/page/file/1282151/dl)).
Its footprint today spans about 18 TV channels and 17 radio stations in 41
languages — Turkish, English, Arabic, German, French, Azeri, and Kurdish among
them — covering general entertainment, music, sports, children's programming,
and news, with all channels governed by RTÜK, the Radio and Television Supreme
Council, under the European Convention on Transfrontier Television
([C21Media](https://www.c21media.net/marketplace/trt/)). It is a founding
member of the European Broadcasting Union (1972) and joined the Asia-Pacific
Broadcasting Union in 2004
([C21Media](https://www.c21media.net/marketplace/trt/)). Its then-chairman
Ibrahim Eren called it the "visual and auditory memory of Turkey" and "a
school of broadcasting in the nation"
([Anadolu Agency](https://en.haberler.com/turkish-public-global-broadcaster-trt-marks-57th-1594257/)).

## The broadcaster and its scale

TRT's scale is best understood through its channel architecture. The flagship
TRT 1 carries general entertainment and drama; specialist channels include TRT
Haber (news), TRT Spor (sports), TRT Çocuk (children), TRT Müzik (music),
TRT Belgesel (documentaries), TRT Türk and TRT Avaz (general and
Turkic-language programming), TRT Kurdî (Kurdish), TRT Arabi (Arabic), and TRT
World, the English-language international news channel — alongside TRT EBA TV,
which broadcasts school lessons
([C21Media](https://www.c21media.net/marketplace/trt/)). TRT World's profile
is distinct: launched in 2015 as Turkey's first English-language international
television and digital news service, it broadcasts from its main studio complex
in Istanbul with additional studios in London and Washington DC, and became
the first Turkish broadcaster to join the Association for International
Broadcasting in May 2017
([Association for International Broadcasting](http://aib.org.uk/trt-world-becomes-an-aib-member/)).

| Segment | What it covers |
|---|---|
| General & drama | TRT 1 (flagship), TRT 2 (arts and culture), TRT Türk, TRT Avaz |
| News | TRT Haber, TRT World (English), TRT Arabi (Arabic) |
| Sports | TRT Spor, TRT Spor Yıldız, TRT 3 |
| Children's | TRT Çocuk, TRT Diyanet Çocuk — TRT Kids is Turkey's dominant kids channel |
| Culture & education | TRT Müzik, TRT Belgesel, TRT EBA TV, TRT Kurdî |

That portfolio reaches far beyond Turkey's borders. TRT has exported more than
250 titles to over 100 countries in more than 45 languages, with dramas like
*The Great Seljuks* and *Barbaros: The Sultan's Order* drawing large audiences
in the Middle East, South America, the United Arab Emirates, and the Balkans
([C21Media](https://www.c21media.net/marketplace/trt/)). Its documentary
channel claims more than 800 hours of content across 60 countries and calls
itself the most-watched documentary channel in Turkey
([C21Media](https://www.c21media.net/marketplace/trt/)).

## The FARA determination: a public broadcaster labeled a foreign agent

The most documented episode in TRT's recent history comes not from a ratings
chart but from a U.S. federal filing cabinet. In a twelve-page letter dated 1
August 2019, the U.S. Department of Justice determined that TRT's U.S. branch
office was obligated to register under the Foreign Agents Registration Act —
as an agent of two foreign principals: the Government of Turkey and TRT
itself
([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/nsd-fara/page/file/1282151/dl)).
The DOJ's reasoning was specific: the Turkish government "exercises direction
and control of TRT by regulation and oversight, and by controlling its
leadership, budget, and content" — through RTÜK, whose members are elected by
parliament; through the Council of Ministers' control over the composition of
TRT's board; through a requirement that the Prime Minister approve TRT's
international broadcasting agreements; and through financial control, since
TRT's funds come from Turkish taxes and government grants in addition to
advertising
([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/nsd-fara/page/file/1282151/dl)).
The DOJ also noted that Turkish law requires TRT to comply with "the State's
national security politics" and found that TRT World content consistently
mirrors government positions — citing U.S.-produced reporting that adopted
Ankara's lines on Kurdish fighters in Syria and on Fethullah Gülen
([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/nsd-fara/page/file/1282151/dl)).

TRT resisted registration for over a year, then registered on 12 March 2020,
while maintaining in its filing that its activities are "like those performed
by other news and broadcast organizations that are not controlled by foreign
governments"
([Foundation for Defense of Democracies](https://www.fdd.org/analysis/2020/03/31/it-is-time-for-al-jazeera-to-register-under-fara/)).
The DOJ's finding sits alongside an academic one: an Oxford Internet Institute
study concluded that TRT World, launched with the slogan "where news inspires
change," had shifted from being Turkey's public-diplomacy tool into "the
AKP's voice to the world," and traced TRT's loss of autonomy to a 1972
amendment of the TRT Law, with the corporation coming under the Directorate
of Communications — direct presidential control — in 2018
([Oxford Internet Institute](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9527443/)).

## The money, and the documentary that reached the Oscars

TRT's funding has long been unusual among European public broadcasters. A
Court of Accounts report found that 1.2 billion liras flowed to TRT from the
electricity-bill levy in a single year; in November 2021, President Erdoğan
removed the fixed 2% TRT share (plus the 0.7% Energy Fund) from electricity
bills, producing an effective ~2.7% bill reduction and roughly 930 million
liras in household savings
([Hürriyet Daily News](https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/government-to-abolish-some-fees-from-electricity-bills-169245)).
A stat with an implication: that 1.2 billion liras means every Turkish
household was, in effect, a compulsory subscriber to the state broadcaster —
paying for TRT whether it watched a single minute or not.

TRT's programming has also shaped one of Turkey's most-watched modern stories.
During the Korean President's visit to Turkey in 2010, "the state-run Turkish
Radio and Television Corporation (TRT) produced a documentary about Turkish
soldiers in the Korean War," and Sgt. Süleyman Dilbirliği's story — the
Turkish sergeant who cared for the orphaned Korean girl he named Ayla — became
public, leading to their emotional reunion
([Hürriyet Daily News](https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/turkeys-oscar-nominee-ayla-brings-korean-war-memories-117633)).
That broadcast was the seed of [Ayla: The Daughter of War](/creative-works/ayla-the-daughter-of-war/)
(2017), directed by Can Ülkay and submitted as Turkey's foreign-language Oscar
nominee, with Turkish Airlines as main sponsor and the Culture and Tourism
Ministry as main supporter of its campaign
([Hürriyet Daily News](https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/turkeys-oscar-nominee-ayla-brings-korean-war-memories-117633)).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is TRT independent if its autonomy is written into the constitution?

On paper, yes: the constitution describes TRT as an impartial public economic
enterprise with mandated autonomy. In practice, the autonomy was curtailed by a
1972 amendment to the TRT Law, and since 2018 the corporation has been under
the Directorate of Communications — direct presidential control per the
European Commission's 2020 assessment. The U.S. DOJ's 2019 finding cites
parliament-elected RTÜK governance, board control, and budget control as
evidence of state direction.

### What exactly did the U.S. Justice Department decide about TRT?

It determined that TRT's Washington branch is an agent of the Government of
Turkey and of TRT under the Foreign Agents Registration Act, because it
engages in "political activities" and acts as a publicity and
information-service agent in the United States. TRT registered on 12 March
2020 while disputing the characterization, claiming its journalism is like
that of outlets not controlled by foreign governments.

### How is TRT funded now that the electricity-bill levy is gone?

The levy — a fixed 2% on every electricity bill that delivered 1.2 billion
liras to TRT in one year — was abolished in November 2021. TRT continues to
be funded by Turkish taxes and government grants in addition to advertising,
per the DOJ's description of its finances. A license tax on television and
radio receivers remains part of the funding mix.

### What is TRT World's relationship to TRT?

TRT World is TRT's English-language international news channel, launched in
2015 — Turkey's first English-language international news service. It is a
division of the TRT corporation (the DOJ letter notes it does not appear to
be a separate legal entity), broadcasting from Istanbul with studios in
London and Washington DC. Its stated mission is to show events from Turkey's
viewpoint to non-Turkish audiences.
