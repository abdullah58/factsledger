## Overview

Morgan Stanley is an American multinational investment bank and financial services firm headquartered in New York City. Founded in 1935, when the Glass-Steagall Act split J.P. Morgan's investment banking business from its commercial bank, it is now one of the world's largest wealth managers, overseeing $7.9 trillion in client assets at the end of 2024 ([The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/06/30/the-story-of-jp-morgan-the-most-powerful-bank-in-a.aspx); [Morgan Stanley Form 10-K](https://sp.morganstanley.com/download/prospectus/343346aa-6b5f-42f9-8f40-3143d12e5b9d)).

The firm runs three businesses: Institutional Securities (investment banking, trading, and advice for corporations and governments), Wealth Management (advisors, self-directed brokerage, and workplace stock plans for individuals), and Investment Management (funds and portfolios for institutions and individuals) ([Morgan Stanley Form 10-K](https://sp.morganstanley.com/download/prospectus/343346aa-6b5f-42f9-8f40-3143d12e5b9d)). In 2024 it reported net revenues of $61.8 billion and net income of $13.4 billion, according to its annual report filed with the [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](/organizations/us-securities-and-exchange-commission/) ([Morgan Stanley Form 10-K](https://sp.morganstanley.com/download/prospectus/343346aa-6b5f-42f9-8f40-3143d12e5b9d)), with wealth management — the business it spent fifteen years building through crisis-era deals — producing $28.4 billion of that revenue, nearly half the firm. About 80,000 people work for the firm across 42 countries, and its shares trade on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker MS ([Morgan Stanley Form 10-K](https://sp.morganstanley.com/download/prospectus/343346aa-6b5f-42f9-8f40-3143d12e5b9d)).

## Born from a law: the 1935 split and the Dean Witter merger

Morgan Stanley exists because a statute said it had to. The Glass-Steagall Act of 1933, passed after the 1929 crash, barred a single firm from combining deposit-taking with securities underwriting, and [J.P. Morgan & Co.](/organizations/jpmorgan-chase/) chose to stay a commercial bank ([The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/06/30/the-story-of-jp-morgan-the-most-powerful-bank-in-a.aspx)). Two of its partners — Henry Sturgis Morgan, a grandson of J.P. Morgan, and Harold Stanley — left to found an investment bank that took their names, opening at 2 Wall Street on September 16, 1935 ([The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/06/30/the-story-of-jp-morgan-the-most-powerful-bank-in-a.aspx)). The new firm was an instant force: in its first year it took about a quarter of the entire industry's underwriting business ([The Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/06/30/the-story-of-jp-morgan-the-most-powerful-bank-in-a.aspx)).

The modern firm is the product of a second, stranger lineage. Dean Witter & Co., founded as a San Francisco bond house in 1924, merged with Reynolds Securities in 1978, was bought by retailer Sears in 1981 for about $600 million, launched the Discover Card in 1986, and was spun off by Sears as Dean Witter, Discover & Co. in 1993 ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/11/Morgan-Stanley-Dean-Witter-Company.html); [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/economics/economics-magazines/morgan-stanley-dean-witter-co)). In February 1997 the two firms announced a merger valued at $10.22 billion, finalized on May 31, 1997, creating the largest securities firm in the United States by equity capital — an aristocratic investment bank fused to a Main Street brokerage with more than 450 U.S. offices ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/11/Morgan-Stanley-Dean-Witter-Company.html)). Dean Witter's chairman and CEO, Philip J. Purcell, led the merged firm, with Morgan Stanley's John J. Mack as president and chief operating officer, and the company shortened its name to Morgan Stanley Dean Witter & Co. in 1998 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/economics/economics-magazines/morgan-stanley-dean-witter-co)). The credit-card half of that inheritance did not last: in 2007 Morgan Stanley spun off Discover Financial Services, distributing one Discover share for every two Morgan Stanley shares on June 30, 2007 ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/895421/000119312507150955/d8k.htm)).

## Surviving 2008: conversion, MUFG, and the wealth-management pivot

The 2008 crisis nearly ended the firm — and remade it. On September 21, 2008, a week after Lehman Brothers failed, Morgan Stanley and [Goldman Sachs](/organizations/goldman-sachs/) announced their intent to become bank holding companies, accepting Federal Reserve supervision in exchange for access to central-bank funding ([The Motley Fool](http://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/09/21/the-end-of-investment-banks.aspx)). The move was a confession of fragility: Morgan Stanley's leverage ratio was about 30-to-1, matching Lehman's pre-bankruptcy level, and in the months that followed it drew on as much as $107 billion in federal funds ([The Motley Fool](http://www.fool.com/investing/general/2013/09/21/the-end-of-investment-banks.aspx)). It also took $10 billion under the Treasury's Capital Purchase Program in late October 2008 and returned the entire amount on June 9, 2009 ([Taxpayers for Common Sense](https://www.taxpayer.net/budget-appropriations-tax/bailout-bank-bio-morgan-stanley/)).

Private capital arrived alongside the public backstop. In October 2008, Mitsubishi UFJ Financial Group closed a $9 billion equity investment in Morgan Stanley, taking a 21 percent ownership interest on a fully diluted basis — $7.8 billion of convertible preferred stock paying a 10 percent dividend, plus $1.2 billion of non-convertible preferred — along with a board seat and a global strategic alliance that lifted Morgan Stanley's Tier 1 capital ratio above 15.5 percent ([Morgan Stanley](https://www.morganstanley.com/press-releases/mitsubishi-ufj-financial-group-closes-9-billion-equity-investment-in-morgan-stanley-as-part-of-global-strategic-alliance_7025/)). Then, in January 2009, Morgan Stanley moved to buy the stability it had just survived without: it announced a joint venture combining its Global Wealth Management Group with Citi's Smith Barney brokerage, taking a 51 percent stake for $2.7 billion in cash in a business with more than 20,000 financial advisors, $1.7 trillion in client assets, and 6.8 million client households ([Morgan Stanley](https://www.morganstanley.com/press-releases/morgan-stanley-and-citi-to-form-industry-leading-wealth-management-business-through-joint-venture_af3e409a-e1b7-11dd-84e6-b390c77322d3/)). Then-CEO John Mack called it "an important step forward in our effort to build our wealth management franchise," and the deal set the firm's direction for the next decade and a half: durable, fee-based wealth revenue instead of trading bets ([Morgan Stanley](https://www.morganstanley.com/press-releases/morgan-stanley-and-citi-to-form-industry-leading-wealth-management-business-through-joint-venture_af3e409a-e1b7-11dd-84e6-b390c77322d3/)).

## Buying the future: E*TRADE, Eaton Vance, and the Chime IPO

Morgan Stanley finished the pivot with two acquisitions in thirteen months. In February 2020 it announced the purchase of online brokerage E*TRADE in an all-stock deal valued at about $13 billion, the largest acquisition by a U.S. bank since the 2008 crisis, bringing in more than 5 million customer accounts and $360 billion in assets ([FIA](https://www.fia.org/marketvoice/articles/2020-ma-roundup-deals-shaping-derivatives-markets)). The deal closed on October 2, 2020, and at closing the combined Wealth Management franchise oversaw $3.3 trillion in assets ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/morgan-stanley-closes-acquisition-of-etrade-financial-quick-facts-2020-10-02)). Five months later it completed the roughly $7 billion acquisition of investment manager Eaton Vance, which held more than $500 billion in assets under management, taking the firm's combined client assets to $5.4 trillion across Wealth Management and Investment Management ([Private Banker International](https://www.privatebankerinternational.com/news/morgan-stanley-completes-eaton-vance-acquisition/)).

The table shows the result: by 2024, wealth management produced nearly as much revenue as the institutional securities business that had defined the firm for most of its history, and total client assets had reached $7.9 trillion ([Morgan Stanley Form 10-K](https://sp.morganstanley.com/download/prospectus/343346aa-6b5f-42f9-8f40-3143d12e5b9d)). Leadership changed with the model. Edward (Ted) Pick, who joined Morgan Stanley in 1990 and ran its Institutional Securities Group, became Chief Executive Officer on January 1, 2024, succeeding James Gorman after his 14 years as CEO; Gorman became Executive Chairman ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/895421/000095010323015457/dp201919_ex9901.htm)).

The investment-banking franchise still shows up where it matters most to this site: [Reuters](/organizations/reuters/) reported that on June 12, 2025, [Chime](/organizations/chime/) debuted on the [Nasdaq](/organizations/nasdaq/) after pricing 32 million shares at $27 and raising $864 million, with Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, and J.P. Morgan as the lead underwriters ([Reuters](https://www.tradingview.com/news/reuters.com,2025:newsml_L4N3SF0KU:0-chime-set-for-long-awaited-market-debut-after-864-million-us-ipo/)). The offering valued the digital bank at $11.6 billion, down from its $25 billion private valuation in 2021, and its major backers included [DST Global](/organizations/dst-global/) and [General Atlantic](/organizations/general-atlantic/), which had helped fund Chime's $2.65 billion in private raises ([Reuters](https://www.tradingview.com/news/reuters.com,2025:newsml_L4N3SF0KU:0-chime-set-for-long-awaited-market-debut-after-864-million-us-ipo/)).
