## Overview

Bloomberg LP is one of the largest privately held companies in the world — and one whose future is, for the first time, a genuinely open question. In September 2023, at 81, Michael Bloomberg said at The New York Times Climate Summit that his Bloomberg Philanthropies foundation would inherit the company when he dies and, "because of the tax laws," would have to "sell it someplace or other over the first five years" ([The Business Times](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/companies-markets/telcos-media-tech/michael-bloomberg-outlines-succession-plan-media-empire)). Three years later, the company was reported to have begun informal discussions with investment bankers about a potential IPO or other strategic transaction — preliminary conversations, no decisions made — while its 84-year-old founder quietly moved some personal shares into the foundation; the official line stayed that "Mike controls the firm, is the majority shareholder, and has no plans to sell the company" ([Citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/880704/bloomberg-explores-future-ownership-options-as-succession-planning-takes-shape/)). Behind that succession drama sits a machine of unusual stability: a financial-data business founded in 1981 that still runs on the same subscription model it invented — a terminal on (and in) more than 300,000 desks, each paying over $30,000 a year ([Citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/880704/bloomberg-explores-future-ownership-options-as-succession-planning-takes-shape/); [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3051883/the-bloomberg-terminal)).

## The succession arc: from "sell it in five years" to banker talks

Bloomberg's 2023 remarks were unusually specific for a founder who normally keeps succession off the agenda. "At 81 years old, common sense says I should have succession plans," he said, before spelling out the plan: "I give virtually all of the company's profits to the foundation, and the foundation gave away last year US$1.7 billion" — and "when I die, the foundation inherits the company," at which point tax law would force a sale "someplace or other over the first five years" ([The Business Times](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/companies-markets/telcos-media-tech/michael-bloomberg-outlines-succession-plan-media-empire)). He was careful to add that he had no plans to sell "in the immediate future" and hoped to keep working for at least three to five more years ([The Business Times](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/companies-markets/telcos-media-tech/michael-bloomberg-outlines-succession-plan-media-empire)). The numbers attached to the statement were large even by his standards: an 88% personal stake in a company that had generated more than $12 billion in revenue in 2022 ([The Business Times](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/companies-markets/telcos-media-tech/michael-bloomberg-outlines-succession-plan-media-empire)).

The 2023 announcement followed a management overhaul a month earlier. In an August internal memo, Bloomberg named product head Vlad Kliatchko chief executive and former Bank of England governor Mark Carney chair of a newly formed board of directors, with JP Zammitt becoming president and Patti Roskill chief financial officer; existing board members would move to emeritus status ([Reuters via Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/world-news/bloomberg-lp-names-product-head-vlad-kliatchko-as-new-ceo-report-123082100928_1.html)). Bloomberg anticipated the question everyone would ask and answered it in the memo: "I'm sure these changes raise questions about me, so let me put them to rest: I'm not going anywhere" — noting he had never used a company title, just "Mike" ([Reuters via Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/amp/world-news/bloomberg-lp-names-product-head-vlad-kliatchko-as-new-ceo-report-123082100928_1.html)). The board has since been made more independent; by 2026 it reportedly included Netflix co-founder [Reed Hastings](https://factsledger.com/people/reed-hastings/) and veteran banker Bob Steel alongside Carney ([Citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/880704/bloomberg-explores-future-ownership-options-as-succession-planning-takes-shape/)).

Then came the escalation no one had seen before. In 2026, Semafor reported that Bloomberg LP had begun informal discussions with investment bankers about a potential initial public offering or other strategic transaction — "the clearest indication yet," per the reporting, that long-term succession planning was underway ([Citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/880704/bloomberg-explores-future-ownership-options-as-succession-planning-takes-shape/)). The same report said Bloomberg had recently transferred some of his personal Bloomberg LP shares to Bloomberg Philanthropies, a move described as potentially reducing tax liabilities if the company went public or sold an ownership stake ([Citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/880704/bloomberg-explores-future-ownership-options-as-succession-planning-takes-shape/)). Bankers cited by Semafor estimated the company could be worth more than $80 billion, and the report noted that a minority investment could attract exchanges, asset managers and AI companies seeking access to Bloomberg's proprietary financial data ([Citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/880704/bloomberg-explores-future-ownership-options-as-succession-planning-takes-shape/)). For a company that denied a 2020 report of stake-sale talks with Bill Ackman's SPAC — "The company is not for sale," it said at the time — even preliminary banker conversations mark a shift in posture. This profile treats the banker talks as reported, not confirmed: the conversations were explicitly described as preliminary, and the company's own statement denies any current plan to sell.

## What the company actually is

The foundation of Bloomberg LP is one of finance's great founding stories, and it begins with a firing. Michael Bloomberg had been a general partner at investment bank Salomon Brothers, where he had been shunted into heading IT development and then "pushed out of the company altogether." In 1981, at 39, he took his $10 million severance and started a company called Innovative Market Systems — later Bloomberg LP — to build a computerized system delivering real-time market data and financial analytics to Wall Street firms ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3051883/the-bloomberg-terminal)). Its product was first called Market Master; the first 20 units went into service at Merrill Lynch at the end of 1982 ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3051883/the-bloomberg-terminal)). Merrill Lynch, the first customer, owned 30% of the young company and benefited from an exclusivity agreement that kept Bloomberg from selling to Merrill's major rivals; in the late 1980s Bloomberg lobbied successfully to end the restriction, and the company began growing 25% to 30% a year ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3051883/the-bloomberg-terminal)).

The product — officially Bloomberg Professional, but known to everyone as the Bloomberg Terminal — is still the engine. By 2015 it served more than 325,000 subscribers and processed 60 billion pieces of market data a day ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3051883/the-bloomberg-terminal)); by 2026 the subscriber count was reported at more than 300,000, each paying over $30,000 a year, with [Forbes](https://factsledger.com/organizations/forbes/) estimating the company's annual revenue at roughly $15 billion ([Citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/880704/bloomberg-explores-future-ownership-options-as-succession-planning-takes-shape/)). That subscription base is the asset everyone wants: banks, asset managers, exchanges, and AI companies all see value in the proprietary financial data it represents.

Around the data business, Bloomberg built one of the world's largest media operations. The company operates a global newsroom of roughly 2,700 journalists, with Bloomberg News (launched in 1990) feeding the terminal, Bloomberg Television, Bloomberg Radio, Bloomberg Businessweek, Bloomberg Markets and Bloomberg.com ([Citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/880704/bloomberg-explores-future-ownership-options-as-succession-planning-takes-shape/)). The portfolio also includes industry products — Bloomberg Law, Bloomberg Tax, Bloomberg Government and Bloomberg New Energy Finance among them — that extend the data-and-news model into professional services. In 2026 the company also made what it called its most significant push yet into private markets: a definitive agreement to acquire Canoe Intelligence, an AI-powered platform that automates private-markets data collection and delivery ([PRNewswire via WCIA](https://www.wcia.com/business/press-releases/cision/20260729NY14003/bloomberg-to-acquire-canoe-intelligence-taking-a-defining-step-in-its-mission-to-transform-private-markets-investing/)).

## The Canoe bet and the private-markets turn

The Canoe deal, announced July 29, 2026, shows where Bloomberg is pointing its acquisition firepower. Canoe processes over 1.5 million documents a month across more than 44,000 funds, delivering structured portfolio data to over 500 institutional clients representing more than $11 trillion in assets under service ([PRNewswire via WCIA](https://www.wcia.com/business/press-releases/cision/20260729NY14003/bloomberg-to-acquire-canoe-intelligence-taking-a-defining-step-in-its-mission-to-transform-private-markets-investing/)). Bloomberg said the acquisition continues a multi-year strategy of extending its public-markets intelligence into private markets, where it already tracks more than three million private companies, 50,000 private funds and 16,000 private direct loans ([PRNewswire via WCIA](https://www.wcia.com/business/press-releases/cision/20260729NY14003/bloomberg-to-acquire-canoe-intelligence-taking-a-defining-step-in-its-mission-to-transform-private-markets-investing/)). The two companies had already integrated earlier in 2026, connecting Canoe's fund data directly into Bloomberg PORT Enterprise for cross-asset portfolio analysis ([PRNewswire via WCIA](https://www.wcia.com/business/press-releases/cision/20260729NY14003/bloomberg-to-acquire-canoe-intelligence-taking-a-defining-step-in-its-mission-to-transform-private-markets-investing/)). Kliatchko framed it as a second act: he said that four decades ago Bloomberg had brought transparency and efficiency to public markets, and that private markets were now primed for a similar transformation ([PRNewswire via WCIA](https://www.wcia.com/business/press-releases/cision/20260729NY14003/bloomberg-to-acquire-canoe-intelligence-taking-a-defining-step-in-its-mission-to-transform-private-markets-investing/)). Financial terms were not disclosed. Canoe had been incubated by 10East and 22C Capital and was backed by institutional investors including Blackstone Innovations Investments, Carlyle AlpInvest, Goldman Sachs Alternatives and Nasdaq Ventures ([PRNewswire via WCIA](https://www.wcia.com/business/press-releases/cision/20260729NY14003/bloomberg-to-acquire-canoe-intelligence-taking-a-defining-step-in-its-mission-to-transform-private-markets-investing/)).

## The profits-to-philanthropy pipeline

What makes Bloomberg unusual among private giants is where the money goes. Bloomberg has said he gives "virtually all of the company's profits" to Bloomberg Philanthropies, which gave away $1.7 billion in 2022 ([The Business Times](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/companies-markets/telcos-media-tech/michael-bloomberg-outlines-succession-plan-media-empire)). The headline gift of 2024: $1 billion to his alma mater, Johns Hopkins University, announced in July — enough to make tuition free for most medical students, with students from households earning less than $300,000 paying no tuition and those below $175,000 also getting fees and living expenses covered, starting that fall ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2024-07-09/bloomberg-gives-1-billion-to-hopkins-to-make-tuition-free-for-most-medical-students)). Nearly two-thirds of current and incoming medical students were expected to be eligible, at a school where graduates had averaged $105,000 in debt the prior year ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2024-07-09/bloomberg-gives-1-billion-to-hopkins-to-make-tuition-free-for-most-medical-students)). The money also funds graduate aid across public health, nursing, education, engineering, business, government and policy, the arts and sciences, international studies and the Peabody Institute ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2024-07-09/bloomberg-gives-1-billion-to-hopkins-to-make-tuition-free-for-most-medical-students)). It was a continuation, not a debut: Bloomberg had already given Hopkins $1.8 billion in 2018 for undergraduate financial aid and partnered with the school in 2021 on the $150 million Vivien Thomas Scholars Initiative ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2024-07-09/bloomberg-gives-1-billion-to-hopkins-to-make-tuition-free-for-most-medical-students)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim Bloomberg LP is going public or will be sold — the banker discussions were explicitly reported as preliminary, and the company denies any such plan. It does not assert the company's valuation or revenue, which are private; the $80-billion-plus estimate and the ~$15-billion revenue figure are attributed to bankers and Forbes, not stated as facts. It does not name any specific honoree on a Bloomberg "40 Under 40" list; that editorial-queue note could not be verified to a fully-read source. And it treats Bloomberg's stated succession plan — the foundation inherits the company, then sells within five years — as what he said in 2023, not as a legal certainty, since the 2026 reporting suggests the picture is still being worked out.
