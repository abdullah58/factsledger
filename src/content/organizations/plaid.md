## Overview

Plaid is the San Francisco fintech that became the connective tissue of consumer finance — the "plumbing," as it is often described, that lets apps like Venmo, Robinhood, Acorns, and Betterment link directly to users' bank accounts ([Fortune](https://www.thewealthadvisor.com/article/plaid-visa-and-best-fintech-deal-never-happened?page=9)). It was founded in 2013 by Zach Perret and William Hockey, two former Bain & Company associates who abandoned their own consumer finance app when they realized the underlying API technology for bank-account connectivity was the more valuable business ([Fortune](https://www.thewealthadvisor.com/article/plaid-visa-and-best-fintech-deal-never-happened?page=9)). Plaid is fundamentally a business-to-business company with virtually no consumer-facing presence; it makes money by charging its fintech customers for every API call linking a consumer's bank data to their services ([Fortune](https://www.thewealthadvisor.com/article/plaid-visa-and-best-fintech-deal-never-happened?page=9)).

## The deal that never happened

On January 13, 2020, [Visa](/organizations/visa/) announced plans to acquire Plaid for $5.3 billion ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/atr/division-operations/division-update-spring-2021/protecting-nascent-competition-visa-and-plaid-abandon-anticompetitive-merger)). The Department of Justice sued to block the merger that November in the Northern District of California, arguing under the Clayton and Sherman Acts that Plaid — with connections to some 200 million U.S. consumer bank accounts — planned to launch a lower-cost online debit product that would compete directly with Visa ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/atr/division-operations/division-update-spring-2021/protecting-nascent-competition-visa-and-plaid-abandon-anticompetitive-merger)). The complaint alleged Visa, which it called a monopolist in online debit, sought to buy Plaid as an "insurance policy" to neutralize a "threat to our important US debit business" — language drawn from Visa's own internal documents, including one executive's sketch of Plaid as an island volcano whose visible tip concealed "a massive opportunity — one that threatens Visa" ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/atr/division-operations/division-update-spring-2021/protecting-nascent-competition-visa-and-plaid-abandon-anticompetitive-merger)).

Antitrust lawyers read the case as a landmark "killer acquisition" challenge — the rare merger suit aimed not at existing competition but at a nascent rival a dominant firm allegedly buys to smother ([Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2021/01/01_14-visa-abandons-acquisition)). Visa maintained the lawsuit was flawed and the capabilities complementary rather than competitive, but on January 12, 2021 — a day shy of the deal's first anniversary — the companies terminated the agreement rather than face protracted litigation ([DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/atr/division-operations/division-update-spring-2021/protecting-nascent-competition-visa-and-plaid-abandon-anticompetitive-merger)).

## Worth more alone

The DOJ's intervention turned out to be the best thing that could have happened to Plaid's valuation. In April 2021, three months after the deal collapsed, Plaid raised $425 million in a Series D that valued it at roughly $13.4 billion — more than 2.5 times what Visa had offered ([Fortune](https://www.thewealthadvisor.com/article/plaid-visa-and-best-fintech-deal-never-happened?page=9)). Perret later said Plaid "wouldn't have entered into the deal" had it known the antitrust scrutiny awaiting it, crediting the pandemic's acceleration of digital finance with making Plaid "a fundamentally different company" than the one Visa had bid on ([Fortune](https://www.thewealthadvisor.com/article/plaid-visa-and-best-fintech-deal-never-happened?page=9)). The company's rise had been hard-won: Hockey described 2012–2016 as a "constant existential crisis" in which the startup "almost died like 10 times," before a 2018 round at a $2.65 billion valuation — led by Kleiner Perkins with Andreessen Horowitz joining, alongside existing investors Visa and [Mastercard](/organizations/mastercard/) — made it a unicorn ([Fortune](https://www.thewealthadvisor.com/article/plaid-visa-and-best-fintech-deal-never-happened?page=9)).
