## Overview

WeTransfer is the Amsterdam file-transfer service that turned sending a large file into sharing a link: no account, no software, free for files up to 2GB ([VentureBeat](http://venturebeat.com/2015/02/17/wetransfer-nabs-25m-to-help-bring-easy-file-transfers-to-the-global-masses/)). Founded in 2009 and bootstrapped for years, it came within days of a €629–716 million Amsterdam listing in 2022, but was instead sold to Milan-based [Bending Spoons](/organizations/bending-spoons/) in July 2024, which then cut about 75% of its workforce ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/09/08/bending-spoons-plans-to-lay-off-75-of-wetransfer-staff-after-acquisition/); [Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/wetransfer-cancels-amsterdam-ipo/)).

## A tool built for people who hated tools

WeTransfer launched in beta in December 2009, built by Bas Beerens and Nalden — the blogger whose real name is Ronald Hans ([MarketingWeek](https://www.marketingweek.com/digital-professional-nalden-co-founder-wetransfer/)). The origin was deliberately unglamorous: Beerens had built a basic version inside his own communications agency to send large files and save on courier costs, while Nalden was building a tool so his parents could send him files and photos, because they did not understand the alternatives on offer. When the two compared notes, they decided to build something together. The design brief never changed: a service so simple that, in the company's own phrase, "your mum could use it" — no registration, no login screen, just a file sent and received ([MarketingWeek](https://www.marketingweek.com/digital-professional-nalden-co-founder-wetransfer/)).

That simplicity was also the business model. Instead of banner ads, WeTransfer showed full-screen "wallpaper" images behind the transfer screen — work by artists, brands and event organisers — an idea Nalden had developed on his blog, Nalden.net ([Tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2015/03/04/the-story-behind-wetransfer-profile)). Nalden said half the advertising inventory was given free to support the creative community, and the company reinforced the creative positioning with an arts scholarship run with Central Saint Martins and a video series, The Creative Class ([MarketingWeek](https://www.marketingweek.com/digital-professional-nalden-co-founder-wetransfer/); [Tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2015/03/04/the-story-behind-wetransfer-profile)). Alongside advertising sat the freemium ladder: free transfers up to 2GB, downloadable for seven days, and a paid Plus tier — 5GB uploads in the early years, later $120 a year for 10GB transfers to multiple recipients plus 50GB of storage ([VentureBeat](http://venturebeat.com/2015/02/17/wetransfer-nabs-25m-to-help-bring-easy-file-transfers-to-the-global-masses/)). Under the hood, the early scaling pain — two years spent keeping servers online as users flooded in — ended with a move to Amazon Web Services ([MarketingWeek](https://www.marketingweek.com/digital-professional-nalden-co-founder-wetransfer/)).

The company was bootstrapped until 2015, an unusual path beside heavily funded rivals such as Dropbox and Box, and it claimed to have been profitable since 2013 ([Tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2015/03/04/the-story-behind-wetransfer-profile)). Its first external funding, a $25 million Series A from Highland Capital Partners Europe, arrived in February 2015, when it reported 25 million monthly active users sending about 70 million transfers a month, with Highland partners Tony Zappalà and Irena Goldenberg joining the board ([VentureBeat](http://venturebeat.com/2015/02/17/wetransfer-nabs-25m-to-help-bring-easy-file-transfers-to-the-global-masses/); [Tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2015/03/04/the-story-behind-wetransfer-profile)). Co-founder Nalden's explanation for taking money at all was revealing: the company did not need it, but valued the advice and network — "why raise money when you can earn it yourself and have total control over your business?" ([Tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2015/03/04/the-story-behind-wetransfer-profile)).

## The IPO that never happened — and the sale that did

By early 2022 WeTransfer looked ready to graduate. Its parent company, WeRock N.V., announced plans on 12 January 2022 to list on Euronext Amsterdam, publishing a prospectus that valued the company at €629–716 million, with shares priced at €17.50–20.50 and about €125 million in fresh capital sought ([Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/wetransfer-cancels-amsterdam-ipo/)). The prospectus showed revenue of €72 million and adjusted EBITDA of €21.3 million for the first nine months of 2021. Then, days before trading was due to begin, the company pulled the listing, citing volatile market conditions while insisting investor demand had been substantial. CEO Gordon Willoughby thanked users, partners, staff and shareholders and said the company would keep pursuing its strategy and growth trajectory ([Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/wetransfer-cancels-amsterdam-ipo/)).

Two and a half years later, the exit came privately instead. In July 2024, Milan-based Bending Spoons acquired WeTransfer for an undisclosed amount ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/09/08/bending-spoons-plans-to-lay-off-75-of-wetransfer-staff-after-acquisition/)). Bending Spoons, which had raised $155 million in February 2024 at a $2.55 billion valuation, was on an acquisition run — and its playbook was already visible from earlier deals: it had cut 129 staff at Evernote in February 2023, let go of Filmic's entire workforce in December 2023, and cut staff at Meetup in 2024 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/09/08/bending-spoons-plans-to-lay-off-75-of-wetransfer-staff-after-acquisition/)). In September 2024 it confirmed the same treatment for WeTransfer: a plan to cut about 75% of staff, with those affected to be informed after local layoff regulations in each country were worked through. Dutch media put the headcount at over 350; Eurofound, the EU agency tracking restructurings, records 260 jobs lost — around 75% of 350 — announced on 8 September 2024 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/09/08/bending-spoons-plans-to-lay-off-75-of-wetransfer-staff-after-acquisition/); [Eurofound](https://apps.eurofound.europa.eu/restructuring-events/detail/201724)). CEO Luca Ferrari framed it as focus, not distress: the vision was "a smaller, more sharply focused WeTransfer organization," and Eurofound's factsheet notes there had been no stress situation within the company before the announcement ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/09/08/bending-spoons-plans-to-lay-off-75-of-wetransfer-staff-after-acquisition/); [Eurofound](https://apps.eurofound.europa.eu/restructuring-events/detail/201724)). TechCrunch later confirmed the company also tightened its free plan under the new owner, introducing stricter limits ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/05/what-is-bending-spoons-everything-to-know-about-aols-acquirer/)).

## The 2025 AI-terms row

If the layoffs damaged WeTransfer inside the industry, the next controversy damaged it with its own users. In mid-2025 an update to its terms of service granted the company rights over user content including using it "to improve performance of machine learning models that enhance our content moderation process" ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2025/07/16/wetransfer-changes-policy-after-concern-it-could-train-ai-on-users-photos/)). Photographers and other creatives read the clause as permission to use, sell or share their files with AI companies, and the backlash spread rapidly on social media. WeTransfer clarified repeatedly, telling the BBC: "We don't use machine learning or any form of AI to process content shared via WeTransfer, nor do we sell content or data to any third parties." It said the clause had only ever contemplated AI for internal content moderation, revised the terms to a simpler royalty-free licence "for the purposes of operating, developing, and improving the Service," and removed the machine-learning mention — with the revised terms taking effect for existing users on 8 August 2025 ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2025/07/16/wetransfer-changes-policy-after-concern-it-could-train-ai-on-users-photos/)). The episode became a case study in how quickly trust can evaporate for a brand whose entire pitch was simplicity and goodwill. By December 2025, TechCrunch reported, co-founder Nalden himself was publicly criticising Bending Spoons' decisions and building another file-transfer service ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/05/what-is-bending-spoons-everything-to-know-about-aols-acquirer/)).
