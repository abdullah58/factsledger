## Overview

Kroll Bond Rating Agency (KBRA) is the first credit rating agency founded after the 2008 financial crisis — a challenger built by Jules Kroll, the corporate investigator who made his name hunting hidden billions for governments, to break the grip of the three agencies whose AAA stamps had helped inflate the mortgage bubble ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). Established in 2010 and registered with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission as a nationally recognized statistical rating organization (NRSRO) in all five rating categories, KBRA is, by its own account to Congress, one of the five largest rating agencies globally and the largest founded after the crisis ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA16/20210721/113951/HHRG-117-BA16-Wstate-NadlerJ-20210721.pdf); [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/statistics-data-visualizations/nationally-recognized-statistical-rating-organizations-nrsros)). Its story is the story of how hard it is to compete with an oligopoly — and how a newcomer did it anyway, one commercial mortgage deal at a time.

The market KBRA entered was, and remains, extraordinarily concentrated. The three large NRSROs — S&P, Moody's, and Fitch — issued about 95% of all outstanding ratings and took 91% of NRSRO revenue, according to the Congressional Research Service's 2024 figures ([Congressional Research Service](https://d27r7ebsgu0es2.cloudfront.net/reports/IF11916.html)). As of December 31, 2025, only ten agencies were registered as NRSROs at all, and only five — DBRS, Fitch, KBRA, Moody's, and S&P — were registered in all five rating categories, from financial institutions and insurers to corporate issuers, asset-backed securities, and government securities ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/statistics-data-visualizations/nationally-recognized-statistical-rating-organizations-nrsros)).

## A detective enters the ratings business

Jules Kroll sensed opportunity in the wreckage. In the wake of the 2008 financial collapse, the reputations of the Big Three credit-rating companies "lay in tatters": Standard & Poor's, Moody's, and Fitch had all blessed mortgage-backed securities with their highest ratings despite the often shaky subprime loans underneath ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). Kroll, who had made his mark as a financial-crimes private investigator with offices in more than 15 countries, reckoned he could come up with a better way ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). He had pocketed more than $100 million when he sold his corporate detective firm, Kroll Associates, to Marsh & McLennan for $1.9 billion in 2004 ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)).

Kroll teamed up with Jerome Fons, who had quit his job as managing director of credit policy at Moody's in August 2007, just as the financial system — and Moody's reputation — was teetering. A year later Fons testified to Congress that "a large part of the blame can be placed on the inherent conflicts of interest found in the issuer-pays model" ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). The two men's first idea was to fix that conflict directly: rather than have issuers pay for a rating, they would charge the bond buyers. The strategy flopped before it began — investors made it clear they would not pay for a service they had long received for free, and the service was never launched ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)).

Instead, KBRA bought its way in. In 2010 Kroll purchased a tiny company called Lace Financial and picked up its NRSRO credential — the only practical route into a club whose incumbents had held their designations since 1975 ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). The Big Three accounted for 94.5% of the industry's $5.4 billion in revenue in 2013, according to the SEC, and KBRA ranked only fifth in ratings issued, behind Toronto-based DBRS ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). The economics of the incumbency were formidable: Moody's, the only free-standing public company among the Big Three, had a pretax profit margin of 43.8% the prior year — higher than Google or Apple — while KBRA, privately held, discloses no financial results ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)).

## The CMBS beachhead

KBRA's first rating, in July 2011, was for a commercial mortgage-backed security — and the timing was fortunate. Later that month, S&P's CMBS business took a huge hit: investors complained that S&P's AAA rating on part of an offering was inflated, and S&P stunned the market by abruptly pulling its ratings on a $1.5 billion offering by Goldman Sachs and Citigroup, saying it had found flaws in its methodology ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). Morgan Stanley told clients that S&P's action had "severely eroded investor and issuer confidence in its ratings" ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). S&P's loss was KBRA's gain: in 2011 S&P ranked third in CMBS ratings and KBRA a distant sixth; by 2014 S&P had fallen to fifth while KBRA had risen to No. 2, rating 65 CMBS deals that raised $53.8 billion from investors that year, with Moody's still No. 1 ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)).

KBRA also competed on price and on markets the incumbents ignored. In March 2012, Connecticut became the first U.S. state to hire KBRA to grade its general obligation bonds, at an annual introductory rate for three years of just $50,000 — 91% less than Moody's price of $542,525 for fiscal 2014 — with State Treasurer Denise L. Nappier saying KBRA was added as a fourth rating company "in the interest of fostering competition" ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). KBRA began rating community banks in 2013, after studying post-crisis bank defaults and finding that community banks had performed better than their much larger counterparts, due in large part to management strength; it has since rated more than 200 community and regional banks, and the incumbent agencies followed suit and began rating community banks as well ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA16/20210721/113951/HHRG-117-BA16-Wstate-NadlerJ-20210721.pdf)). A quarter of KBRA's ratings were for securities not rated by any other firm ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)).

The Chicago episode of 2015 showed both KBRA's independence and the risk of it. On March 17, 2015, KBRA won the city of Chicago as a client for its general obligation bonds, assigning A with a stable outlook; less than two months later Moody's slashed Chicago's rating two notches to junk (Ba1) after the Illinois Supreme Court rejected a pension-overhaul plan, and Fitch and S&P cut within three days, each with a negative outlook — while only KBRA left its rating untouched, at A and stable, as of July 2 ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). KBRA declined to comment, and the episode remains genuinely unresolved in the record: as NYU professor Lawrence White put it, "Kroll will say, 'We're better.' That may be so, or they may be giving in to make the issuer happy. We won't know until five years from now" ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)).

## The competition argument

By July 2021, when KBRA president and CEO Jim Nadler testified to the House Financial Services Committee, the agency had grown to more than 400 employees in offices in the [United States](/places/united-states/), Europe, and the UK, and had issued more than 44,500 ratings representing $2.2 trillion in rated issuances ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA16/20210721/113951/HHRG-117-BA16-Wstate-NadlerJ-20210721.pdf)). Nadler's testimony was, in effect, a complaint about entrenchment: the three largest NRSROs still commanded over 95% market share, down from over 98% before the crisis, and the barriers were structural — many institutional investors, including public and private pensions, still require the use of one or more of the largest NRSROs by name in guidelines written 40 or more years ago, before other rating agencies existed ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA16/20210721/113951/HHRG-117-BA16-Wstate-NadlerJ-20210721.pdf)).

KBRA's position was that the Dodd-Frank reforms had largely worked, but that competition itself remained the unfinished business ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA16/20210721/113951/HHRG-117-BA16-Wstate-NadlerJ-20210721.pdf)). The testimony also flagged a quieter barrier: SEC rules require new NRSRO applicants to produce written statements from investors who have used the applicant's ratings for at least three years, a requirement KBRA argued "essentially blocks new entrants" ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA16/20210721/113951/HHRG-117-BA16-Wstate-NadlerJ-20210721.pdf)).

## KBRA and the new fintech securitizations

KBRA's ratings now reach into the newest corners of consumer finance. In February 2026, [Avant](/organizations/avant/) closed a $200 million personal-loan securitization — its 23rd since founding and its seventh revolving transaction — that became the first of its deals to earn AAA ratings from both [Fitch Ratings](/organizations/fitch-ratings/) and Kroll Bond Rating Agency ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/loans/2026/fintech-avant-boosts-financing-capacity-with-new-200-million-dollar-deal/)). The 24-month revolving structure is expected to provide more than $500 million in financing capacity, bringing Avant's long-term funding commitments to about $2.5 billion ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/loans/2026/fintech-avant-boosts-financing-capacity-with-new-200-million-dollar-deal/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Kroll Bond Rating Agency?**
KBRA is a global, full-service credit rating agency established in 2010 by Jules Kroll and registered with the SEC as an NRSRO in all five rating categories ([U.S. House of Representatives](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/BA/BA16/20210721/113951/HHRG-117-BA16-Wstate-NadlerJ-20210721.pdf); [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/statistics-data-visualizations/nationally-recognized-statistical-rating-organizations-nrsros)). It rates corporate, financial-institution, insurance, structured-finance, and government debt.

**Who founded KBRA?**
Jules Kroll, the corporate investigator who founded Kroll Inc. in 1972 and sold Kroll Associates to Marsh & McLennan for $1.9 billion in 2004 ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)). He launched KBRA in 2010 with Jerome Fons, formerly Moody's managing director of credit policy ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)).

**How is KBRA different from the Big Three?**
KBRA was founded after the 2008 crisis specifically to challenge the Big Three (S&P, Moody's, Fitch), which together issue about 95% of outstanding ratings ([Congressional Research Service](https://d27r7ebsgu0es2.cloudfront.net/reports/IF11916.html)). It built its position in CMBS, community banks, and other niches the incumbents under-served ([Bloomberg Markets](https://bondcasebriefs.com/2015/07/14/finance-and-accounting/taking-on-the-ratings-triopoly/?print=pdf)).

**Is KBRA an NRSRO?**
Yes. KBRA is registered with the SEC as a nationally recognized statistical rating organization in all five rating categories — financial institutions, insurance companies, corporate issuers, asset-backed securities, and government securities ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/statistics-data-visualizations/nationally-recognized-statistical-rating-organizations-nrsros)).

**What did KBRA rate for Avant?**
In February 2026, KBRA — alongside Fitch Ratings — gave its first AAA rating to an Avant securitization, a $200 million personal-loan deal ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/loans/2026/fintech-avant-boosts-financing-capacity-with-new-200-million-dollar-deal/)).

- This profile does not state a current headcount, rating count, or revenue figure for KBRA: the scale figures are KBRA-stated in July 2021 testimony and are dated as such. KBRA is privately held and does not disclose financial results.
- It does not present the "one of the five largest rating agencies globally" or "largest rating agency established after the 2008 crisis" claims as independently verified; they are KBRA's own claims, made in congressional testimony.
- It does not endorse KBRA's 2015 Chicago A/stable rating as correct: the Big Three disagreed sharply, KBRA declined to comment, and the record is left open as Bloomberg reported it.
- The founding year follows 2010 (the Lace Financial acquisition and launch); some sources date the formation to 2009, and the variant is flagged.
- Market-share figures are the Congressional Research Service's 2024 figures; older sources give different shares, and the range is flagged.
