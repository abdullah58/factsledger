## Overview

The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) is an independent, non-governmental organization that develops voluntary international standards through the national standards bodies of 177 countries ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/structure.html)). Founded in Geneva on 23 February 1947, it has published more than 22,000 standards covering almost every field of technology, management and manufacturing, from screw threads to quality management ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)). Its best-known products are management-system standards such as ISO 9001 for quality and ISO 14001 for the environment — and its name is not an acronym at all, but comes from the Greek word *isos*, meaning "equal" ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/about)).

ISO does not make laws and does not certify companies. Its standards are voluntary agreements, written by consensus among experts nominated by its member bodies, and they work because buyers, regulators and manufacturers around the world choose to rely on them ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/about); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/International-Organization-for-Standardization)). That voluntary model has one hard edge, and it was tested in public in 2002, when ISO considered withdrawing the JPEG image standard altogether rather than let a patent holder charge royalties on it ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/news/jpeg-no-more-iso-mulls-withdrawal-of-standard/)).

## From London 1946 to Geneva 1947

International standardization began before ISO did. Its earliest predecessor, the International Federation of the National Standardizing Associations (ISA), was founded in 1926 and focused mainly on mechanical engineering, but it suspended its activities during the Second World War ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)). In 1944 the [United Nations](/organizations/united-nations/) Standards Coordinating Committee (UNSCC) was introduced to support international cooperation during postwar reconstruction ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)).

In October 1946, delegates from 25 countries gathered in [London](/places/london/) to discuss a new approach to international standardization, and that meeting led to the formal establishment of the International Organization for Standardization on 23 February 1947 ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)). The new body was designed to unite national standardization bodies on equal terms, so that all countries had equal rights and responsibilities in developing standards, and its structure and procedures were greatly influenced by those of the former ISA ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)).

ISO started work with 67 technical committees, each dedicated to a field such as screw threads, marine technology, food, textiles, paints and laboratory equipment ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)). Its secretariat was established in Geneva, Switzerland, where it remains headquartered, and the Central Secretariat began with just five staff members in the early 1950s ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)). Its first standard appeared in 1951 — ISO/R 1:1951, the standard reference temperature for industrial length measurements — at a time when standards were still called "recommendations" ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)).

## How ISO is run: members, Council and the Secretariat

ISO is a network of 177 national standards bodies, with only one member per country, each representing ISO in its own country ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/members.html)). Membership comes in three categories with different levels of access and influence: full member bodies set the course for standards development, vote in technical and policy meetings, and sell and adopt ISO standards nationally; correspondent members attend meetings as observers and sell and adopt standards nationally; subscriber members keep up to date with ISO's work but cannot participate in it and do not sell or adopt standards ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/members.html)).

| Category | Voting rights | Can sell and adopt ISO standards nationally |
| -------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Member bodies (full members) | Yes — participate and vote in technical and policy meetings | Yes |
| Correspondent members | No — attend as observers | Yes |
| Subscriber members | No — cannot participate | No |

Source: [ISO](https://www.iso.org/members.html).

The members meet once a year at the General Assembly, which is the overarching organ and ultimate authority of the organization and decides its strategic objectives ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/structure.html)). Between assemblies, the ISO Council is the core governance body: it meets three times a year and is made up of 21 member bodies, the ISO Officers and the chairs of the policy development committees CASCO (conformity assessment), COPOLCO (consumer issues) and DEVCO (developing countries) ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/structure.html)). The Technical Management Board, which reports to the Council, manages the technical work and the technical committees that lead standards development ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/structure.html)). Day-to-day operations are coordinated by the Central Secretariat in Geneva, overseen by the Secretary-General ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/structure.html)).

In Western industrial countries the member body is usually a private organization, such as the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) in the United States and the British Standards Institution (BSI) in the United Kingdom, while in most other countries it is a governmental organization ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/International-Organization-for-Standardization)). Other large member bodies include Germany's DIN, France's AFNOR, Japan's JISC, China's SAC and India's BIS ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/members.html)).

ISO's independence is financial as well as organizational. Its national members pay subscriptions that meet the Central Secretariat's operational costs, each subscription in proportion to the country's gross national income and trade figures, and another source of revenue is the sale of standards ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/structure.html)). But the Central Secretariat's operations represent only about one fifth of the cost of the whole system — the rest is borne by member bodies and businesses that allow their experts to participate and pay their travel expenses ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/structure.html)). In other words, the standards are mostly written by volunteers whose employers foot the bill.

## The standards the world actually uses

ISO's catalogue spans almost every aspect of technology, management and manufacturing, but two families dominate its public reputation. The ISO 9000 series of quality management standards, published in 1987, quickly became some of the most recognized and widely used standards in the world ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)). ISO 9001, first published in 1987, is the world's most widely adopted quality management system standard, and it has been revised in 1994, 2000, 2008, 2015 and 2026, each edition shifting the emphasis — from documented procedures, to process-based management, to risk-based thinking and leadership accountability, and now to quality culture, ethics, risk, knowledge and climate-related considerations ([NSF](https://www.nsf.org/knowledge-library/iso-90012026-published-on-september-16-2026-what-quality-leaders-need-to-know)). The 2026 edition was published on 16 September 2026, with a three-year transition period for organizations certified to the 2015 edition ([NSF](https://www.nsf.org/knowledge-library/iso-90012026-published-on-september-16-2026-what-quality-leaders-need-to-know)).

| Standard | First published | What it covers |
| -------- | --------------- | -------------- |
| ISO/R 1 (now ISO 1) | 1951 | Standard reference temperature for industrial length measurements |
| ISO 9001 | 1987 | Quality management systems — the world's most widely adopted QMS standard |
| ISO 14001 | 1996 | Environmental management systems |
| ISO 45001 | — | Occupational health and safety management (integrates with ISO 9001:2026) |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | — | Information security management (integrates with ISO 9001:2026) |

Sources: [ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso); [NSF](https://www.nsf.org/knowledge-library/iso-90012026-published-on-september-16-2026-what-quality-leaders-need-to-know).

ISO 14001, the environmental management standard, followed in 1996, giving organizations tools to measure and reduce their environmental impact ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)). ISO launched its first website in 1995 to make standards more accessible globally ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)). One boundary is worth stating plainly: electrical and electronic engineering is not ISO's field at all — that is the responsibility of the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/International-Organization-for-Standardization)). Because technology keeps moving, ISO standards are optimally reviewed for possible revision every five years ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/International-Organization-for-Standardization)).

## The JPEG test: when ISO nearly withdrew a standard

ISO's most revealing episode is one its own history pages do not mention. In July 2002, [Asure Software](/organizations/asure-software/) — then known as Forgent Networks — began enforcing a patent it had acquired, demanding royalties from manufacturers using JPEG compression, and ISO considered withdrawing the JPEG standard altogether ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/news/jpeg-no-more-iso-mulls-withdrawal-of-standard/)). The move was described at the time as unprecedented, and it followed directly from ISO's own rules: its standards must be implementable free, or under "reasonable and non-discriminatory" (RAND) terms ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/news/jpeg-no-more-iso-mulls-withdrawal-of-standard/)).

Forgent was not offering RAND terms. It required payment from manufacturers using JPEG compression, charged no uniform fee, and made each company negotiate directly without knowing what its competitors had agreed — an arrangement the contemporary reporting said was "certainly not 'reasonable and non-discriminatory' licensing" ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/news/jpeg-no-more-iso-mulls-withdrawal-of-standard/)). Rather than accept that, the JPEG committee sought proof of prior art against Forgent's patent claim, treating the dispute as a test of whether a standard could survive a royalty grab ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/news/jpeg-no-more-iso-mulls-withdrawal-of-standard/)).

The episode also showed the limits of ISO's leverage. Even if the standard had been withdrawn, JPEG was already so widely used that it would have remained a "de facto standard" in practice, and manufacturers would still have had to choose between paying Forgent or fighting the claim in court ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/news/jpeg-no-more-iso-mulls-withdrawal-of-standard/)). ISO never did withdraw JPEG — but the threat itself is the clearest public demonstration of what ISO's royalty-free rule means: an ISO standard that cannot be implemented without paying a patent holder is, in ISO's own terms, not an ISO standard at all.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is ISO?**
ISO is the International Organization for Standardization, an independent, non-governmental organization whose 177 national member bodies develop voluntary international standards by consensus ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/structure.html)). It was founded in Geneva on 23 February 1947 and has published more than 22,000 standards covering technology, management and manufacturing ([ISO Help Centre](https://help.iso.org/en/articles/376239-history-of-iso)).

**What does "ISO" stand for?**
Nothing, strictly speaking: ISO is not an acronym but a name inspired by the Greek word *isos*, meaning "equal", chosen because the organization's full name — and its initials — would differ across languages ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/about)). The short name is the same in every country and every language.

**Does ISO certify companies?**
No. ISO develops standards; it does not audit organizations or issue certificates — that is done by independent certification bodies. The confusion is common because certificates say "ISO 9001 certified", but the certificate comes from a certification body, not from ISO itself ([NSF](https://www.nsf.org/knowledge-library/iso-90012026-published-on-september-16-2026-what-quality-leaders-need-to-know)).

**Who are ISO's members?**
National standards bodies — one per country — such as ANSI in the United States and BSI in the United Kingdom ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/International-Organization-for-Standardization)). Individuals and companies cannot become ISO members, though they can contribute to standardization work through their national member body ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/members.html)).

**Why did ISO threaten to withdraw the JPEG standard in 2002?**
Because Forgent Networks (now Asure Software) began demanding patent royalties on JPEG compression, and ISO rules require its standards to be implementable free or on reasonable and non-discriminatory terms — which negotiated, non-uniform royalty demands were not ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/news/jpeg-no-more-iso-mulls-withdrawal-of-standard/)).
