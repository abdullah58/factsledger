## Overview

Tax Notes is the tax news and commentary portfolio of [Tax Analysts](/organizations/tax-analysts/), a nonprofit, nonpartisan publisher based in Falls Church, Virginia. Its three weekly magazines — Tax Notes Federal (1972), Tax Notes International (1989) and Tax Notes State (1991) — plus the daily Tax Notes Today online services cover federal, state and international taxation through news, analysis and commentary aimed at the practitioners, officials and academics who write, interpret and enforce tax law ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

Its stated mission is "to shed light on tax policy and administration through aggressive, unbiased reporting and informed commentary from the leaders in the field" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). That mission has a practical edge: Tax Notes does not only report on tax administration, it has repeatedly sued the government to force tax documents into the open, and the documents won in those cases became part of what it publishes. Its readers, by the publisher's own count, number more than 150,000 tax professionals in law and accounting firms, corporations, government agencies and academic institutions ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). It is a trade publication with an unusually public-interest origin: it began as a one-man newsletter written in a suburban backyard.

## A newsletter written in a backyard

Tax Notes began with Thomas F. Field, a 38-year-old lawyer in the [U.S. Department of the Treasury](/organizations/us-department-of-the-treasury/)'s Office of Tax Legislative Counsel, who believed tax legislation was dominated by special interests urging narrow measures "designed to confer tax benefits on the few at the expense of the many" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)). On Bastille Day in 1970 he founded two organizations: Tax Analysts and Advocates, a charitable and educational group modelled on a public-interest law firm, and Taxation With Representation, a taxpayers' lobby ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)).

Field was the new venture's only employee, working unpaid from his basement, and his wife Marcia Field — herself a Treasury economist — was its first benefactor ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)). *Tax Notes* first appeared in 1972 as a simple news summary, initially distributed free to members of the media. Field wrote the first issue himself, typewritten, "in the backyard of my Arlington home on a sunny Saturday morning," and in the early years it was printed on color-coded paper — yellow pages for news, blue for [Internal Revenue Service](/organizations/internal-revenue-service/) materials — prompting jokes about "yellow journalism" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)).

The publication became the organization's lifeline. When seed funding ran out and Field had cut almost all staff, he began charging for *Tax Notes*; "in relative short order" several hundred subscriptions were sold, enough to keep the operation going ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)). Selling full-text documents alongside the newsletter added a second revenue stream, and the documents dovetailed with the disclosure litigation: as Tax Analysts fought for the public release of agencies' working law, it made the resulting documents available to practitioners ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)). By 1980 the lobbying arm had been absorbed and Tax Analysts had emerged in roughly its current form ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)).

## The three weekly magazines — and the daily services

The portfolio grew in three steps, each adding a jurisdiction. *Tax Notes Federal*, the flagship, has appeared weekly since 1972 with news, commentary and analysis on federal taxation; *Tax Notes International* followed in 1989 with a weekly focus on international taxation; and *Tax Notes State* rounded out the set in 1991 with state and local tax coverage ([American Association of Law Libraries](https://ir.law.utk.edu/aall_websites/26/)). Each magazine covers court opinions, legislative action and revenue rulings as well as policy debate, with commentary from outside practitioners, scholars and government officials alongside staff reporting ([American Association of Law Libraries](https://ir.law.utk.edu/aall_websites/26/)).

The daily services — *Tax Notes Today Federal*, *Tax Notes Today International* and *Tax Notes Today State* — were added between 1987 and 1991, so the daily federal service predates two of the three weeklies ([American Association of Law Libraries](https://ir.law.utk.edu/aall_websites/26/)). Beyond the six core titles, Tax Notes produces research and reference tools and specialized services covering tax-exempt organizations, state tax audit guidance and multinational tax treaties, and holds conferences on tax policy and administration ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). It states plainly that it is a tax publisher that does not provide tax advice or preparation services ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

## Suing for the documents it publishes

Tax Notes' most distinctive feature is that its journalism and litigation are one project. In 1972 Tax Analysts first sued the Internal Revenue Service to gain public access to private letter rulings (PLRs) and technical advice memoranda (TAMs) — documents through which the IRS gave legal advice to specific taxpayers and its own field agents ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). The D.C. Circuit decided the case in 1974, holding that letter rulings were "statements of policy and interpretations which have been adopted by the agency" under the Freedom of Information Act and must be made available for public inspection ([U.S. Court of Appeals, D.C. Circuit](https://www.taxnotes.com/tax-analysts-litigation/foia-requires-disclosure-letter-rulings/2023/12/17/1m32d)).

The same decision drew a line the publisher's own summary does not: it held technical advice memoranda "specifically exempted from disclosure by statute" because they dealt directly with information in filed returns ([U.S. Court of Appeals, D.C. Circuit](https://www.taxnotes.com/tax-analysts-litigation/foia-requires-disclosure-letter-rulings/2023/12/17/1m32d)). Congress later required the IRS to publicly disclose TAMs as well, and Tax Analysts kept litigating disclosure for decades, including suits over field service advice and Chief Counsel emails ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). The publisher describes the through-line plainly: through investigative journalism and "if necessary, the courts," it brings "secretive decision-making into the open" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

The publication was already acting as an investigator before the first lawsuit was decided. In 1973, amid the scandal over President Nixon's deduction for donating his vice-presidential papers to the National Archives, Tax Analysts was tipped off by former IRS Commissioner Sheldon S. Cohen and produced an extensive report detailing problems and irregularities in the deduction, which it circulated among Washington journalists ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)).

## Editors and editorial standards

Christopher Bergin shaped the modern publication. He arrived from Prentice Hall in 1991, became publisher and executive director in 2001 — replacing founder Field — and served as president and publisher until 2017 ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-christopher-e-bergin-shoulders-we-stand/2020/07/24/2c7ml)). His first reforms were editorial: an editorial review process under which outside submissions could be rejected on qualitative grounds, and a three-level pipeline of line edit, copy edit and proofread, which made Tax Notes "an editors' shop, with less deference given to the author's original language" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-christopher-e-bergin-shoulders-we-stand/2020/07/24/2c7ml)). The stated priority was the reader's time: burying the lede became "a cardinal sin" and nondescript headlines were banished ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-christopher-e-bergin-shoulders-we-stand/2020/07/24/2c7ml)).

Bergin also rescued the business, taking over when the organization was two months from being unable to make payroll, and consolidated its scattered offices into a new headquarters building in Falls Church, Virginia, in December 2006 ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-christopher-e-bergin-shoulders-we-stand/2020/07/24/2c7ml)). Cara Griffith succeeded him as president and CEO in 2017, having previously overseen the editorial department and begun her career as a writer covering state and local taxation ([AICPA](https://aicpaconferences.com/b/sp/cara-griffith-33221)). The current masthead includes Ariel Greenblum as editor in chief of *Tax Notes Federal* ([Acast](https://shows.acast.com/on-tax/episodes/ariel-greenblum-of-tax-notes-federal)) and David D. Stewart as editor in chief of *Tax Notes Today International* ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/tax-notes-live/tax-notes-podcasts/tax-notes-talk/government-shutdown-ends-tax-questions-remain/7t99y)). The publisher reports a headquarters staff of almost 200 and more than 250 correspondents spanning more than 125 countries ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

## Notable coverage and contributors

Two bylines define the publication's influence. Martin Sullivan, its chief economist, wrote more than 1,000 articles for Tax Notes before retiring in April 2026, his first byline appearing nearly 45 years earlier ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/marty-sullivan-longtime-tax-notes-chief-economist-retires/2026/04/24/7vr5c)). He began writing regularly in 1995, and from the mid-2000s he sounded the alarm about U.S. multinationals shifting profits to offshore tax havens; his calculations of the low effective tax rates on the overseas profits of Apple and other multinationals helped put a spotlight on what came to be known as "stateless income" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/marty-sullivan-longtime-tax-notes-chief-economist-retires/2026/04/24/7vr5c)). His work was credited with helping spur the OECD's base erosion and profit shifting project and the international tax reforms in the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, including the mandatory repatriation provision, the base erosion antiabuse tax and the global intangible low-taxed income regime ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/marty-sullivan-longtime-tax-notes-chief-economist-retires/2026/04/24/7vr5c)). Steven Pearlstein profiled him in the [Washington Post](/organizations/washington-post/) in October 2013, calling his columns "fair and rational" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/marty-sullivan-longtime-tax-notes-chief-economist-retires/2026/04/24/7vr5c)).

The second is David Cay Johnston, the Pulitzer Prize-winning former tax reporter for the [New York Times](/organizations/new-york-times/), who wrote the "Johnston's Take" column in Tax Notes after leaving the newspaper in 2008 — the June 22, 2009 issue carries his column on page 1473 ([Tax Notes](https://www.woodllp.com/Media/Press/pdf/Finding.pdf)). The pattern — a specialist publication that mainstream reporters and policymakers read — is the point: Tax Notes' customers include the IRS, the U.S. Tax Court and the tax writers in Congress, and it calls itself "an indispensable resource" for those who teach and study tax law ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

## Beyond print: podcast, history project and recognition

Tax Notes extends beyond its magazines. It produces *Tax Notes Talk*, a weekly podcast hosted by David D. Stewart, in which its reporters explain fast-moving developments — a November 2026 episode covered the record 43-day government shutdown and the IRS furlough of roughly half its workforce ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/tax-notes-live/tax-notes-podcasts/tax-notes-talk/government-shutdown-ends-tax-questions-remain/7t99y)). The Tax History Project, established in 1995, provides scholars, policymakers, journalists and the public with information on the history of U.S. public finance, including an archive of presidential tax returns ([University of Illinois Library](https://www.library.illinois.edu/govinfo/u-of-i-unit-library-homepage/researchtools/subject/taxes/)).

Recognition has come from journalism bodies rather than the tax world alone. Tax Notes reporter Jonathan Curry was a finalist in the Society of Professional Journalists' Washington chapter 2024 Dateline Awards, in the newsletter/trade features category, for "A New Era: How Revenue Officers Went From Cowboys to Desk Jockeys" ([Society of Professional Journalists, Washington D.C. Chapter](https://mailchi.mp/80d8f240c1cc/dateline-awards-2024-finalists-and-winners-press-release)). In 2026 the publication's LinkedIn account was a national finalist in the American Society of Business Publication Editors' Azbee Awards, in the Social Media Account of the Year category, in a competition that drew more than 740 entries ([American Society of Business Publication Editors](https://asbpe.org/news/2026/04/22/2026-national-azbee-awards-finalists/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Tax Notes?** Tax Notes is the portfolio of tax news and commentary publications from Tax Analysts, a nonprofit, nonpartisan publisher: three weekly magazines (Federal, International and State) and three daily online services, all focused on tax policy and administration ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). It is a publication, not a law firm or advisory service, and it states that it does not provide tax advice or preparation services ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

**When was Tax Notes first published?** The first issue appeared in 1972, written by founder Thomas F. Field in his Arlington backyard and initially distributed free to members of the media before subscriptions were charged ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)). The publisher itself, Tax Analysts, was founded in 1970 ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

**What is the difference between Tax Notes and Tax Analysts?** Tax Analysts is the nonprofit publisher; Tax Notes is the brand under which it publishes its magazines, daily services and research tools ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). The publisher also runs conferences, a history project and disclosure litigation that are not themselves the publication.

**How did Tax Notes force the IRS to disclose secret rulings?** Tax Analysts sued under the Freedom of Information Act in 1972, and the D.C. Circuit held in 1974 that IRS letter rulings must be disclosed; Congress later required disclosure of technical advice memoranda as well ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). The documents won in that litigation became part of the publication's document service for practitioners ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)).

**Who reads Tax Notes?** By the publisher's own account, more than 150,000 tax professionals in law and accounting firms, corporations, government agencies and academic institutions, including the IRS, the U.S. Tax Court and congressional tax writers ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). Its most prolific voice, Martin Sullivan, wrote more than 1,000 articles over nearly 45 years ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/marty-sullivan-longtime-tax-notes-chief-economist-retires/2026/04/24/7vr5c)).
