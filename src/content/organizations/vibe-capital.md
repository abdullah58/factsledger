## Overview

Vibe Capital is a solo general-partner venture fund founded by Teachable founder Ankur Nagpal that backs pre-seed and seed founders, with roughly half its investments intended for emerging markets ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/)). It raised $70 million, backed [Alt](/organizations/alt/)'s Series B, then shrank by roughly 43% in 2023 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/25/venture-capital-solo-gps/)).This profile covers Vibe Capital, Nagpal's fund — not Teachable, and not his later startup — and follows it through its launch, its Alt backing, and its contraction.

## An operator fund, built for founders outside the usual map

Nagpal's pitch for Vibe was explicitly that of a peer, not an institution. He had spent seven years building Teachable, a marketplace for teachers to turn online courses into businesses, raising only $12.5 million before selling to Hotmart in a deal reportedly worth a quarter of a billion dollars, and he wanted to use that founder experience to help other founders scale ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/)). His argument was that entrepreneurs want investments from people who feel like peers, are on their level, and work at their speed — in his phrase, founders want investors with good vibes ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/)). Vibe officially launched in October 2021 as a $60 million fund to back pre-seed and seed founders across all sectors, especially physical and mental health, with a further $10 million to be raised post-launch from community and other limited partners via AngelList ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/)).

The international angle was both thesis and biography. Nagpal said Vibe would make roughly half its investments in emerging markets, because of a shortage of operator solo-general-partner funds focused outside the United States, and among its first ten investments in the new fund were companies in Portugal, Kenya, Zambia, Canada, Pakistan, India and Chile ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/)). Vibe's own site states the same theses in its own terms: that venture capital will shape the next decade in emerging markets, that talent is equally distributed but opportunity is not, that enabling entrepreneurs is the most exciting category, that the financialisation of everything has begun, and that proactive wellness will explode ([Vibe Capital](https://www.vibe.vc/)). Nagpal, a first-generation American who had lived around the world, planned to spend weeks at a time in the geographies he invested in — a month in India that December, two months in Latin America in January ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/)).

Vibe was not Nagpal's first fund. He had debuted an $11.75 million fund the previous spring, whose portfolio performance he described as well above expectations, with 24 companies having a priced-round mark-up and six more a substantial SAFE mark-up, for around 70% net internal rate of return on paper — while admitting he was unsure how much of that growth was the portfolio and how much was simply market conditions ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/)). Vibe's own site rounds that first fund to $12 million and lists Fund 2 at $70 million; the $11.75 million versus $12 million difference is rounding, and both figures are shown here rather than silently picking one ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/); [Vibe Capital](https://www.vibe.vc/)). As a founder, Nagpal had also joined Spearhead, the Ravikant and Fagnan program for turning founders into investors, and Spearhead in turn became an investor in Vibe's fund ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/)). Its limited partners included Bessemer Venture Partners, Tribe Capital and Foundation Capital, alongside individuals including GSV's Deborah Quazzo, Andreessen Horowitz's Andrew Chen, Better Tomorrow Ventures' Sheel Mohnot, Gumroad founder Sahil Lavingia and Sounding Board's Christine Tao ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/10/20/vibe-capital-is-a-new-venture-firm-built-for-scrappy-international-founders/)).

## The no-fee structure, the Alt bet, and the contraction

Vibe structured itself differently from most funds, and that structure is part of why the 2023 contraction matters. The average venture fund charges "2 and 20" — a 2% management fee and a 20% share of profits — but Vibe charges no management fee, so every dollar raised is invested into startups, and about a third of the capital in every fund has been Nagpal's own money ([Vibe Capital](https://www.vibe.vc/)). That alignment argument — no fee drag, substantial personal capital — was central to how Nagpal sold the fund to its more than 200 investors.

One month after launch, Vibe appeared on a very different kind of cap table: Alt's $75 million Series B in November 2021, led by Spearhead, with Vibe Capital listed among the backers alongside Seven Seven Six, Breyer Capital, Shrug Capital and Apollo Projects ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/11/11/collectible-card-trading-platform-alt-raises-75m-as-it-eyes-other-asset-categories-and-preps-its-mobile-launch/)). The Alt backing fits Vibe's financialisation thesis — a marketplace turning collectibles into investable, tradable assets — even though the sources read do not state Vibe's check size or its reasoning for the deal, and neither is claimed here.

Then came the contraction that makes Vibe a case study in the solo-general-partner cycle rather than just another launch story. In March 2023 TechCrunch reported that Nagpal had raised a $70 million Vibe fund from over 200 investors the previous year, and was now shrinking it by roughly 43%, cancelling capital calls and sending back money that had already been wired ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/25/venture-capital-solo-gps/)). Nagpal told TechCrunch the contraction happened because he was busy building his own startup, Ocho, and because the funding environment had shifted to more realistic expectations: "What looked like a $10 billion outcome is now a $1 billion dollar outcome," and he was more confident of returning a higher multiple from a smaller fund ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/25/venture-capital-solo-gps/)). His limited partners were surprised but "super happy" to get the capital back, he said, and one of his biggest investors was Tiger Global, then notorious for retreating from venture-fund bets ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/25/venture-capital-solo-gps/)). The move would let him put 90% of his time into his new startup ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/25/venture-capital-solo-gps/)). That is where the verified record in the sources read ends: a fund launched on an operator thesis, briefly scaled to $70 million, and then deliberately shrunk — not failed, on the evidence read, but consciously reduced.
