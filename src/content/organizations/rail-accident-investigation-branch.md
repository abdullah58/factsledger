## Overview

The Rail Accident Investigation Branch is the United Kingdom's independent rail accident investigator — a government body whose entire job is to find out why a railway accident happened, and whose inspectors are legally forbidden from doing the one thing every other investigator does: point the finger. Born from the ashes of the 1999 Ladbroke Grove crash and operational since October 2005, RAIB is a small organisation — 43 people working from two centres, in Derby and Farnborough — with unusually large statutory powers: its inspectors can compel evidence, and it can overrule the police at an accident scene ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/rail-accident-investigation-branch/about)). Everything it publishes serves one purpose — making the railway safer — and it is explicitly barred from the two purposes that usually attach to a government investigation: establishing blame and prosecuting offenders.

## No blame, with legal teeth

"Without apportioning blame" sounds like a mission statement. In RAIB's case it is an enforced legal regime, built into the 2003 Act that created the Branch. The Branch's fundamental purpose is to undertake investigations "which look for the root causes of accidents and incidents without apportioning blame," and it has no prosecution function at all — possible breaches of legislation are left to the police and safety authorities ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/20/notes/division/4/1); [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/rail-accident-investigation-branch/about)).

But the statute gives the Branch hard powers precisely so that the no-blame principle works in practice. RAIB inspectors can enter railway property and adjoining land where evidence may lie; failing to comply with an inspector's requirement, or knowingly giving misleading evidence, is a criminal offence; and when police or other investigators are working the same scene, the Chief Inspector of Rail Accidents gets to decide what course of action may be taken — RAIB has primacy ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/20/notes/division/4/1)). Most tellingly, the legislation protects witnesses from the investigation itself: a statement given to RAIB is not disclosed to third parties — not even the police or the safety executive — unless the witness personally releases it or a court orders disclosure ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/20/notes/division/4/1)). People talk to RAIB because they know their words cannot be handed to the people who might prosecute them. That is what the "independence" of an accident investigation branch actually means: independence not just from the railway industry, but from the criminal process too.

## Born from Ladbroke Grove

RAIB exists because of one crash. On 5 October 1999, two trains collided head-on at high speed at Ladbroke Grove Junction, about two miles west of Paddington station. Thirty-one people died, including both train drivers, and more than 400 were injured ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/20/notes/division/4/1)). The public inquiry led by Lord Cullen reported in two parts; the second, on rail safety management and regulation, made 74 recommendations, of which 17 concerned accident investigation — including the creation of an independent investigation branch with proper powers and duties ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/20/notes/division/4/1)). RAIB's own history begins there: it describes itself as created on Lord Cullen's recommendation ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/rail-accident-investigation-branch/about)).

Part 1 of the Railways and Transport Safety Act 2003 implemented that recommendation, establishing RAIB on the model of the two older investigation branches — the Air Accidents Investigation Branch and the Marine Accident Investigation Branch ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/20/notes/division/4/1)). The Branch sits administratively within the Department for Transport but is operationally independent in how it conducts investigations, and it sends its reports directly to the Secretary of State — an arrangement that mirrors the air and marine branches ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/20/notes/division/4/1)). The 2005 regulations that set out its detailed duties came into force on 17 October 2005, the date RAIB became operational, with the UK part of the Channel Tunnel added to its remit from 31 January 2006 ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/rail-accident-investigation-branch/about)).

## What it investigates — and what it leaves alone

RAIB's scope covers the UK main line networks ([Network Rail](/organizations/network-rail/) and Northern Ireland Railways), the London Underground, other metro systems, tramways, heritage railways and the UK part of the Channel Tunnel — and railway and tramway operators are legally required to notify it of specified accidents and incidents ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/rail-accident-investigation-branch/about)). Beyond notified events, the Branch monitors industry reporting systems and has the discretion to investigate anything in scope, whether or not it had to be notified ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/rail-accident-investigation-branch/about)). Not everything that looks like a railway is in scope: self-contained railways entirely on private land, industrial and dock systems, museums, funfairs and cliff lifts are all outside its powers ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/rail-accident-investigation-branch/about)).

Serious accidents must be investigated; for non-serious accidents and incidents the Branch has discretion, and it exercises that discretion frankly. Some fatal events on the main line are never investigated at all — usually because the evidence suggests the person deliberately put themselves in harm's way, or because, after enquiries, RAIB is satisfied there are no safety lessons to be learned ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/rail-accident-investigation-branch/about)). When it does investigate, it publishes a full report; for smaller events it publishes "safety digests," short learning-point summaries that emerge from preliminary examinations ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/rail-accident-investigation-branch/about)). One of those full reports — Report 18/2008 — covered the [Barrow upon Soar crash](https://factsledger.com/events/barrow-upon-soar-rail-crash) of 1 February 2008, in which a tipper lorry's raised body demolished a footbridge and derailed a passing passenger train; that investigation's recommendations were aimed at Network Rail's procedures for managing road vehicles near live railway lines ([Barrow upon Soar rail crash](/events/barrow-upon-soar-rail-crash/), whose record is built from the full RAIB Report 18/2008).

Leadership of the Branch turns over at the top in November 2026, when Andy Lewis becomes Chief Inspector of Rail Accidents as Andrew Hall retires after almost five years in the role ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/raib-has-a-new-chief-inspector)).
