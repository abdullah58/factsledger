## Overview

Two heads of government, a decade apart, tried to sue The Wall Street Journal into silence — and both times the paper stood by its reporting ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/asia/se-asia/malaysian-pm-najib-to-take-legal-action-against-wall-street-journal-report); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/28/politics/trump-refiles-lawsuit-wall-street-journal-epstein?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). In 2015, Malaysian prime minister Najib Razak threatened legal action over the Journal's reporting that about US$700 million of state-fund money reached his personal accounts ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/asia/se-asia/malaysian-pm-najib-to-take-legal-action-against-wall-street-journal-report)). In 2025–26, President Donald Trump filed, lost, and refiled a $10 billion defamation suit over the paper's report on a birthday letter to Jeffrey Epstein bearing his name ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/28/politics/trump-refiles-lawsuit-wall-street-journal-epstein?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). That posture — publish, get threatened, hold the line — runs through the paper's whole identity: a 137-year-old financial daily built on Charles Dow and Edward Jones's bulletins to Wall Street traders, bought by Rupert Murdoch in 2007 for roughly $5 billion, and still recognizable by its hand-drawn stipple portraits and its quirk-filled front page ([Museum of American Finance](https://www.moaf.org/publications-collections/financial-history-magazine/109/_res/id=Attachments/index=0/Wall%20Street%20Journal%20125th%20Anniversary.pdf); [Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/archive/301343)).

## The Trump lawsuits, 2025–2026

The Journal's July 2025 report described a collection of letters given to Epstein for his 50th birthday in 2003 — one of which, the paper reported, bore Trump's name and an outline of a naked woman ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/28/politics/trump-refiles-lawsuit-wall-street-journal-epstein?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). Trump denied writing it and sued the Journal's publisher and two of its reporters ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/28/politics/trump-refiles-lawsuit-wall-street-journal-epstein?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). A Dow Jones spokesperson said the company had "full confidence in the rigor and accuracy of our reporting" and would defend vigorously ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/28/politics/trump-refiles-lawsuit-wall-street-journal-epstein?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). The first suit was dismissed after a federal judge ruled that Trump had "failed to plausibly allege" the newspaper acted with "actual malice" ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/28/politics/trump-refiles-lawsuit-wall-street-journal-epstein?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). In late May 2026 he refiled, seeking $10 billion in damages and alleging "glaring failures in journalistic ethics and standards of accurate reporting" ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/28/politics/trump-refiles-lawsuit-wall-street-journal-epstein?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). CNN reported that legal experts it consulted could not recall any past instance of a sitting president suing a news outlet over a story — and noted the suit fit a pattern: Trump filed a $15 billion defamation suit against the New York Times in September and a $10 billion suit against the BBC in December, the latter over spliced footage of his January 6, 2021 speech ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/28/politics/trump-refiles-lawsuit-wall-street-journal-epstein?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)).

## The 1MDB precedent, 2015

The Trump arc was not the first time a head of government threatened the paper over a money-in-politics investigation. In July 2015 the Journal reported, citing documents it said it had obtained — including bank transfer forms and flow charts prepared by government investigators — that almost US$700 million (RM2.6 billion) from the state investment fund 1MDB went into Najib Razak's personal accounts ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/asia/se-asia/malaysian-pm-najib-to-take-legal-action-against-wall-street-journal-report)). Najib's political secretary told a Malay daily the report was "done with bad intention and based on unsubstantiated and dubious sources," adding "We will take legal action"; the prime minister's office called the report "political sabotage" aimed at removing a democratically elected prime minister ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/asia/se-asia/malaysian-pm-najib-to-take-legal-action-against-wall-street-journal-report)). The paper did not retract, and publicly stated that it stood by the report ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/asia/se-asia/malaysian-pm-najib-to-take-legal-action-against-wall-street-journal-report)).

## Ownership and the newsroom

The paper's first edition was printed on July 8, 1889, by three partners — Charles H. Dow, Edward D. Jones and Charles Bergstresser — and sold for two cents ([Museum of American Finance](https://www.moaf.org/publications-collections/financial-history-magazine/109/_res/id=Attachments/index=0/Wall%20Street%20Journal%20125th%20Anniversary.pdf)). Dow and Jones had left the Kiernan News Agency in November 1882 to form their own firm in a small room at 15 Wall Street, next to the Stock Exchange; a printed daily news sheet they began in 1883, slipped into the last delivery of the day, became the Journal's direct forerunner ([Museum of American Finance](https://www.moaf.org/publications-collections/financial-history-magazine/109/_res/id=Attachments/index=0/Wall%20Street%20Journal%20125th%20Anniversary.pdf)). The Dow Jones Industrial Average followed in 1896 ([Museum of American Finance](https://www.moaf.org/publications-collections/financial-history-magazine/109/_res/id=Attachments/index=0/Wall%20Street%20Journal%20125th%20Anniversary.pdf)). In 1902 Clarence Barron bought Dow Jones & Co. for $130,000, when the paper's circulation was about 7,000 ([Museum of American Finance](https://www.moaf.org/publications-collections/financial-history-magazine/109/_res/id=Attachments/index=0/Wall%20Street%20Journal%20125th%20Anniversary.pdf)).

The modern paper was shaped by managing editor Bernard Kilgore, who remade it from what [[Columbia Journalism Review](/organizations/columbia-journalism-review/)](https://factsledger.com/organizations/columbia-journalism-review/) calls "a daily blizzard of random financial data" into a literate storytelling paper — a bet that carried circulation from 30,000 to more than a million ([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/the_audit/a_quality_barometer_for_the_wa.php)). The Bancroft family, Barron's heirs, controlled Dow Jones for over a century until Rupert Murdoch's News Corp won control in a roughly $5 billion takeover in August 2007, backed by about 37 percent of the shareholder vote ([Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/archive/301343)). The deal created a five-member committee charged with "assuring the continued journalistic and editorial integrity and independence of Dow Jones' publications and services" ([Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/archive/301343)) — a body that still matters: it unanimously approved Emma Tucker's appointment as editor-in-chief in 2022 ([News Corp](http://newscorp.com/2022/12/12/emma-tucker-named-next-editor-in-chief-of-the-wall-street-journal-and-dow-jones-newswires-matt-murray-takes-new-role-at-news-corp/)). Tucker, named on December 12, 2022 and in the job from February 1, 2023, is the first woman to lead the 133-year-old paper; she succeeded Matt Murray, whose tenure since June 2018 produced a 2019 Pulitzer for National Reporting and the Facebook Files series, and doubled digital-only subscriptions from about 1.6 million to nearly 3.2 million ([News Corp](http://newscorp.com/2022/12/12/emma-tucker-named-next-editor-in-chief-of-the-wall-street-journal-and-dow-jones-newswires-matt-murray-takes-new-role-at-news-corp/)).

## How it looks and reads

The Journal's most recognizable visual signature is the hedcut — the stipple portrait, hand-drawn with dots and dashes instead of lines, that has graced its pages since 1979, when artist Kevin Sprouls devised the technique ([Taipei Times](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/biz/archives/2002/04/28/0000133739)). The engraving-like style is a deliberate nod to stock-certificate illustration and U.S. dollar bills ([Taipei Times](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/biz/archives/2002/04/28/0000133739)). Sprouls started at Dow Jones as a freelancer in the marketing department; his style caught the eye of page-one editor Glynn Mapes ([Taipei Times](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/biz/archives/2002/04/28/0000133739)). The Smithsonian's National Portrait Gallery calls the hedcuts "an American icon," and in the spring of 2002 the Journal donated a group of them to the museum, which opened the exhibition "Picturing Business in America" ([Smithsonian National Portrait Gallery](https://npg.si.edu/exh/journal/)). That same year the paper auctioned 582 hedcuts on eBay — Tiger Woods's portrait fetched $860, Michael Jordan's $733.45 — with proceeds going to the Dow Jones Newspaper Fund, founded in 1958, to support minority journalism scholarships ([Taipei Times](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/biz/archives/2002/04/28/0000133739)). The paper publishes about ten hedcuts a day from an archive of more than 9,000 portraits ([Taipei Times](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/biz/archives/2002/04/28/0000133739)). The style exists because of a legendary resistance to photographs: retired executive editor Fred Taylor once said, "I've always thought that one word was worth a thousand pictures" ([Smithsonian National Portrait Gallery](https://npg.si.edu/exh/journal/)).

The front page's other signature is the A-hed — the offbeat, apropos-of-nothing feature that has run on Page One since 1943, named for the shape of its headline back in the day ([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/the_audit/a_quality_barometer_for_the_wa.php)). Close Journal-watchers long treated it as a barometer of the paper's editorial health ([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/the_audit/a_quality_barometer_for_the_wa.php)). And behind it all sits the paywall: launched in 1996 as the industry's early major "hard" paywall — paying customers only, modeled on the print subscription — and still, in Columbia Journalism Review's testing, the tightest among daily newspapers, with no metered access and no exceptions for search traffic ([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/business_of_news/news-paywalls-new-york-times-wall-street-journal.php)).

## Record and reach

In 2025 the Journal's staff won the Pulitzer Prize for National Reporting for its coverage of Elon Musk's political and personal evolution — "This is the Journal's 40th Pulitzer Prize," the announcement said ([Editor & Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/the-2025-pulitzer-prizes-honor-exemplary-journalism,256264)). The paper's global reporting has come at a cost: South Asia bureau chief Daniel Pearl was kidnapped and murdered in Pakistan in 2002 while on assignment ([Museum of American Finance](https://www.moaf.org/publications-collections/financial-history-magazine/109/_res/id=Attachments/index=0/Wall%20Street%20Journal%20125th%20Anniversary.pdf)). By the paper's 125th anniversary in 2014 it was the largest-circulation daily in the United States, at about 2.4 million ([Museum of American Finance](https://www.moaf.org/publications-collections/financial-history-magazine/109/_res/id=Attachments/index=0/Wall%20Street%20Journal%20125th%20Anniversary.pdf)).
