## Overview

The American Society for Engineering Education (ASEE) is the odd one out among ABET's founders — and the most important one for understanding what accreditation is for. Founded in 1893 in Chicago as the Society for the Promotion of Engineering Education (SPEE), it is the organization of the people who *teach* engineers, not the people who *employ* them. By its stated figures it has about 12,000 individual members and 400+ institutional affiliates, headquartered in Washington, D.C. In 1932, SPEE took the educators' seat at the table where the accreditation system was created: it was one of the seven societies that founded the Engineers' Council for Professional Development — now [ABET](/organizations/abet/) — in [New York City](/places/new-york-city/) ([ABET](https://www.abet.org/about-abet/history/)).

## Born at the World's Fair: the curriculum wars

The society began as an outgrowth of Division E of the World Engineering Congress, held amid Chicago's 1893 World's Columbian Exposition — the fair that introduced millions of Americans to the wonders of electricity. Seventy educators formed the group; 14 of them would eventually become its president, and dues were $3.50 a year ([ASEE](http://peer.asee.org/history-of-the-development-of-engineering-economic-representation-within-asee.pdf)).

The founding fight was pedagogical. The 1862 Morrill Land-Grant Act had seeded public institutions teaching "the Mechanic Arts," and a generation of their graduates had entered industry with practical skills but thin grounding in science. SPEE's founders concluded engineering curricula should stress fundamental scientific and mathematical principles, not hands-on apprenticeship — and organized to make that view win. By 1910 the society counted 938 members (767 teachers, 171 practitioners) across 121 engineering-teaching colleges, and its *Journal of Engineering Education* began publication that year. In 1911 Andrew Carnegie joined as a Life Member, and Frank B. Gilbreth — the scientific-management pioneer — joined and held a 1912 symposium on the subject. (A 1931 proposal to rename the society the "Engineering Education Society" failed for lack of council support ([ASEE](http://peer.asee.org/history-of-the-development-of-engineering-economic-representation-within-asee.pdf)); the current name dates to a 1946 reorganization ([Northeastern University Archives](https://archivesspace.library.northeastern.edu/repositories/2/resources/847)).)

Over the decades the society issued the landmark studies that periodically rewrote American engineering curricula — the Mann Report (1907), the Wickenden Study of the 1920s, and the Grinter Report (1955) — each pushing the field further toward science-based education.

## The educators' seat at the accreditation table

The society did not just theorize about standards — it pushed for their enforcement. Its archives record that in the 1930s SPEE "encouraged the accreditation of engineering programs to tighten curricular requirements" ([Northeastern University Archives](https://archivesspace.library.northeastern.edu/repositories/2/resources/847)). When seven societies created the Engineers' Council for Professional Development in 1932, six represented practicing engineers — the [American Society of Civil Engineers](/organizations/asce/), the American Institute of Mining and Metallurgical Engineers (now [AIME](/organizations/aime/)), the [American Society of Mechanical Engineers](/organizations/asme/), the American Institute of Electrical Engineers (now [IEEE](/organizations/ieee/)), the [American Institute of Chemical Engineers](/organizations/aiche/), and the National Council of State Boards of Engineering Examiners (now [NCEES](/organizations/ncees/)). The seventh was SPEE: the teachers ([ABET](https://www.abet.org/about-abet/history/)). That composition is the whole theory of accreditation in miniature — the profession sets the standards, but the educators who must meet them get a founding vote. ASEE's journals (*Prism* magazine, the *Journal of Engineering Education*, *Advances in Engineering Education*) remain the profession's teaching literature, and it works with ABET on educational outcomes in the [United States](/places/united-states/) and beyond.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does ASEE stand for?**
The American Society for Engineering Education — a nonprofit association of engineering educators, founded 1893 in Chicago as the Society for the Promotion of Engineering Education (SPEE), headquartered in Washington, D.C.

**Why was ASEE founded?**
To organize engineering professors behind science-and-mathematics-based curricula rather than pure hands-on apprenticeship, amid the post-Morrill-Act expansion of engineering schools. It was born at the 1893 Chicago World's Fair out of the World Engineering Congress.

**What is ASEE's connection to ABET?**
As SPEE, it was one of seven societies that founded ABET's predecessor — the Engineers' Council for Professional Development — in New York City in 1932. It was the only teaching-focused society among the founders.

**How big is ASEE?**
By its stated figures: about 12,000 individual members (deans, professors, instructors, students, industry representatives) and more than 400 institutional affiliates.

**What are the Mann, Wickenden, and Grinter reports?**
Landmark ASEE curriculum studies — 1907, the 1920s, and 1955 respectively — that successively pushed American engineering education toward science-based fundamentals.
