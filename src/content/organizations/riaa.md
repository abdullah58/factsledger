## Overview

The Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA) is the trade association for the [United States](/places/united-states/) recording industry, founded in 1952 and based in Washington, D.C. It certifies Gold and Platinum records, publishes the industry's official US revenue data, and litigates and lobbies on copyright — and it is also the body behind the RIAA equalization curve used in vinyl playback ([Martech Zone](https://martech.zone/acronym/riaa/); [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/companies/riaa/)).

Two different things share the initials, and they belong to the same organisation. The RIAA is a trade association of record companies; the "RIAA curve" is a technical standard that association set for vinyl playback, issued in 1954 ([Hifi Pig](https://www.hifipig.com/record-equalisation-explained/)). When a turntable offers an "RIAA" setting, it means the curve, not the trade group.

Its members — the three majors, Universal Music, Sony Music and Warner Music, plus independents from Disney Music Group to Tommy Boy, Partisan Records and Concord — create, manufacture or distribute nearly 85% of all legitimate recorded music produced and sold in the United States ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/companies/riaa/)). That reach is what makes its two public roles matter: its awards are the industry's sales scoreboard, and its revenue reports are the standard account of the American recorded-music business ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/companies/riaa/)).

## From technical standards to the Gold record

The RIAA began as a standards body, not a sales-awards body. It was formed in 1952 to administer recording copyright fees and problems, work with trade unions, and do research relating to the record industry and government regulations ([Martech Zone](https://martech.zone/acronym/riaa/)). Its early standards included the RIAA equalization curve, the format of the stereophonic record groove, and the dimensions of 33 1/3, 45 and 78 rpm records ([Martech Zone](https://martech.zone/acronym/riaa/)). The curve mattered because, from the 1940s, every record company applied its own equalization — more than 100 combinations were in use, from Columbia and RCA Victor to Decca, EMI and the BBC ([Hifi Pig](https://www.hifipig.com/record-equalisation-explained/)). The RIAA curve, issued in 1954, gave the industry one playback standard, though not until 1958 did all the major companies adopt it ([Hifi Pig](https://www.hifipig.com/record-equalisation-explained/)).

Its most famous creation arrived in that same year, 1958, when it launched the Gold record program to give sales awards an independent, audited basis ([The Saturday Evening Post](https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2023/03/who-first-and-how-big-record-records/)). On 14 March 1958, Perry Como's "Catch a Falling Star" became the first Gold single; at the time Gold meant one million units, while Silver denoted 500,000 ([The Saturday Evening Post](https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2023/03/who-first-and-how-big-record-records/)). The first Gold album followed later in 1958: the film soundtrack for *Oklahoma!* ([The Saturday Evening Post](https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2023/03/who-first-and-how-big-record-records/)). The program then grew tier by tier: Platinum arrived in 1976, first awarded to the Eagles' *Their Greatest Hits (1971–1975)*; Multi-Platinum followed in 1984 and Diamond, for ten million units, in 1999 ([The Saturday Evening Post](https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2023/03/who-first-and-how-big-record-records/); [RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/about-awards/)). Its record book still stands: Elvis Presley holds the most Gold albums at 101 and Barbra Streisand leads women with 51 ([The Saturday Evening Post](https://www.saturdayeveningpost.com/2023/03/who-first-and-how-big-record-records/)).

## How Gold and Platinum certification works today

A Gold or Platinum plaque is not handed out automatically — the label has to apply for it. Certifications are awarded in "units", not raw sales: for a single, one unit is 150 on-demand audio or video streams or one permanent download or physical sale, while for an album one unit is 1,500 on-demand streams, 10 track downloads, or one album sale, and the formats can be combined ([RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/apply/)). The requestor submits its sales and streaming data, and the independent firm Gelfand, Rennert & Feldman — whose East Coast audit team is based in [New York City](/places/new-york-city/) — audits the figures before any award is made ([RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/apply/)).

| Award | Units required | Established |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gold | 500,000 | 1958 |
| Platinum | 1,000,000 | 1976 |
| Multi-Platinum | 2,000,000, then each additional 1,000,000 | 1984 |
| Diamond | 10,000,000 | 1999 |
| Oro (Latin) | 30,000 | 2001 |
| Platino (Latin) | 60,000 | 2001 |
| Diamante (Latin) | 600,000 | 2001 |

Only activity inside the United States counts — exports are excluded ([RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/about-awards/)). Only on-demand, non-user-generated streams qualify, and several familiar numbers do not count: Luminate point-of-sale figures are not used, since certification rests on shipments and digital sales and streams minus returns, while Spotify Discovery Mode, Pandora Radio/Plus, YouTube streams outside official artist or label channels, and most social-media listening are not accepted ([RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/apply/)). The Latin program, Los Premios de Oro y Platino, applies the same structure at lower thresholds to releases with more than 50% Spanish-language lyrics ([RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/about-awards/)).

## The RIAA, the charts, and the industry's numbers

An RIAA certification and a Billboard chart position measure two different things. Billboard's charts rank songs weekly by current popularity: its Global 200 and Billboard Global Excl. U.S., launched on 15 September 2020, collect data from more than 200 territories — official-only streams plus downloads from key online retailers — and its first Global 200 No. 1 was "WAP" by Cardi B featuring Megan Thee Stallion ([Music Ally](https://musically.com/2020/09/15/billboard-and-mrc-launch-two-new-global-music-charts/)). An RIAA certification, by contrast, is a lifetime award for cumulative US units, granted only when a label applies and an auditor verifies the figures ([RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/apply/)).

The association is also the industry's statistician: its 2025 year-end report put US wholesale revenue at $11.535 billion, up 3.1% on 2024 — a record high on the new wholesale basis, which aligns RIAA reporting with the IFPI's Global Music Report ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/us-paid-music-streaming-subscriptions-hit-106-5m-in-2025-adding-6-5m-accounts-yoy-the-strongest-growth-since-2022/); [RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/reports/2025-year-end-music-industry-revenue-report-riaa/)). Streaming supplied $9.474 billion of that total, or 82% of revenue for the fifth consecutive year, and paid subscriptions reached 106.5 million accounts ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/us-paid-music-streaming-subscriptions-hit-106-5m-in-2025-adding-6-5m-accounts-yoy-the-strongest-growth-since-2022/)). US recorded-music revenue reached $11.535 billion at wholesale in 2025, up 3.1% ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/us-paid-music-streaming-subscriptions-hit-106-5m-in-2025-adding-6-5m-accounts-yoy-the-strongest-growth-since-2022/)), which means the Gold and Platinum thresholds now measure success in a streaming market bigger, on the RIAA's own accounting, than ever before. Vinyl is the report's other headline: $1.04 billion in 2025, up 9.3% — its 19th consecutive year of growth ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/us-paid-music-streaming-subscriptions-hit-106-5m-in-2025-adding-6-5m-accounts-yoy-the-strongest-growth-since-2022/)).

## Copyright enforcement: from Grokster to AI

The association has been led since January 2019 by Mitch Glazier, a former House Judiciary staffer who helped enact the Music Modernization Act ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/mitch-glazier-appointed-chairman-and-ceo-of-recording-industry-association-of-america/)). Its most consequential win came on 27 June 2005, when the US Supreme Court ruled unanimously in MGM v. Grokster that Grokster Ltd. and StreamCast Networks could be held liable for copyright infringements committed by users of their peer-to-peer file-sharing software ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1457538/u-s-supreme-court-rules-against-grokster.html)). The plaintiffs included the Motion Picture Association of America, the National Music Publishers' Association and the RIAA, who had taken the case to the highest court after losing below ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1457538/u-s-supreme-court-rules-against-grokster.html)). The Court left the 1984 Sony Betamax ruling untouched but held it does not shelter promoters of infringement: Justice David Souter wrote that a distributor who promotes a device's infringing use "is liable for the resulting acts of infringement by third parties", and the case was sent back to a lower court ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1457538/u-s-supreme-court-rules-against-grokster.html)).

Two decades later the fight moved to artificial intelligence. On 24 June 2024 the major labels sued the AI music service Suno in federal court in Massachusetts, alleging it had copied copyrighted sound recordings en masse to train its model, and a parallel RIAA-coordinated suit targeted Udio ([U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts](https://www.riaa.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Suno-complaint-file-stamped20.pdf); [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/us-musicians-union-files-amended-lawsuit-against-universal-and-warner-over-suno-and-udio-ai-deals/)). The Suno complaint alleged a service with more than 10 million users that had raised $125 million, and argued that fair use does not protect copying recordings to build a competing music generator ([U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts](https://www.riaa.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Suno-complaint-file-stamped20.pdf)). Those are allegations, not findings — but the commercial outcome is already visible: Universal settled with Udio in October 2025, Warner settled with Udio and then with Suno in November 2025, and Sony has settled with neither ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/us-musicians-union-files-amended-lawsuit-against-universal-and-warner-over-suno-and-udio-ai-deals/)). The settlements then drew a second suit, from the American Federation of Musicians, over pay for the session musicians whose recordings were licensed ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/us-musicians-union-files-amended-lawsuit-against-universal-and-warner-over-suno-and-udio-ai-deals/)).

The association has also been drawn into cultural fights it did not start. In 1985 the Parents Music Resource Center, led by Tipper Gore, published its "Filthy Fifteen" list of songs it deemed unsuitable for young listeners, including tracks by Venom, Black Sabbath and Mercyful Fate ([Louder](https://www.loudersound.com/features/lucifer-unleashed-the-satanic-history-of-the-parental-advisory-label)). At a Senate hearing on 19 September 1985 the group urged the RIAA to adopt a ratings system, while Frank Zappa, Dee Snider and John Denver testified against censorship ([Louder](https://www.loudersound.com/features/lucifer-unleashed-the-satanic-history-of-the-parental-advisory-label)). The compromise, born in November 1985, was the Parental Advisory label, which the RIAA agreed to use on selected records at its own discretion — a voluntary industry mark, not a government rating, still in use today ([Louder](https://www.loudersound.com/features/lucifer-unleashed-the-satanic-history-of-the-parental-advisory-label)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What does RIAA stand for?**
RIAA stands for the Recording Industry Association of America, the trade association for the United States recording industry. Founded in 1952 and based in Washington, D.C., it represents major record companies, certifies Gold and Platinum records, publishes US recorded-music revenue data, and advocates on copyright law ([Martech Zone](https://martech.zone/acronym/riaa/); [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/companies/riaa/)).

**How many streams does it take to go Gold?**
A Gold single and a Gold album each need 500,000 units. One single unit equals 150 on-demand US streams or one download, while one album unit equals 1,500 streams, 10 track downloads, or one album sale. Streams alone, a Gold single needs 75 million US streams and a Gold album 750 million ([RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/apply/)).

**What is the difference between an RIAA certification and a Billboard chart position?**
A Billboard chart ranks songs or albums weekly by current popularity, using streaming and download data from more than 200 territories. An RIAA certification is different: it is a lifetime, cumulative award for US units only, granted when a label applies and an independent auditor verifies the figures ([Music Ally](https://musically.com/2020/09/15/billboard-and-mrc-launch-two-new-global-music-charts/); [RIAA](https://www.riaa.com/gold-platinum/apply/)).

**What is the RIAA equalization curve?**
The RIAA equalization curve is a technical standard for vinyl records, issued in 1954. It boosts treble and cuts bass when a record is cut, and a phono preamplifier reverses it on playback. Before it, each label used its own curve — more than 100 were in use ([Hifi Pig](https://www.hifipig.com/record-equalisation-explained/)).

**Is the RIAA still suing over AI music?**
The RIAA's big courtroom fight today is over AI training data: in June 2024 the major labels, coordinated by the RIAA, sued Suno and Udio for allegedly training models on copyrighted recordings. Universal settled with Udio in October 2025 and Warner with both services that November; Sony has settled with neither ([Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/us-musicians-union-files-amended-lawsuit-against-universal-and-warner-over-suno-and-udio-ai-deals/)).
