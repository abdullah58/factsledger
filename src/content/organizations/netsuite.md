## Overview

NetSuite was one of the first software-as-a-service companies: founded in 1998, in part with $125 million from Oracle co-founder Larry Ellison, it built a cloud suite of financials, enterprise resource planning (ERP), and omnichannel commerce software that by 2016 ran the businesses of more than 30,000 companies, organizations, and subsidiaries in over 100 countries ([Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/450402443/Oracle-consummates-acquisition-of-NetSuite-eyes-1bn-cloud-revenue)). It competed especially among small and midsize enterprises against [Salesforce](/organizations/salesforce/) — itself founded in 1999 by former Oracle executive Marc Benioff ([Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/450402443/Oracle-consummates-acquisition-of-NetSuite-eyes-1bn-cloud-revenue)).

In November 2016, Oracle completed its $9.3 billion all-cash acquisition of NetSuite at $109 per share, a deal that added roughly $1 billion to Oracle's annual cloud revenues — NetSuite's 2015 revenue had been $741 million ([Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/450402443/Oracle-consummates-acquisition-of-NetSuite-eyes-1bn-cloud-revenue)).

## A textbook related-party deal — and the shareholder fight it triggered

The acquisition's defining feature was who already owned the target. Oracle co-founder and executive chairman Larry Ellison owned 39.7% of NetSuite's shares, per Fortune figures cited at announcement — an irony GeekWire noted, given Ellison's "vigorous and very public skepticism about cloud computing in 2009," when he had called it "complete gibberish" and "idiocy" ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2016/oracle-buys-netsuite-9-3-billion-bolstering-cloud-efforts-amid-increasing-competition/)). The conflict forced both companies' boards to establish independent committees to review the deal, and the tender terms required that a majority of shares *not* owned by Ellison, his family, or affiliates be tendered for the deal to close ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2016/oracle-buys-netsuite-9-3-billion-bolstering-cloud-efforts-amid-increasing-competition/)).

That minority-protection condition nearly killed it. Mutual fund group T. Rowe Price, which held 18% of NetSuite's shares, held out for more than the $109-per-share offer, and in early October 2016 Oracle extended the tender deadline to November 4 ([Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/450402443/Oracle-consummates-acquisition-of-NetSuite-eyes-1bn-cloud-revenue)). When the deadline arrived, only 56% of NetSuite's shareholders had endorsed the bid, according to the Financial Times — and Ellison himself did not vote ([Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/450402443/Oracle-consummates-acquisition-of-NetSuite-eyes-1bn-cloud-revenue)). The deal closed on November 7, 2016 ([Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/450402443/Oracle-consummates-acquisition-of-NetSuite-eyes-1bn-cloud-revenue)).

There was also a personal symmetry to the combination: NetSuite president and CEO Zach Nelson had served as Oracle's marketing vice president from 1996 to 1998, leading a Forbes reporter to describe the acquisition as "a homecoming of sorts for NetSuite, but one the company had fended off for years" ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2016/oracle-buys-netsuite-9-3-billion-bolstering-cloud-efforts-amid-increasing-competition/)).
