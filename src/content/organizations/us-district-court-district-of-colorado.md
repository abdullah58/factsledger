## Overview

The United States District Court for the District of Colorado — cited as D. Colo. — is the federal trial court for the entire state of [Colorado](/places/colorado/), created by Congress on June 26, 1876, with a single judgeship and now holding seven ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/5706)). Its decisions are appealed to the Tenth Circuit, and its Denver courtroom hosted the 1997 trial of Oklahoma City bomber Timothy McVeigh. In 2018 it also ruled on online lending, sending Colorado's case against [Avant](/organizations/avant/) back to state court.

Congress organized Colorado as one judicial district on June 26, 1876, with one judgeship in the Eighth Circuit, effective on statehood, August 1, 1876 ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/5706)). The court is the state's only federal judicial district, covering all of Colorado, and its first judge, Moses Hallett, served from 1877 to 1906 ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/history/courts/us-district-court-district-colorado-succession-chart)).

## From one judgeship to seven

For nearly eighty years the court ran on a single judgeship; Congress added seats in 1954, 1961, 1970, two in 1978, and one in 1984 — six additions in thirty years, bringing the total to seven, where it has stayed ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/5706)). No new judgeship has been authorized since 1984 ([Tenth Circuit Historical Society](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54170cd0e4b00eba52a2db00/t/5b6c828040ec9a40bde51dd4/1533837955683/District)).

On February 28, 1929, Congress moved the district to the newly organized Tenth Circuit, where its appeals still go — except patent and larger Tucker Act claims against the [United States](/places/united-states/), which go to the Federal Circuit ([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/node/5706); [Tenth Circuit Historical Society](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54170cd0e4b00eba52a2db00/t/5b6c9824758d4683cab44b55/1533843495643/District+of+Colorado+1990-2008+Erickson.pdf)).

As of the court's 2017 history, the district served more than 5.5 million people across more than 104,000 square miles — the largest federal district without judicial subdivisions other than Alaska, though it uses jury divisions administratively ([Tenth Circuit Historical Society](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54170cd0e4b00eba52a2db00/t/5b6c828040ec9a40bde51dd4/1533837955683/District)). Its docket spans Front Range cities, Eastern Plains land use, and Western Slope public-lands cases ([Tenth Circuit Historical Society](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54170cd0e4b00eba52a2db00/t/5b6c828040ec9a40bde51dd4/1533837955683/District)). The same history ranks it among the busiest federal districts in the country — top 15% by caseload per judgeship — and records calls for more judgeships given the district's size, federal lands, and Denver-headquartered agencies ([Tenth Circuit Historical Society](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54170cd0e4b00eba52a2db00/t/5b6c828040ec9a40bde51dd4/1533837955683/District)).

The bench today is seven active district judges — Regina M. Rodriguez (listed as chief on the court's roster), Philip A. Brimmer, Daniel D. Domenico, Charlotte N. Sweeney, Nina Y. Wang, Gordon P. Gallagher, and S. Kato Crews — plus senior judges and nine magistrate judges, including Scott T. Varholak, author of the recommendation adopted in [*Meade v. Avant*](/events/meade-v-avant/) ([U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado](http://www.cod.uscourts.gov/JudicialOfficers.aspx)).

## A courthouse built on a backlog plan

Chief Judge Alfred A. Arraj led the court from 1959 to 1976, taking over with more than 300 pending cases — one of the most intense backlogs in state history ([Law Week Colorado](https://www.lawweekcolorado.com/article/alfred-arrajs-long-lasting-impacts-on-the-district-court/)). His 1966 plan — an intense trial calendar plus out-of-state judges — cleared the backlog and was copied by other districts; among more than 90 federal courts, the District of Colorado "was routinely near the top of the list in efficient use of jury time and in providing prompt trials in both civil and criminal cases," according to the court's website ([Law Week Colorado](https://www.lawweekcolorado.com/article/alfred-arrajs-long-lasting-impacts-on-the-district-court/)). Arraj served 35 years, working daily after taking senior status in 1976 until two weeks before his death in October 1992, at 86; eight years later Congress named Denver's new courthouse after him ([Law Week Colorado](https://www.lawweekcolorado.com/article/alfred-arrajs-long-lasting-impacts-on-the-district-court/)).

The Alfred A. Arraj U.S. Courthouse at 19th and Champa Streets in Denver cost $85 million, was completed in October 2002, and holds ten district-judge courtrooms, four magistrate-judge courtrooms, and a special-proceedings courtroom ([Tenth Circuit Historical Society](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54170cd0e4b00eba52a2db00/t/5b6c9824758d4683cab44b55/1533843495643/District+of+Colorado+1990-2008+Erickson.pdf)). It replaced the Byron G. Rogers courthouse a block away, the former primary courthouse and site of several high-profile trials, including McVeigh's ([Tenth Circuit Historical Society](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54170cd0e4b00eba52a2db00/t/5b6c9824758d4683cab44b55/1533843495643/District+of+Colorado+1990-2008+Erickson.pdf)). The court also sits in Grand Junction, at the Wayne N. Aspinall Federal Building — a 1918 former post office on the National Register — and in Durango ([Tenth Circuit Historical Society](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54170cd0e4b00eba52a2db00/t/5b6c9824758d4683cab44b55/1533843495643/District+of+Colorado+1990-2008+Erickson.pdf)). The Byron R. White courthouse, clad in Colorado Yule marble, houses the Tenth Circuit; renamed in 1994 for the Coloradoan who served on the [U.S. Supreme Court](/organizations/us-supreme-court/) from 1962 to 1993 ([Tenth Circuit Historical Society](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/54170cd0e4b00eba52a2db00/t/5b6c9824758d4683cab44b55/1533843495643/District+of+Colorado+1990-2008+Erickson.pdf)).

Arraj's rulings left a mark as well. In February 1971 he ordered student-newspaper editor Dorothy Trujillo reinstated with back pay after her firing over a "controversial" editorial, writing that "the state is not necessarily the unfettered master of all it creates" ([Law Week Colorado](https://www.lawweekcolorado.com/article/alfred-arrajs-long-lasting-impacts-on-the-district-court/)). In 1977 he decided the Blue River water case after 28 years of litigation, ruling Denver had to release Green Mountain Reservoir water to the Western Slope; an appeal was denied two years later ([Law Week Colorado](https://www.lawweekcolorado.com/article/alfred-arrajs-long-lasting-impacts-on-the-district-court/)).

## Notable cases: McVeigh, Nacchio, and Meade v. Avant

On April 19, 1995, a truck bomb destroyed the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building in Oklahoma City, killing 168 people ([Tenth Circuit](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/153/1166/619745/)). After the Oklahoma trial judge was recused, the Tenth Circuit's chief judge designated Chief Judge Richard P. Matsch of the District of Colorado to preside, and on February 19, 1996, the court transferred the case to Denver ([Tenth Circuit](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/153/1166/619745/)). McVeigh's trial began with voir dire on March 31, 1997; on June 2, 1997, the jury convicted him on all eleven counts, and on August 14, 1997, the court sentenced him to death ([Tenth Circuit](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/153/1166/619745/)). The Tenth Circuit affirmed on September 8, 1998 ([Tenth Circuit](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/153/1166/619745/)).

A decade later came the insider-trading prosecution of Joseph Nacchio, former chief executive of Denver-based Qwest Communications. A Denver jury convicted Nacchio of nineteen counts of insider trading and acquitted him on twenty-three, over stock sales in April and May 2001 while Qwest's internal revenue outlook was deteriorating ([Tenth Circuit](https://storage.courtlistener.com/pdf/2008/03/17/united_states_v._nacchio.pdf)). Judge Edward Nottingham presided; Nacchio drew six years in prison, a $19 million fine, and a $52 million forfeiture ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/technology/qwests-ex-chief-nacchio-loses-appeal-10466164)). In March 2008 a Tenth Circuit panel reversed the conviction and ordered a new trial over the exclusion of defense expert testimony — but in February 2009 the full court, sitting en banc, voted 5–4 to uphold it ([Tenth Circuit](https://storage.courtlistener.com/pdf/2008/03/17/united_states_v._nacchio.pdf); [TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/technology/qwests-ex-chief-nacchio-loses-appeal-10466164)).

In *Meade v. Avant*, decided March 1, 2018, Julie Ann Meade, the Administrator of the Colorado Uniform Consumer Credit Code, sued Avant in Denver District Court, alleging the online lender charged excess finance and delinquency charges in violation of Colorado law ([U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). Avant, a Colorado supervised lender since 2013, had a 2014 lending-program agreement with [WebBank](/organizations/webbank/), a Utah-chartered industrial bank: WebBank made loans to consumers who applied through Avant's website and, within two business days, sold them to purchasers including Avant ([U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). The Administrator alleged WebBank was not the "true lender": Avant paid the program's costs, serviced the loans, bore all default risk, and kept about 99% of the profits to WebBank's 1% ([U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)).

Avant removed the case, arguing Section 27 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act completely preempted the state claims. Judge William J. Martínez, adopting Magistrate Judge Scott T. Varholak's recommendation after de novo review, rejected that argument: the claims were asserted only against Avant, a non-bank, and Section 27 governs state-chartered banks, not non-bank lenders ([U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf); [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b4560b61-7957-4ba9-baab-abd52db8ad48)). The court remanded the case to the District Court for the City and County of Denver ([U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)).

The ruling decided only complete preemption — the jurisdictional question — leaving open express or conflict preemption, the valid-when-made doctrine, and the "true lender" merits ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b4560b61-7957-4ba9-baab-abd52db8ad48)). Avant's argument that WebBank was the true lender was, the court held, "at best a defense that could not create federal jurisdiction" ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=b4560b61-7957-4ba9-baab-abd52db8ad48)). The court also noted the complaint's allegations suggested "the non-bank is the true lender" and that "Avant is for all practical purposes in control of the Avant loans, and it has indemnified WebBank, whose role was short-lived and is now entirely in the past" ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2018/03/Federal-Court-Sends-Colorados-Case-Against-Online-Lending-Platform-Back-to-State-Court)). The ruling, Orrick stressed, has "no binding impact on the merits of Avant's preemption defense," which remained for state court — and by statute the remand order is not appealable ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2018/03/Federal-Court-Sends-Colorados-Case-Against-Online-Lending-Platform-Back-to-State-Court)). A parallel Marlette suit and declaratory-judgment actions by WebBank and Cross River Bank went to Judge Philip Brimmer, who was not bound by Judge Martínez's approach ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2018/03/Federal-Court-Sends-Colorados-Case-Against-Online-Lending-Platform-Back-to-State-Court)).

## FAQ

**When was the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado created?**
Congress created it on June 26, 1876, with one judgeship, effective on statehood, August 1, 1876. It sat in the Eighth Circuit until moving to the newly organized Tenth Circuit in 1929.

**Where are the court's decisions appealed?**
To the Tenth Circuit, housed in Denver's Byron R. White courthouse. Patent claims and larger Tucker Act claims against the federal government go to the Federal Circuit.

**What did the court decide in Meade v. Avant?**
On March 1, 2018, Judge William J. Martínez held Colorado's claims against the online lender Avant were not completely preempted by federal banking law and remanded the case to Denver District Court. The ruling decided only jurisdiction; the "true lender" question and the preemption merits were left for state court.

**Why was the Oklahoma City bombing trial held in Denver?**
After the Oklahoma trial judge was recused, the Tenth Circuit designated Chief Judge Richard P. Matsch of the District of Colorado to preside, and the court transferred the case to Denver on a change of venue. McVeigh was tried there in 1997, convicted on all eleven counts, and sentenced to death.
