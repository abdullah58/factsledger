## Overview

The Tokyo District Court (東京地方裁判所) is the district court for [Tokyo](/places/tokyo/), [Japan](/places/japan/), based at 1-1-4 Kasumigaseki in Chiyoda ward, and the largest district court in the country. As a court of first instance it hears the civil and criminal cases arising in Tokyo, and its specialised Intellectual Property Divisions hear copyright, trademark and design cases for eastern Japan — a role that made it the venue for one of the most consequential internet-copyright cases ever decided in Japan: the four manga publishers' suit against [Cloudflare](/organizations/cloudflare/). On 19 November 2025 the court held Cloudflare liable for aiding and abetting copyright infringement and ordered it to pay about ¥500 million (about US$3.3 million) in damages ([Tokyo District Court](https://www.courts.go.jp/ip/eng/assets/ip/eng/chizai_en/chizai_en-pdf-3813.pdf)).

District courts are the principal courts of first instance in Japan — there are 50 of them, one in each prefectural capital — and the Court Act of 1947 gives them jurisdiction over first-instance litigation ([Japanese Law Translation](https://www.japaneselawtranslation.go.jp/en/laws/view/3225/en)). The Tokyo court's predecessor, the Tokyo Court, dates to 1871, with its building constructed in 1875 ([J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/aija/65/532/65_KJ00004223705/_article/-char/en)). Its modern form rests on the Court Act (Act No. 59 of 16 April 1947), which lists district courts among the lower courts alongside high courts, family courts and summary courts ([Japanese Law Translation](https://www.japaneselawtranslation.go.jp/en/laws/view/3225/en)).

## Jurisdiction and the intellectual property divisions

The court's IP work is what sets it apart. Patent-infringement suits at district-court level fall under the exclusive jurisdiction of the Tokyo District Court or the Osaka District Court, with Tokyo taking cases where the relevant location is in northern or eastern Japan and Osaka taking the south and west ([WIPO](https://web.patent-judicial-guide.wji.prd.web1.wipo.int/patent-judicial-guide/en/full-guide/japan/7.3.2)). For copyright, trademark, design and unfair-competition cases the two courts instead have overlapping jurisdiction, a arrangement introduced by a 2004 amendment to the Code of Civil Procedure to speed up hearings ([IP High Court](http://www.ip.courts.go.jp/eng/vc-files/eng/file/061207_08_2.pdf)). As of 1 April 2021 the Tokyo District Court had 12 judges in four divisions specialising in IP cases, supported by judicial research officials and technical advisers, and the average IP case took 15.2 months from commencement to final decision in 2021 ([WIPO](https://web.patent-judicial-guide.wji.prd.web1.wipo.int/patent-judicial-guide/en/full-guide/japan/7.3.2)). Appeals from its IP judgments go to the Intellectual Property High Court and then to the Supreme Court; there is no jury in Japanese civil cases ([WIPO](https://web.patent-judicial-guide.wji.prd.web1.wipo.int/patent-judicial-guide/en/full-guide/japan/7.3.2)).

The 2022–2025 manga-piracy case took far longer than that average — three years and nine months from filing to judgment — which reflects both its novelty and its scale: the court had to decide whether a content delivery network (CDN) that merely caches and forwards other people's content could be liable for the piracy it enabled.

## The manga-piracy case: four publishers v. Cloudflare

On 1 February 2022, [Shogakukan](/organizations/shogakukan/), [Kadokawa](/organizations/kadokawa/), [Kodansha](/organizations/kodansha/) and [Shueisha](/organizations/shueisha/) sued Cloudflare, Inc. — headquartered in [San Francisco](/places/san-francisco/), [California](/places/california/) — in the Tokyo District Court, seeking an injunction and damages over two massive manga-piracy sites ([Shogakukan](https://www.shogakukan.co.jp/storage/files/article/2021/20220201e.pdf)). The sites posted more than 4,000 manga works without permission and at their peak drew over 300 million monthly visits combined, according to a survey by Authorized Books of Japan ([Shueisha](https://www.shueisha.co.jp/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/notice20251119eng.pdf)). The judgment itself records the scale precisely: the two sites distributed 4,082 titles comprising 123,631 episodes, free of charge, to users including users in Japan ([Tokyo District Court](https://www.courts.go.jp/ip/eng/assets/ip/eng/chizai_en/chizai_en-pdf-3813.pdf)). The publishers had sent Cloudflare copyright-infringement notices under the US Digital Millennium Copyright Act — Shogakukan on 7 April 2020, Shueisha on 7 December 2020, and Kadokawa and Kodansha on 12 November 2021 — and Cloudflare kept providing its CDN service afterwards ([Tokyo District Court](https://www.courts.go.jp/ip/eng/assets/ip/eng/chizai_en/chizai_en-pdf-3813.pdf)).

The case, 2022 (Wa) No. 2388, was heard by one of the court's Intellectual Property Divisions, with oral argument concluding on 6 August 2025 ([Tokyo District Court](https://www.courts.go.jp/ip/eng/assets/ip/eng/chizai_en/chizai_en-pdf-3813.pdf)). The judgment, rendered on 19 November 2025, split the publishers' case in two. Their primary claim — that Cloudflare itself was the entity responsible for the automatic public transmission of the manga — was dismissed: following the Supreme Court's Maneki TV precedent, the court held that the piracy-site operators who recorded the works on the origin server were the transmitters, not the CDN ([Monolith Law Office](https://monolith.law/en/it/cloudflare-piracy-case-liability)). But their alternative claim succeeded. The court found that Cloudflare's failure to take timely and appropriate action after receiving infringement notices, and its negligent continuation of pirated-content distribution, constituted aiding and abetting copyright infringement under Article 719(2) of the Civil Code — giving weight to the fact that Cloudflare had enabled a massive piracy site to operate "under circumstances where strong anonymity was secured" without conducting identity-verification procedures ([Shueisha](https://www.shueisha.co.jp/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/notice20251119eng.pdf)). The court also rejected Cloudflare's exemption claim under the Information Distribution Platform Act and its argument that cached distribution was merely incidental computer use under Copyright Act Article 47-4 ([Monolith Law Office](https://monolith.law/en/it/cloudflare-piracy-case-liability)).

The figures in the case need careful reading, because three different totals circulate. At filing, the publishers put total damages for the four litigated works (one per company) at about ¥5.6 billion and sought about ¥115 million per company as part of that sum — about ¥460 million in total ([Shogakukan](https://www.shogakukan.co.jp/storage/files/article/2021/20220201e.pdf); [Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2025-11-19/shueisha-kodansha-shogakukan-kadokawa-win-copyright-suit-against-cloudflare/.231199)). By the time of judgment, the amount each publisher claimed had become ¥126.5 million. The court recognised total damages of about ¥3.6 billion (about US$24 million at ¥150 to the dollar) for the four works, but because the publishers had claimed only a portion of what they suffered, the operative order was far smaller ([Shueisha](https://www.shueisha.co.jp/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/notice20251119eng.pdf)).

The operative order was ¥126,500,000 each to Kodansha, Shueisha and Shogakukan, and ¥121,400,928 to Kadokawa — a total of ¥500,900,928 — plus interest at 3% per year from 21 October 2022 until payment, with most litigation costs borne by the defendant ([Tokyo District Court](https://www.courts.go.jp/ip/eng/assets/ip/eng/chizai_en/chizai_en-pdf-3813.pdf)). The judgment allowed 30 days' additional time to file an appeal, and a legal analysis published in April 2026 states that the defendant has appealed, with hearings at the Intellectual Property High Court drawing attention ([Monolith Law Office](https://monolith.law/en/it/cloudflare-piracy-case-liability)). The publishers described the decision as an important step toward proper use of CDN services, while the ruling's practical lesson for infrastructure providers is that ignoring infringement notices and skipping identity verification can now carry liability in Japan ([Shueisha](https://www.shueisha.co.jp/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/notice20251119eng.pdf)).

The suit did not come out of nowhere. The publishers had asked Cloudflare in August 2018 to stop hosting content for several piracy sites, reached a judicial settlement in June 2019 under which Cloudflare agreed to stop caching content on its Japanese servers if the court deemed the sites infringing, and sued the operators of the Hoshi no Romi site in New York in September 2019; a separate publisher, Takeshobo, sued Cloudflare in the Tokyo District Court in January 2020 ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2025-11-19/shueisha-kodansha-shogakukan-kadokawa-win-copyright-suit-against-cloudflare/.231199)). The backdrop was enormous: Authorized Books of Japan estimated the retail value of manga illegally read on the top ten piracy sites at over ¥1 trillion in 2021 alone — a retail-value conversion based on accesses and time spent, not a calculation of lost profit ([Shogakukan](https://www.shogakukan.co.jp/storage/files/article/2021/20220201e.pdf)).

## FAQ

**What is the Tokyo District Court?**
It is the district court for Tokyo, Japan, based in Kasumigaseki, Chiyoda ward — the largest district court in the country and a court of first instance for civil and criminal cases arising in Tokyo. Its Intellectual Property Divisions hear copyright, trademark, design and patent cases for eastern Japan.

**What did the Tokyo District Court decide in the Cloudflare manga case?**
On 19 November 2025 it held that Cloudflare's failure to act on infringement notices and its continued CDN service to two manga-piracy sites amounted to aiding and abetting copyright infringement, and ordered about ¥500 million in damages to four publishers.

**How much did Cloudflare have to pay, and to whom?**
¥126,500,000 each to Kodansha, Shueisha and Shogakukan, and ¥121,400,928 to Kadokawa — ¥500,900,928 in total — plus 3% annual interest from 21 October 2022. The court recognised about ¥3.6 billion in total damages but ordered only the portion the publishers had claimed.

**Why was Cloudflare liable if it did not upload the manga itself?**
The court dismissed the claim that Cloudflare was the direct transmitter, but found it liable as an aider and abettor: its caching and reverse-proxy service substantially supported the piracy sites, concealed the origin servers, and operated without identity verification, and it kept serving the sites after receiving infringement notices.

**Has the judgment been appealed?**
A legal analysis published in April 2026 states that the defendant has appealed and that hearings at the Intellectual Property High Court are awaited. The outcome of the appeal was not verified from a fully-read source for this profile.
