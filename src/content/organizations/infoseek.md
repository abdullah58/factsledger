## Overview

Infoseek was an American internet search engine founded in Sunnyvale, California, in 1994 by entrepreneur Steve Kirsch, with Michael Agostino as co-founder ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2012/12/18/search-engines-we-have-known-before-google-crushed-them/347983?page=4)). It was one of the first search engines of the commercial web, and it pioneered two practices that later became standard across the industry: it was the first engine to sell advertising on a cost-per-thousand-views (CPM) basis, and the first web firm to target users by their online behaviour, using its Ultramatch algorithms ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2012/12/18/search-engines-we-have-known-before-google-crushed-them/347983?page=4)). Within a couple of years of launch it was drawing millions of visitors a month, and in 1996 it listed on Nasdaq under the ticker SEEK ([HPCwire](https://www.hpcwire.com/1996/07/19/greed-haste-and-public-offerings-effects-on-hpc/)). Its corporate life ended in 1999, when The Walt Disney Company acquired the remaining stock and folded Infoseek's technology into the Go.com portal — but its engineering lineage lived on: staff engineer [Robin Li](/people/robin-li/) left Infoseek in 1999 and co-founded [Baidu](/organizations/baidu/), [China](/places/china/)'s dominant search engine ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)).

## A search pioneer that sold the web's first CPM ads

Infoseek launched in January 1994, when the commercial web was barely a year old, and it arrived with a different business model from its rivals. In its early form it also offered a free web-hosting package with no advertising and no limit on file storage — an unusually generous proposition for the time ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2012/12/18/search-engines-we-have-known-before-google-crushed-them/347983?page=4)). Its lasting commercial contribution was advertising: Infoseek was the first search engine to price ads by cost per thousand page views, the CPM model that would become the default unit of web advertising, and its Ultramatch system was the first to target advertising based on a user's browsing behaviour ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2012/12/18/search-engines-we-have-known-before-google-crushed-them/347983?page=4)). Those two choices — charging by impressions and targeting by behaviour — are the direct ancestors of how search advertising works today, including at [Google](/organizations/google/) and [Microsoft](/organizations/microsoft/).

The company grew fast enough to join the 1996 wave of web-service flotations. HPCwire, writing in July 1996, counted Infoseek and Excite among the web services that had "arrived late for the IPO party" and were trading at about half their offer prices within weeks ([HPCwire](https://www.hpcwire.com/1996/07/19/greed-haste-and-public-offerings-effects-on-hpc/)). By July 1998, when chief executive Harry Motro spoke to the Motley Fool, Infoseek's website was typically among the five or six most popular sites on the web ([Motley Fool](https://g.foolcdn.com/foolaudio/transcripts/980708_infoseek.htm)). Early in 1998 it had bought WebChat Broadcasting System, a chat service, as it pushed from pure search toward a broader portal ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2012/12/18/search-engines-we-have-known-before-google-crushed-them/347983?page=4)).

## Disney's 43% stake and the Go Network

Infoseek's defining corporate event was its courtship by Disney. On 18 June 1998 Disney announced an agreement to buy a 43% stake in Infoseek in exchange for $70 million in cash plus its ownership position in Starwave, the web-design company behind ABCNEWS.com and ESPN.com ([InternetNews](https://www.internetnews.com/it-management/disney-to-acquire-stake-in-infoseek/)). Disney would receive 25.8 million Infoseek shares and a minority position on the board, plus warrants that could take it to a majority stake over time, and Infoseek agreed to buy $165 million in advertising on Disney's networks ([InternetNews](https://www.internetnews.com/it-management/disney-to-acquire-stake-in-infoseek/)). Disney chairman Michael Eisner said the deal would create "a new Internet portal service" combining Infoseek's search with Disney's content brands, including ABC.com, Disney.com, ESPN.com and the official league sites of the NFL, NBA and NASCAR ([InternetNews](https://www.internetnews.com/it-management/disney-to-acquire-stake-in-infoseek/)).

The deal closed in stages. In November 1998 shareholders approved Infoseek's acquisition of Starwave for 43% of its outstanding common stock, and Infoseek sold Disney further shares and a warrant in exchange for $70 million in cash and a $139 million promissory note, clearing the way for the combined companies to build a new portal called the Go Network ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/on-prem/1998/11/19/infoseek-gets-go-ahead-for-disney-deal/855706)). The same month exposed Infoseek's vulnerabilities: Netscape, whose Netcenter portal Infoseek powered, sought to renegotiate the deal to make Infoseek less prominent on its pages, and Bell Atlantic — Infoseek's third-largest shareholder, whose BigYellow directory supplied 8.7% of Infoseek's 1997 revenue — moved to sell almost all of its 1.2 million shares ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/on-prem/1998/11/19/infoseek-gets-go-ahead-for-disney-deal/855706)). In 1999 Disney acquired the remaining Infoseek stock it did not own, and Infoseek's technology was merged with Starwave to form the Go.com network ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2012/12/18/search-engines-we-have-known-before-google-crushed-them/347983?page=4)). By 2000 Go.com's visitor numbers were falling, Disney decided to concentrate on entertainment, and in 2001 it ended the Go.com search engine — switching to results bought from GoTo.com — and closed the portal altogether ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2012/12/18/search-engines-we-have-known-before-google-crushed-them/347983?page=4)).

## The engineer who carried Infoseek's ideas to Baidu

Infoseek's most consequential legacy was a hire. In 1997 Robin Li — Li Yanhong — attended a Silicon Valley computer conference to demonstrate the link-analysis ranking algorithms he had developed at IDD Information Services, and William Chang, Infoseek's chief technology officer, hired him as a staff engineer ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). Li worked at Infoseek from July 1997 to December 1999, and one of his achievements there was the picture-search function used by Go.com ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). He grew frustrated with Infoseek's lack of interest in pursuing innovative search techniques, and in 1999 he left, returning to China with Eric Xu to found Baidu in 2000 — taking the ranking ideas he had refined at Infoseek with him ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/robin-li)). The Register put it plainly: "It was an Infoseek engineer Li Yanhong, or Robin Li, who moved to Beijing in 1999 and co-founded China's biggest search engine, Baidu" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2012/12/18/search-engines-we-have-known-before-google-crushed-them/347983?page=4)). After Infoseek went to Disney, Steve Kirsch went on to found Propel Software Corporation, then the spam-filter company Abaca Technology, and later the secure sign-in service OneID ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/software/2012/12/18/search-engines-we-have-known-before-google-crushed-them/347983?page=4)).
