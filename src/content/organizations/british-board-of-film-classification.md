## Overview

**The British Board of Film Classification (BBFC)** is the United Kingdom's film and video classification body — set up in 1912 by the film industry as the British Board of Film Censors, and the statutory authority for classifying video works under the Video Recordings Act 1984 ([The Terrestrial](http://www.theterrestrial.com/uncategorised/bbfc-craig-lapper/)). It is best known internationally for its rare outright refusals: in 2011 it rejected both [*The Human Centipede 2 (Full Sequence)*](https://www.indexoncensorship.org/2011/10/uk-banned-horror-film-gets-18-rating-after-cuts/) — then reversed itself after 32 cuts — and Adam Rehmeier's [The Bunny Game](/creative-works/the-bunny-game/), which it judged "unacceptable to the public" ([Index on Censorship](https://www.indexoncensorship.org/2011/10/uk-banned-horror-film-gets-18-rating-after-cuts/); [Screen Daily](https://www.screendaily.com/distribution/bbfc-bans-adam-rehmeiers-the-bunny-game-/5033304.article)).

## A century of classification

The Board was created in 1912 as an industry-run, independent body intended to bring a degree of uniformity to film classification across the [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/) — a role it has held for more than a century ([The Terrestrial](http://www.theterrestrial.com/uncategorised/bbfc-craig-lapper/)). It did not censor itself in the literal sense: as senior examiner Craig Lapper explained, the BBFC has never implemented cuts itself — it tells the distributor which cuts would secure a given certificate, offers an advice service during post-production, and runs a formal reconsideration process for disputed decisions ([The Terrestrial](http://www.theterrestrial.com/uncategorised/bbfc-craig-lapper/)). The arrival of home video transformed its remit: the Video Recordings Act 1984 made video releases subject to classification, and the Board dropped "Censors" from its name at that point, becoming the British Board of Film Classification ([The Terrestrial](http://www.theterrestrial.com/uncategorised/bbfc-craig-lapper/)). Its modern guiding principles, per Lapper, are that adults should where possible be free to choose their own entertainment while children and vulnerable adults are protected from harmful content ([The Terrestrial](http://www.theterrestrial.com/uncategorised/bbfc-craig-lapper/)).

## 2011: the horror refusals

In June 2011 the BBFC refused to classify Tom Six's *[The Human Centipede 2 (Full Sequence)](/creative-works/the-human-centipede-2/)* for direct-to-video release, warning it posed a "real, as opposed to a fanciful, risk" of harm and could breach the Obscene Publications Act — a refusal the Board initially said cuts could not salvage ([Index on Censorship](https://www.indexoncensorship.org/2011/10/uk-banned-horror-film-gets-18-rating-after-cuts/)). Four months later the distributor's appeal changed everything: 32 cuts later, the Board passed the film with an 18 certificate, with President Sir Quentin Thomas saying the cuts "address all of the concerns raised when the board refused a classification" ([Index on Censorship](https://www.indexoncensorship.org/2011/10/uk-banned-horror-film-gets-18-rating-after-cuts/)). The same month brought a second, less reversible refusal: *The Bunny Game* was rejected outright. The decision was taken by Director David Cooke and the Presidential Team of Sir Quentin Thomas, Alison Hastings and Gerard Lemos, on the grounds that the film's eroticised sexual violence would risk harm under the Video Recordings Act and was "unacceptable to the public" — and that cuts were no viable remedy ([JoBlo](https://www.joblo.com/adam-rehmeiers-the-bunny-game-banned-by-british-board-of-film-classification/amp/); [Screen Daily](https://www.screendaily.com/distribution/bbfc-bans-adam-rehmeiers-the-bunny-game-/5033304.article)). Distributor Trinity, which had acquired the film at Cannes for its Trinity X DVD genre label, called the ban an unwarranted "twisted notoriety" and said it was considering its options ([Screen Daily](https://www.screendaily.com/distribution/bbfc-bans-adam-rehmeiers-the-bunny-game-/5033304.article)).

## How a refusal works

Refusals rest on the Board's published Classification Guidelines — the 2009 edition governed the 2011 cases — which set out its "serious concerns about the portrayal of violence, especially when the violence is sexual or sexualised" and apply a harm test derived from the Video Recordings Act ([BBFC](https://www.bbfc.co.uk/release/the-bunny-game-q29sbgvjdglvbjpwwc00mziymzm)). In *The Bunny Game* decision, the Board wrote that the work's focus was "the unremitting sexual and physical abuse of a helpless woman" combined with "the sadistic and sexual pleasure the man derives from this," and that the emphasis on the woman's nudity tended to eroticise what was shown ([BBFC](https://www.bbfc.co.uk/release/the-bunny-game-q29sbgvjdglvbjpwwc00mziymzm)). The 2011 pair of cases illustrates both the Board's extremes in one year — and the crucial lesson for distributors:

| Work | BBFC decision | Outcome |
| ---- | ------------- | ------- |
| The Human Centipede 2 (Full Sequence) | Refused classification, June 2011 ("real risk" of harm) | Passed 18 after 32 cuts, October 2011 |
| The Bunny Game | Refused a certificate, October 2011 (cuts "very difficult" given pervasive abuse) | No certificate; cannot be legally supplied in the UK |

The fact that an outright June refusal became an October 18 certificate after 32 cuts implies that even the BBFC's most categorical rejections are negotiable through the appeal-and-re-edit route — while the permanent *Bunny Game* refusal shows the Board draws a line where sexualised violence is too pervasive to cut ([Index on Censorship](https://www.indexoncensorship.org/2011/10/uk-banned-horror-film-gets-18-rating-after-cuts/); [JoBlo](https://www.joblo.com/adam-rehmeiers-the-bunny-game-banned-by-british-board-of-film-classification/amp/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the BBFC?**
The British Board of Film Classification is the UK's film and video classification body, founded in 1912 by the film industry as the British Board of Film Censors. It is an independent, non-governmental organisation and the designated authority for classifying video works under the Video Recordings Act 1984.

**Why did the BBFC ban The Bunny Game?**
In October 2011 the Board refused the film a certificate, finding its focus was "the unremitting sexual and physical abuse of a helpless woman" presented in a way that eroticised the violence. It judged that issuing any certificate — even adult-only — would risk harm under the Video Recordings Act and be "unacceptable to the public," and that cuts could not fix it.

**Why did the BBFC reverse its Human Centipede 2 ban?**
After initially refusing the film in June 2011 and saying cuts could not produce a classifiable work, the Board accepted the distributor's appeal and proposed a more extensive series of cuts. The distributor made 32 cuts; President Sir Quentin Thomas then granted the re-edited film an 18 certificate in October 2011.

**Does the BBFC cut films itself?**
No. It never implements cuts itself; it tells the distributor which changes would secure the desired certificate and may suggest alternatives. It also offers an advice service during post-production and a formal reconsideration process for disputed decisions.

**Who decides a BBFC refusal?**
The 2011 Bunny Game refusal was taken by Director David Cooke together with the Presidential Team — President Sir Quentin Thomas and Vice-Presidents Alison Hastings and Gerard Lemos — underscoring that outright refusals are escalated to the Board's top tier, not routine examiner decisions.
