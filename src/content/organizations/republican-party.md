## Overview

Everybody knows the Republican Party's elephant and its nickname "GOP," but both began as something stranger than a mascot. The elephant took hold in 1874 — and it was not a symbol of strength at all. In "The Third-Term Panic," published in *Harper's Weekly*, Thomas Nast, considered the father of the modern political cartoon, drew an elephant labeled "the Republican vote" standing at the edge of a pit, mocking the *New York Herald*'s alarms over President Ulysses Grant's rumored bid for a third term ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/articles/how-did-the-republican-and-democratic-parties-get-their-animal-symbols)). Nast kept drawing the elephant through the 1870s, and by 1880 other cartoonists had adopted it as the party's symbol — though the animal had appeared in Republican imagery earlier, during the Civil War, when soldiers used the expression "seeing the elephant" to mean experiencing combat ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/articles/how-did-the-republican-and-democratic-parties-get-their-animal-symbols)).

And "GOP" did not originally stand for "Grand Old Party." The party was dubbed both the "gallant old party" and the "grand old party" to salute its role in defending the Union during the Civil War, and the three-letter abbreviation itself appeared only in 1884, when newspaperman T.B. Dowden first used it at the end of an article where he had run out of room ([Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/articles/tea-party-gop)).

The Republican Party is one of the two major political parties in the United States — the other being the Democratic Party — founded in 1854 from opposition to slavery's westward expansion ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Republican-Party)). Since 2016 the party has been reshaped around Donald Trump; as of September 2026 it holds the presidency, the Senate, and a narrow House majority ([NPR](https://www.wuot.org/2025-08-22/joe-gruters-a-trump-ally-elected-new-head-of-republican-national-committee)).

## Origins and founding

The party grew out of 1854 antislavery protest meetings against the Kansas-Nebraska Act's extension of slavery into the Kansas and Nebraska territories; its founders were drawn from former Democrats, Whigs, and Free-Soilers ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Republican-Party)). On March 20, 1854, 50 men, three women, and one child met at the Little White Schoolhouse in Ripon, Wisconsin, to protest the act and advocate a new party named "Republican" — the first of many grassroots meetings leading to the party's formal founding, as the U.S. Congress later described it ([Congressional Record](https://www.congress.gov/congressional-record/volume-150/issue-28/senate-section/article/S2309-1)). The schoolhouse was designated a National Historic Landmark on May 30, 1974 ([Congressional Record](https://www.congress.gov/congressional-record/volume-150/issue-28/senate-section/article/S2309-1)).

But the birthplace is not as simple as one schoolhouse. The National Park Service calls the Ripon building "generally regarded as the traditional birthplace" — and documents the long-running debate with Jackson, Michigan, where a large assembly on July 6, 1854, adopted a platform and elected nominees, with a documented claim to the party name and more complete party machinery — Britannica records the party as duly established at this Jackson convention ([National Park Service](https://npgallery.nps.gov/NRHP/GetAsset/088c203b-568a-4a6f-aa30-95d9a7a16c35); [Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Republican-Party)). The NPS record is careful: the party coalesced from disaffected local groups across the Northeast and West, and was the first party formed without an outstanding leader ([National Park Service](https://npgallery.nps.gov/NRHP/GetAsset/088c203b-568a-4a6f-aa30-95d9a7a16c35)).

The first national ticket followed quickly: at the first presidential nominating convention in 1856 — the first [Republican National Convention](/events/republican-national-convention/) — Republicans nominated John C. Frémont, who carried 11 Northern states and nearly two-fifths of the electoral vote ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Republican-Party)). Four years later Abraham Lincoln won the presidency with 18 Northern states, 60 percent of the electoral vote, and 40 percent of the popular vote, and took office in 1861; by his inauguration seven Southern states had seceded, and the Civil War (1861–65) followed ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Republican-Party)). Lincoln's signing of the Emancipation Proclamation — declaring slaves in rebelling states "forever free" — and the Thirteenth Amendment's abolition of slavery are regarded as the party's greatest legacy ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Republican-Party)).

## Platform and ideology

As Britannica's reference summary describes it, Republicans advocate reduced taxes to stimulate the economy and advance individual economic freedom, generally support conservative social policies, oppose extensive government regulation of the economy and government-funded social programs, support states' rights against federal power in most cases, and traditionally favor a strong national defense ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Republican-Party)). That description is a reference summary, and the party's positions have moved a long way — from antislavery to pro-business laissez-faire to modern conservatism and the MAGA movement ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Republican-Party)).

The 2024 platform, adopted by the RNC Platform Committee on July 8, 2024, was explicitly framed as Donald Trump's "America First" agenda and contained 20 headline promises ([Republican National Committee](https://gop.com/press-release/icymi-rnc-platform-committee-adopts-2024-republican-party-platform/)). Its stated priorities included border restrictions and large-scale deportation; lowering inflation; expanding domestic energy production; reducing outsourcing and increasing manufacturing; tax cuts including no tax on tips; speech, religion, and gun-rights protections; military modernization and a national missile defense shield; preserving Social Security and Medicare without changing the retirement age; ending the electric-vehicle mandate and cutting regulations; and election measures such as voter identification, paper ballots, same-day voting, and proof of citizenship ([Republican National Committee](https://gop.com/press-release/icymi-rnc-platform-committee-adopts-2024-republican-party-platform/)). These are stated promises, not enacted results.

## Current standing and leadership (as of September 2026)

The party's national committee, the RNC, has been chaired since August 22, 2025, by Trump ally Joe Gruters, unanimously elected to replace Michael Whatley with Trump's endorsement; NPR reported Trump's control over the party and the RNC was growing stronger through 2025 ([NPR](https://www.wuot.org/2025-08-22/joe-gruters-a-trump-ally-elected-new-head-of-republican-national-committee)). The 2024 election returned Trump to the White House, flipped Senate control to the Republicans — with flips in Montana, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and West Virginia — and left a narrow House majority ([NPR](https://www.wuot.org/2025-08-22/joe-gruters-a-trump-ally-elected-new-head-of-republican-national-committee); [Bloomberg Government](https://about.bgov.com/insights/congress/balance-of-power-in-the-u-s-house-and-senate/)).

As of August 31, 2026, the balance was: House 218 Republican, 214 Democrat, 2 vacancies; Senate 53 Republican, 45 Democrat, and 2 independents caucusing with Democrats ([Bloomberg Government](https://about.bgov.com/insights/congress/balance-of-power-in-the-u-s-house-and-senate/)). Senate Republicans are led by Majority Leader John Thune, elected by his colleagues to lead the 119th Congress, who said the team was united behind Trump's agenda ([Office of Senator John Thune](https://www.thune.senate.gov/public/index.cfm/press-releases?ContentRecord_id=84C2A20A-B5B4-44B6-B646-15993F4B027D)). The RNC's own September 2026 release lists the House leadership as Speaker Mike Johnson (56th Speaker), Majority Leader Steve Scalise, Majority Whip Tom Emmer, and Conference Chair Lisa McClain; Senate Majority Whip John Barrasso; NRSC Chairman Tim Scott; and NRCC Chair Richard Hudson ([Republican National Committee](https://gop.com/press-release/republican-national-committee-announces-keynote-speakers-for-2026-republican-convention/)).

The RNC's September 2026 announcement also set the party's midterm convention for September 9–10 in Dallas, Texas, with Trump keynoting night one and closing night two and Vice President [JD Vance](/people/jd-vance/) keynoting night two — framed around "lower costs, lower taxes, secure borders, and common sense," with Gruters as its public voice ([Republican National Committee](https://gop.com/press-release/republican-national-committee-announces-keynote-speakers-for-2026-republican-convention/)).

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not claim the GOP initials date to the 1870s; the era produced the "gallant old party"/"grand old party" labels, but the three-letter abbreviation first appeared in print in 1884.
- It does not claim Nast invented the elephant as a Republican image; Civil War-era uses — and the soldier slang "seeing the elephant" — came first. It does claim he made it the party's symbol.
- It does not claim Ripon is the undisputed single birthplace of the party; the NPS documents the long-running Ripon-versus-Jackson debate and calls the schoolhouse only "generally regarded as the traditional birthplace."
- It does not claim the party's ideology has stayed constant; Britannica documents large shifts from antislavery to pro-business laissez-faire to modern conservatism and the MAGA movement.
- It does not claim the 2024 platform's promises were all enacted; they are stated promises, not results.
- It does not claim anything about the 2026 midterms' outcome, which had not occurred as of September 30, 2026.
- It does not give the outcomes of the September 9–10, 2026 Dallas convention; only the announcement was covered by a read source.
- It does not give state-level officeholding figures for 2026; those were not verified from a fetched source.
