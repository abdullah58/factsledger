## The night Chuck Lorre joined the Hall of Fame

No standard profile of the Television Academy tells the story of its own
21st Annual Hall of Fame class — and that is where this one begins, because
it is the queue context for the entry: [Chuck Lorre](/people/chuck-lorre/)
was inducted into the Academy's Hall of Fame on March 1, 2012, at the
Beverly Hills Hotel
([Television Academy Interviews](https://interviews.televisionacademy.com/news/tv-academy-announces-21st-annual-hall-of-fame-inductees)).
Two and a Half Men star Jon Cryer hosted the gala, and the presenter for
Lorre was Warner Bros. TV president Peter Roth
([Television Academy Interviews](https://interviews.televisionacademy.com/news/2012-television-academy-hall-of-fame)).

Roth's introduction is the line the night is remembered for: "Years from now,
Chuck Lorre will be in his grave rewriting his own headstone"
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/chuck-lorre-michael-eisner-bunimmurray-inducted-tv-academy-hall-fame-35913/)).
Lorre's own delegation filled about a third of the room's 45 tables —
Ashton Kutcher, Melissa McCarthy, Johnny Galecki, Billy Gardell and Kaley
Cuoco among them — and after vowing to "skip the mean stuff," he reversed
course under loud pleas from his side of the room, called himself a "shit
magnet," and thanked former colleagues he had clashed with, including
Roseanne Barr, Brett Butler and Charlie Sheen
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/chuck-lorre-michael-eisner-bunimmurray-inducted-tv-academy-hall-fame-35913/)).

The class around him showed the Hall of Fame's range. Former ABC and Disney
executive Michael Eisner was inducted — and sparred onstage with his
presenter Garry Marshall over the Happy Days origin story, revealing the
Fonz's original name would have been "Mash"
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/chuck-lorre-michael-eisner-bunimmurray-inducted-tv-academy-hall-fame-35913/)).
Reality pioneers Mary-Ellis Bunim and Jonathan Murray — The Real World, A
Simple Life, Keeping Up with the Kardashians — went in together, with Juliana
Bunim accepting for her late mother Mary-Ellis
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/chuck-lorre-michael-eisner-bunimmurray-inducted-tv-academy-hall-fame-35913/)).
Mario Kreutzberger, better known as Don Francisco, host of Sábado Gigante,
cited "18,000 broadcast hours without missing one single Saturday"; lighting
designer Bill Klages carried credits across the Emmys, Tonys, Grammys, Golden
Globes and the 1984 Olympics; and I Love Lucy cast members Vivian Vance and
William Frawley were inducted posthumously
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/chuck-lorre-michael-eisner-bunimmurray-inducted-tv-academy-hall-fame-35913/)).
The Academy's announcement had also named Sherman Hemsley among the
inductees, though the Academy's own gala report lists eight honorees without
him
([Television Academy Interviews](https://interviews.televisionacademy.com/news/tv-academy-announces-21st-annual-hall-of-fame-inductees))
([Television Academy Interviews](https://interviews.televisionacademy.com/news/2012-television-academy-hall-of-fame)).

## Seven people in a room in 1946

The Academy began with Syd Cassyd, who worked for the Army Signal Corps as a
film editor under then-Col. Frank Capra during World War II. After the war he
moved to Hollywood, edited for Box Office magazine, and worked as a grip at
Paramount Pictures
([Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/bios/syd-cassyd)).
At Paramount he met Klaus Landsberg, known for pioneering live TV news
coverage, and the two worked on an experimental Los Angeles television
station that would become KTLA-TV Channel 5
([Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/bios/syd-cassyd)).
While at KTLA, Cassyd decided television needed an organization where people
could share ideas about the fledgling medium — and he founded the academy
with the seven people who came to the first meeting. By the fifth meeting,
there were 250 members
([Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/bios/syd-cassyd)).

That was 1946. Cassyd became the Academy's fourth president in 1950; in 1991
the Board of Governors created the Syd Cassyd Founder's Award in his honor
and presented the first to him; in 1996 he received a star on the Hollywood
Walk of Fame; he died February 4, 2000, in Los Angeles, at 91
([Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/bios/syd-cassyd)).
In 1947, the well-known radio personality Edgar Bergen — father of Candice
Bergen — agreed to become the organization's first president
([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/January-25/inaugural-emmy-awards-ceremony)).

## The fight over what to call the award

The Academy's own history records a naming debate with more color than the
usual one-line version. When the Emmy Awards were conceived in 1948, founder
Syd Cassyd suggested "Ike," the nickname for the television iconoscope tube.
Pioneer television engineer Harry Lubcke — the future (1949) Academy
president — countered with "Immy," a nickname for the image-orthicon camera
tube instrumental in television's technical development. "Immy" was
feminized as "Emmy" because the statuette, designed by engineer Louis
McManus, who enlisted his wife Dorothy to model for it, depicts the winged
"muse of art uplifting the electron of science"
([Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/sites/default/files/Downloads/67-years-emmy-v1.pdf)).
HISTORY's account agrees on the essentials: Cassyd had originally objected
to the idea of awards at all, arguing the group's goals should be cultural
and educational, but accepted the need for a visible event; after 47 designs
were rejected, the winged woman holding an atom — created by McManus, with
his wife as the model — was selected, and "Emmy" was the feminized "immy,"
shorthand for the image orthicon tube used in TV cameras until the 1960s
([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/January-25/inaugural-emmy-awards-ceremony)).

The first Emmy Awards took place on January 25, 1949, at the Hollywood
Athletic Club. Tickets cost $5.00; the show aired on local station KTSL; there
were fewer than a million television sets in the United States; Walter
O'Keefe was master of ceremonies; six awards were given — and Louis McManus
received a special Emmy for designing the statuette
([Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/sites/default/files/Downloads/67-years-emmy-v1.pdf)).
By 1957, TV Guide was declaring that "the Emmy Awards have finally achieved
the stature of the Oscars," and that "everyone in television feels that the
Emmy is the highest honor in the industry"
([Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/sites/default/files/Downloads/67-years-emmy-v1.pdf)).

## The split, the rebrand, and the reunion question

In 1977 the organization's two coasts parted ways, and the Academy's own
chronicle tells it plainly: "Due to differences between New York and
Hollywood chapters of the Television Academy, and the split of the
organization into two different academies, the Primetime Emmys air in
September. The Hollywood group reverts to its original name — Academy of
Television Arts & Sciences — and the New York group goes by National Academy
of Television Arts & Sciences." Each group took specific award shows: Los
Angeles kept the Primetime Emmys, while NATAS took the Daytime Emmys, News
Documentary and Sports Emmys, plus jurisdiction over 19 chapters around the
U.S. producing local Emmy events
([Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/sites/default/files/Downloads/67-years-emmy-v1.pdf)).

The two bodies did not live happily ever after. In 2026, TheWrap reported
that NATAS and the Television Academy had opened exploratory talks on
reunification, with both boards voting overwhelmingly to evaluate "what a
unified Academy could make possible" — a move that would undo the 1977
split, when tensions between the Los Angeles and New York branches produced
the L.A.-based Academy in charge of the primetime Emmys and the NYC-based
NATAS overseeing news, documentaries, sports, daytime, children's and family
programming, public service and technology
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/awards/natas-atas-tv-academy-reunification-talks/)).
The friction had been real: disputes over digital and broadband content came
to a head in 2007, when an arbitration panel ruled that NATAS's attempts to
launch new awards shows had violated the 1977 agreement, and the two sides
had also clashed over Spanish-language Emmy awards and oversight of the
international Emmys
([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/awards/natas-atas-tv-academy-reunification-talks/)).

Between the split and the reunion talks came the identity overhaul. In 2014,
the branding firm Siegel+Gale rechristened the Academy of Television Arts &
Sciences as the public-facing "Television Academy." The organization was, at
the time, "an important nonprofit trade organization made up of over 18,000
members spanning performers, directors, producers, executives, and more"
([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3027971/how-the-television-academy-got-its-brand-mojo-back)).
Siegel+Gale's Alyson Schonholz put the problem this way: "Everyone knew the
Television Academy as just the Emmys. They were, for most people, a
one-day-a-year brand... We wanted to help them to a 365-day-a-year brand,
and create an identity for them that would allow them to take credit for all
the things they do"
([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3027971/how-the-television-academy-got-its-brand-mojo-back)).
The new logo placed a vertical line — a "portal," an abstract representation
of a screen — beside the wordmark, and the Emmy silhouette was redrawn at a
slight three-quarters angle with a modernized hemline, body, shape and
hairstyle, while the physical statue itself stayed unchanged
([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3027971/how-the-television-academy-got-its-brand-mojo-back)).
The identity rolled out at the 2014 Emmy Awards on August 25
([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3027971/how-the-television-academy-got-its-brand-mojo-back)).

The Academy's headquarters remain at 5220 Lankershim Blvd., North Hollywood,
California
([Television Academy](https://www.televisionacademy.com/bios/syd-cassyd)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not assert the 1955 merger of the Los Angeles academy with
Ed Sullivan's New York academy — that appeared only as a search-snippet lead
and the Academy's own history does not mention it. It does not name a
chairman or CEO, and it states no outcome for the 2026 reunification talks,
which were reported as exploratory with no deadline. It does not resolve why
Sherman Hemsley appeared in the 2012 induction announcement but not in the
Academy's gala report; both are stated as observed. It uses the Academy's
own spelling, Syd Cassyd.
