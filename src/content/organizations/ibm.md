## Overview

International Business Machines Corporation (IBM) is an American technology company founded in 1911 that built the mainframe era, nearly collapsed in the early 1990s, and reinvented itself as a hybrid-cloud, artificial intelligence, and consulting business headquartered in Armonk, New York, with 264,300 employees in more than 175 countries and $67.5 billion in 2025 revenue ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation); [IBM Newsroom](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-01-28-IBM-RELEASES-FOURTH-QUARTER-RESULTS)).

IBM is one of the oldest continuously operating technology companies in the world, and its history reads as a compressed history of computing itself: punch-card tabulators, the first mass-produced computer, the mainframe family that defined enterprise computing, the personal computer it helped standardize and then abandoned, and the cloud and AI business it became ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). It organizes its operations into four business areas — Software, Consulting, Infrastructure, and Financing — with software led by Red Hat and watsonx, its AI platform ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). Its common stock trades on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker symbol IBM ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)).

## From punch cards to System/360

IBM's precursor was incorporated in 1911 as the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company, a consolidation of three smaller companies that made punch-card tabulators and other office products ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). The company assumed its present name in 1924 under the leadership of Thomas Watson, who became general manager in 1914 and gained complete control of the firm by 1924 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). Watson built the floundering company into the leading American manufacturer of punch-card tabulating systems used by governments and private businesses, backed by a highly disciplined and competitive sales force ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)).

In 1933 IBM purchased Electromatic Typewriters and entered the field of electric typewriters, in which it eventually became an industry leader ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). During [World War II](/events/world-war-ii/), IBM helped construct several high-speed electromechanical calculators that became precursors of electronic computers, but it held back from producing electronic data-processing systems until Watson's son, Thomas Watson Jr., became president in 1952 and sponsored an all-out push into the field ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). Introduced in 1954, the IBM 650 became the first mass-produced computer and one of the most widely used systems for business and scientific applications ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)).

The defining bet came on 7 April 1964, when IBM announced System/360: five models spanning a 50-to-1 performance range, plus 40 completely new peripherals, all able to run the same software largely without modification ([Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/1964/)). IBM's initial investment of $5 billion was quickly returned as orders climbed to 1,000 per month within two years, and the release marked the company's transition from discrete transistors to integrated circuits and from punched-card equipment to electronic computer systems ([Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/1964/)). By the 1960s IBM was producing 70% of the world's computers and 80% of those used in the United States ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). The same year, IBM's SABRE reservation system for American Airlines came online, linking 2,000 terminals in 65 cities to a pair of IBM 7090 computers and delivering data on any flight in less than three seconds — an early demonstration of online transaction processing ([Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/1964/)).

## The PC, the near-collapse, and the Gerstner turnaround

IBM's specialty was mainframes, and it did not enter the growing personal computer market until 1981, when it introduced the IBM Personal Computer ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). Built largely from readily available components and accompanied by published technical specifications, the PC encouraged other companies to develop compatible software, peripherals, and machines; the resulting ecosystem made the IBM PC architecture an industry standard and brought [Microsoft](/organizations/microsoft/)'s MS-DOS operating system to a mass market, with more than 750,000 IBM PCs sold within two years ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). But the same design choices that accelerated adoption also enabled competitors to manufacture cheaper "IBM-compatible" computers, weakening IBM's control of the market, and by the 1990s the company had downsized considerably ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)).

The crisis was severe: IBM lost $2.9 billion in 1991 and $5 billion in 1992, and by 1993 its losses surpassed $8 billion while the value of its stock dropped from $42.6 billion to $19.7 billion ([Encyclopedia.com](http://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/lou-gerstner)). John Akers resigned as CEO on 26 January 1993, and the board hired Louis Gerstner — the first outsider ever to hold the position — as chairman and CEO ([Encyclopedia.com](http://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/lou-gerstner)). Gerstner slashed the global workforce from a 1985 high of 406,000 down to 220,000, cut costs by more than $6 billion, and demanded $1 billion be cut from the research and development budget ([Encyclopedia.com](http://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/lou-gerstner)). In 1994 IBM posted a profit of over $3 billion, its first time in the black in a decade, and by 1997 revenues exceeded $78 billion while the stock price had quadrupled ([Encyclopedia.com](http://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/lou-gerstner)).

Gerstner also rebuilt IBM through acquisition, buying Lotus Development Corporation for $3.52 billion in 1995 to expand its software division ([Encyclopedia.com](http://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/lou-gerstner)). The rebirth was symbolized by Deep Blue, which defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a highly publicized six-game rematch in 1997 — the first time a reigning world champion was defeated by a computer under standard tournament conditions ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation); [Encyclopedia.com](http://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/lou-gerstner)). IBM kept shedding commodity hardware: in 2002 it sold its magnetic hard drive business to Hitachi for $2.05 billion, and in May 2005 it sold its personal computer division to Lenovo Group, taking an 18.9% stake in Lenovo, which gained the right to market its PCs under the IBM label through 2010 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)).

## Patents, Watson, and the cloud reinvention

IBM's research output is part of its identity: it led all U.S. companies in annual patent grants for 29 consecutive years, from 1993 through 2021, and was the first company to generate more than 3,000 patents in one year (2001) and later more than 4,000 in one year (2008) ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). Its Zurich laboratory produced two Nobel Prizes in Physics in as many years — Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer in 1986 for inventing the scanning tunneling microscope, and Georg Bednorz and Alex Müller in 1987 for pioneering work on superconductivity in a new class of metal oxides ([The Scientist](https://www.the-scientist.com/freedom-leads-to-fame-for-ibms-lab-in-zurich-63347)).

Beginning in the late 2000s, IBM accelerated its transformation from a computer hardware manufacturer into a company focused on software and technology services ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). In 2011 its Watson system defeated the two greatest champions on the television quiz show *Jeopardy!*, demonstrating advances in artificial intelligence and natural language processing ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). It acquired cloud company SoftLayer in 2013, agreed to sell its x86 server business in 2014, and completed the sale of its semiconductor manufacturing business to GlobalFoundries in 2015 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). In 2018 it agreed to acquire Red Hat, completing the $34 billion purchase the following year and making Red Hat central to its cloud computing strategy ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). To sharpen its focus on software, cloud, and AI, IBM spun off its managed infrastructure services business; the new company, Kyndryl Holdings, began operating independently in 2021 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)). In 2023 the company introduced watsonx, software tools for developing and deploying AI applications for businesses, and in 2025 it expanded its AI and cloud businesses by acquiring infrastructure software company HashiCorp ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)).

The mainframe never went away — it became an AI machine. Unveiled in April 2025, the IBM z17 features a second-generation on-chip AI accelerator built on the IBM Telum II processor and a 32-core IBM Spyre accelerator, and can process 50% more AI inference operations per day than its predecessor, the z16 ([Data Centre Dynamics](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/ibm-deploys-z17-mainframe-at-marist-university-to-support-ai-research-projects/)). IBM Z revenue rose 67% in the fourth quarter of 2025 ([IBM Newsroom](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-01-28-IBM-RELEASES-FOURTH-QUARTER-RESULTS)). For full-year 2025 IBM reported revenue of $67.5 billion, up 8%, and free cash flow of $14.7 billion, with a generative AI book of business of more than $12.5 billion ([IBM Newsroom](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-01-28-IBM-RELEASES-FOURTH-QUARTER-RESULTS)). It has paid consecutive quarterly dividends every year since 1916 ([IBM Newsroom](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-01-28-IBM-RELEASES-FOURTH-QUARTER-RESULTS)).

### The patent paradox: IBM and the Forgent JPEG lawsuits

IBM's patent record has a mirror image that the standard company histories omit: in April 2004, IBM was on the receiving end of a patent-assertion campaign. Forgent Networks filed two patent-infringement lawsuits in U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Texas against 31 companies ranging from [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/) and [Apple](/organizations/apple/) to IBM and [Hewlett-Packard](/organizations/hp/), seeking injunctions and damages over digital image compression technology it said was fundamental to the JPEG format ([eWeek](http://www.eweek.com/enterprise-apps/jpeg-hits-new-patent-infringement-snag)). Forgent held the patent — U.S. Patent 4,698,672 — through its subsidiary [Compression Labs](/organizations/compression-labs/), and had already collected about $90 million in licensing revenue from 30 companies, including a $16 million deal with [Sony](/organizations/sony/) in mid-2002 ([eWeek](http://www.eweek.com/enterprise-apps/jpeg-hits-new-patent-infringement-snag)). The campaign collapsed: in June 2006 a court ruled the patent applied only to video, severely limiting Forgent's claims, and in November 2006 Forgent settled its remaining cases — with Hewlett-Packard, IBM, Microsoft, and Sun among the settling companies — for $8 million, a fraction of what it had sought ([Law.com](https://www.law.com/article/almID/900005551917/); [Techgage](https://techgage.com/news/forgent_settles_jpeg_patent_cases/)). Forgent later renamed itself [Asure Software](/organizations/asure-software/) and reinvented itself as a payroll and HR software company — the full story is told in that profile, which records the same episode from the plaintiff's side.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is IBM?**
IBM — International Business Machines Corporation — is an American technology company founded in 1911 that provides software, consulting, computing infrastructure, cloud computing, and AI products and services for businesses, governments, and other organizations, headquartered in Armonk, New York ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)).

**When was IBM founded, and what was it originally called?**
Its precursor was incorporated in 1911 as the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company, a consolidation of three smaller companies making punch-card tabulators, and it assumed the name International Business Machines in 1924 under Thomas Watson, who became general manager in 1914 and built it into the leading American maker of punch-card systems ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)).

**What does IBM sell today?**
IBM organizes its business into Software (including Red Hat and watsonx), Consulting, Infrastructure (IBM Z mainframes, Power servers, storage), and Financing; in 2025 software was its largest segment at $29.962 billion of $67.5 billion in total revenue ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation); [IBM Newsroom](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-01-28-IBM-RELEASES-FOURTH-QUARTER-RESULTS)).

**Why did IBM sell its PC business?**
IBM sold its personal computer division to Lenovo in May 2005 as part of a shift away from commodity hardware toward services, software, supercomputing, and scientific research — the same shift that saw it sell its hard-drive business to Hitachi in 2002 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/International-Business-Machines-Corporation)).

**Was IBM really sued over the JPEG format?**
Yes. In April 2004 Forgent Networks sued 31 companies, including IBM, Adobe, and Apple, claiming a patent covering JPEG image compression; the case ended in November 2006 with an $8 million settlement after a court limited the patent to video ([eWeek](http://www.eweek.com/enterprise-apps/jpeg-hits-new-patent-infringement-snag); [Law.com](https://www.law.com/article/almID/900005551917/)).
