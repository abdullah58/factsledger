## Overview

Tax Analysts is a nonpartisan, nonprofit publisher of tax news, commentary, and research tools, best known for the [Tax Notes](/organizations/tax-notes/) portfolio and for decades of litigation that forced the [Internal Revenue Service](/organizations/internal-revenue-service/) to disclose its private tax guidance to the public. Founded in 1970 and based in Falls Church, Virginia, it serves more than 150,000 tax professionals with a headquarters staff of almost 200 and correspondents in more than 125 countries.

The organization describes its mission as shedding light on tax policy and administration "through aggressive, unbiased reporting and informed commentary from the leaders in the field" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). Its readers include the people who write, interpret, and enforce tax law in the [United States](/places/united-states/) — the IRS, the U.S. Tax Court, and tax writers in Congress — as well as academics, practitioners, and nonprofits that debate the future of tax law ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). Tax Analysts is a tax publisher, not an adviser: it states plainly that it does not provide tax advice or preparation services ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). It is both a newsroom and a transparency litigant — it reports on tax administration and sues tax authorities to open it up.

## Founding and early years

Tax Analysts was founded in 1970 "to foster an open and informed discussion about taxes" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). Thomas F. Field, a lawyer in Treasury's Office of Tax Legislative Counsel, established the group on Bastille Day, 14 July 1970, founding two organizations — Tax Analysts and Advocates, a charitable and educational group, and Taxation With Representation, a taxpayers' lobby — which consolidated into today's Tax Analysts by 1980; Field served as founder and CEO until 2001 ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-thomas-f-field-speaking-public-interest/2020/07/13/2cktl)). Its first act was a lawsuit: in 1972 it sued the IRS to gain public access to private letter rulings (PLRs) and technical advice memoranda (TAMs), the documents through which the IRS gave legal advice to specific taxpayers and its own field agents ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). The early organization went by the name Tax Analysts and Advocates, and Field was a named plaintiff alongside it in that suit ([Justia](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/505/350/453333/)). Publishing began the same year, with the first federal tax magazine appearing in 1972; the international magazine followed in 1989 and the state magazine in 1991, with daily online news services added between 1987 and 1991 ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

The founding logic was that tax policymaking happened behind closed doors and favored those with inside access. The 1974 court opinion captured the problem in the language of the Freedom of Information Act: information that "creates or provides a way of determining the extent of substantive rights and liabilities constitutes a form of law that cannot be withheld from the public," and FOIA "condemns 'secret law' and requires that it be made public" ([Justia](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/505/350/453333/)). From a tiny nonprofit operating on a shoestring budget, the organization grew into a global publisher — a transition marked in 2006, when it moved into a new headquarters building in Falls Church, Virginia ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

## What Tax Analysts publishes and sells

The Tax Notes portfolio is the commercial core: three weekly magazines covering federal, state, and international tax, each paired with daily online news services. Around that core, Tax Analysts sells research tools and specialized services rather than advice — a distinction it repeats in its own description of the business ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

| Product or service | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Tax Notes Federal, State, and International | Weekly magazines with news and commentary on tax policy, court opinions, legislation, and revenue rulings |
| Tax Notes Research | A federal tax law library of primary sources, including the Internal Revenue Code, regulations, and Treasury decisions |
| Worldwide Tax Treaties | A database of international tax treaties with comparison tools |
| Audit Insight | A research tool built from state tax audit manuals obtained through public-records requests and lawsuits |
| Conferences and forums | Events on timely tax policy and administration issues, including a nationwide panel series co-led with the American Bar Foundation |

The product list above is drawn from the organization's own account and the Blue J announcement, which names Tax Notes Federal, Tax Notes State, Tax Notes International, Tax Notes Research, and Worldwide Tax Treaties as key products ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240118030832/en/Blue-J-and-Tax-Analysts-Strike-Strategic-Agreement-to-Revolutionize-Tax-Research-With-Generative-AI?utm_campaign=Tax%20Notes&utm_content=278948219&utm_medium=social&utm_source=linkedin&hss_channel=lcp-16185175)). Audit Insight, launched on 1 March 2018, is the clearest example of the publishing and litigation sides feeding each other: Tax Analysts requested field audit manuals and audit training manuals from every state, sued when departments refused, and turned the documents it won into a product ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/tax-analysts-receives-illinois-audit-manual-settlement/2020/12/07/2cymf)). Conferences serve the same dual purpose — the organization holds and sponsors events on timely tax issues ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)), and in 2017 it co-led a nationwide series of tax-reform panels with the American Bar Foundation, including one at Boston University School of Law ([Boston University School of Law](https://www.bu.edu/law/2017/11/15/costs-benefits-of-tax-reform/)).

## The transparency lawsuits: fifty years of forcing disclosure

No other part of Tax Analysts' record explains it as well as its docket. The organization has sued the IRS and state revenue departments repeatedly, and the court records — not the organization's own telling — show a pattern of wins, losses, and partial wins that gradually pried open the tax system's working law.

The opening case set the template. In Tax Analysts and Advocates v. IRS, decided by the D.C. Circuit on 19 August 1974, the court affirmed the lower court's order that the IRS disclose two unpublished private letter rulings, holding that letter rulings are "statements of policy and interpretations which have been adopted by the agency" that FOIA requires be made public ([Justia](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/505/350/453333/)). But the outcome was split, not a clean win: the same court held that the eight technical advice memoranda the lower court had also ordered disclosed were "specifically exempted from disclosure by statute" because they dealt directly with information in tax returns, and it modified the lower court's order in part ([Justia](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/505/350/453333/)). The organization's own history records the sequel accurately — "the courts gave us access to PLRs, and Congress soon required the IRS to publicly disclose its TAMs" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

The campaign continued for decades. In 1995 Tax Analysts asked the IRS under FOIA for several categories of unpublished internal memoranda; after intervening legislation and a partial settlement, most categories were released, but Legal Memoranda and certain Technical Assistance memoranda remained in dispute ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/294/71/545645/)). The D.C. Circuit's 2002 decision in that case was again mixed: it affirmed that Legal Memoranda and intradivisional Technical Assistance memoranda were exempt under FOIA's deliberative process privilege, but it also affirmed that five Technical Assistance memoranda reflecting the Office of Chief Counsel's considered legal conclusions had to be released, and it sent the Exemption 7(E) dispute over eight more memoranda back for reassessment ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/294/71/545645/)). The same opinion notes that an earlier Tax Analysts victory had lasting legislative effect: in 1997 the D.C. Circuit held that Field Service Advice memoranda had to be disclosed, and in 1998 Congress codified that holding by amending 26 U.S.C. § 6110 to expressly include Chief Counsel advice among the written determinations open to public inspection ([Justia](https://cases.justia.com/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/06-5136/06-5136a-2011-03-24.pdf?ts=1411133302)).

The most recent chapter in that line came in 2007, when the D.C. Circuit held that the IRS had to disclose emails containing legal advice that Chief Counsel lawyers sent to field personnel, rejecting the agency's "two-hour rule" — a policy of withholding written advice that took less than two hours to prepare ([Justia](https://cases.justia.com/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/06-5136/06-5136a-2011-03-24.pdf?ts=1411133302)). The court concluded that the statute's plain language "mandates that the IRS disclose the e-mails," a holding that turned an internal IRS shortcut into a disclosure obligation ([Justia](https://cases.justia.com/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/06-5136/06-5136a-2011-03-24.pdf?ts=1411133302)).

The same approach moved to the states. After the Illinois Department of Revenue denied Tax Analysts' 2017 public-records request for its audit manuals, the organization sued in December 2018; a court granted summary judgment in its favor in December 2019 and awarded full attorney fees in February 2020, and the department settled on 3 December 2020, handing over 42 chapters of its audit manual plus updates and paying $35,000 ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/tax-analysts-receives-illinois-audit-manual-settlement/2020/12/07/2cymf)). Including Illinois, 40 states and the District of Columbia had provided audit materials or made them publicly available by December 2020 ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/tax-analysts-receives-illinois-audit-manual-settlement/2020/12/07/2cymf)). President and CEO Cara Griffith framed the purpose directly: "Tax Analysts undertakes these lawsuits for the benefit of the public to improve the transparency of federal and state tax systems, and to provide taxpayers with access to a taxing authority's working law" ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/tax-analysts-receives-illinois-audit-manual-settlement/2020/12/07/2cymf)).

## Leadership, scale, and funding

Cara Griffith has led Tax Analysts as president and CEO since 2017 — she held the title at a Boston University tax-reform panel in October 2017, and was still in the role in the 2020 Illinois settlement and the 2024 Blue J announcement ([Boston University School of Law](https://www.bu.edu/law/2017/11/15/costs-benefits-of-tax-reform/)). Her predecessor, Christopher E. Bergin, served as president and publisher from 2001 — when he replaced founder Field — until 2017 ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-analysis/reflections-christopher-e-bergin-shoulders-we-stand/2020/07/24/2c7ml)). She came to the job from the editorial side of the organization, with a background in state and local tax coverage ([Boston University School of Law](https://www.bu.edu/law/2017/11/15/costs-benefits-of-tax-reform/)). The D.C. Circuit has described the organization simply as "a non-profit organization that publishes news and other material on taxation" ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/294/71/545645/)).

The scale figures are the organization's own: a full-time headquarters staff of almost 200, more than 250 domestic and international correspondents, and more than 150,000 tax professionals in law and accounting firms, corporations, government agencies, and academic institutions who rely on its federal, state, and international content every day ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). The correspondents span more than 125 countries ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). That combination — a nonprofit publisher with a correspondent network the size of a wire service — is what lets a Falls Church organization cover tax developments worldwide. What the profile does not state is the organization's revenue: published figures conflict across aggregator summaries and none was verified from a Form 990 read in full.

## The Tax History Project and the Blue J deal

Two projects show the organization's reach beyond daily news. The Tax History Project, its archive of the history of American taxation, has compiled an online collection of tax returns publicly released by presidential candidates — the first place FactCheck.org says it turns with questions about presidential tax returns ([FactCheck.org](https://www.factcheck.org/2016/09/kaine-muffs-trump-nixon-comparison/?ref=factiverse.ghost.io)). The archive includes Richard Nixon's returns from 1969 to 1972, released near the end of his presidency in 1973 amid speculation about tax improprieties; the resulting investigation found Nixon owed $476,431 in back taxes and interest, according to Tax Analysts historian Joseph J. Thorndike ([FactCheck.org](https://www.factcheck.org/2016/09/kaine-muffs-trump-nixon-comparison/?ref=factiverse.ghost.io)).

The second is commercial and current. In January 2024 Tax Analysts agreed to license its content to [Blue J](/organizations/blue-j/) for Ask Blue J, a generative-AI platform for tax research launched in spring 2023 ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240118030832/en/Blue-J-and-Tax-Analysts-Strike-Strategic-Agreement-to-Revolutionize-Tax-Research-With-Generative-AI?utm_campaign=Tax%20Notes&utm_content=278948219&utm_medium=social&utm_source=linkedin&hss_channel=lcp-16185175)). Griffith said the organization's "news and commentary are unmatched in the marketplace, and our primary law is very well organized," and that its content would "enhance the ability of Ask Blue J to provide comprehensive answers to tax questions" ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20240118030832/en/Blue-J-and-Tax-Analysts-Strike-Strategic-Agreement-to-Revolutionize-Tax-Research-With-Generative-AI?utm_campaign=Tax%20Notes&utm_content=278948219&utm_medium=social&utm_source=linkedin&hss_channel=lcp-16185175)). The deal matters for what it signals: a fifty-year-old nonprofit publisher's archive has become training and retrieval material for AI tax research, and the organization is licensing it rather than giving it away. The [U.S. Department of the Treasury](/organizations/us-department-of-the-treasury/) connection runs through the work itself — proposed revenue rulings pass through Treasury approval before publication, a process Tax Analysts' litigation has repeatedly forced into the open ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/294/71/545645/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Tax Analysts?** Tax Analysts is a nonpartisan, nonprofit publisher of tax news, commentary, and research tools under the Tax Notes brand, founded in 1970 and based in Falls Church, Virginia ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)). It serves more than 150,000 tax professionals and is distinct from the Tax Notes publications themselves, which are its products ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).

**Is Tax Analysts a nonprofit?** Yes. It describes itself as a non-partisan, nonprofit publisher, the D.C. Circuit has described it as "a non-profit organization that publishes news and other material on taxation," and it points to its Forms 990 on GuideStar ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts); [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/294/71/545645/)).

**Who leads Tax Analysts?** Cara Griffith, its president and CEO since 2017, when she held the title at a Boston University tax-reform panel that October ([Boston University School of Law](https://www.bu.edu/law/2017/11/15/costs-benefits-of-tax-reform/)). She previously worked in state and local tax coverage at the organization ([Boston University School of Law](https://www.bu.edu/law/2017/11/15/costs-benefits-of-tax-reform/)).

**What did Tax Analysts win from the IRS in court?** Its signature wins forced disclosure of private letter rulings (1974), Field Service Advice memoranda (1997, then codified by Congress in 1998), and Chief Counsel email advice (2007), plus state audit manuals — 40 states and the District of Columbia by December 2020 ([Justia](http://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/505/350/453333/); [Justia](https://cases.justia.com/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/06-5136/06-5136a-2011-03-24.pdf?ts=1411133302); [Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/featured-news/tax-analysts-receives-illinois-audit-manual-settlement/2020/12/07/2cymf)).

**Does Tax Analysts give tax advice?** No. It states that it is a tax publisher and does not provide tax advice or preparation services ([Tax Notes](https://www.taxnotes.com/document/about-tax-notes?tab=Tax+Analysts)).
