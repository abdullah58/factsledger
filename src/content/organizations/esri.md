## Overview

Esri is the company that invented the digital map — and then spent five decades in near-obscurity while becoming one of the most improbable powerhouses in tech. Founded in 1969 as the Environmental Systems Research Institute by Jack and Laura Dangermond in Redlands, California, it began as a land-use-planning research group before shipping its first software product, ARC/INFO, in 1982 ([eDesiderata](https://edesiderata.crl.edu/providers/esri), [Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)). Today its ArcGIS software owns more than half of the global GIS (geographic information systems) market, used by some 350,000 businesses, government agencies, and NGOs that collectively create 150 million new maps every day ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)).

## The original unicorn — without the unicorn playbook

Esri reached the billion-dollar club without doing a single thing the modern way. Jack Dangermond never took outside financing; other than a $5,000 loan from his mother in the early days, the company never borrowed money — and it has been profitable since day one ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)). "Venture capital can be attractive, but it comes at an enormous cost," Dangermond told [Forbes](/organizations/forbes/). "You have to buy into someone else's vision."

The Dangermonds rebuffed acquisition offers over the years, stayed private, and avoided the stock market's short-term pressures — and the company, while it has let people go, never carried out cost-cutting layoffs ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)). By 2014 Esri had $1.1 billion in sales, with Forbes estimating its value at $3 billion — a valuation built on retained earnings, not fundraising rounds ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)). The culture matches the ownership: employees are paid hourly, and even Dangermond pays for his own meals in the cafeteria ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)).

## Surviving Google

The maps everyone knows belong to Google — and Google was supposed to kill Esri. As Google cemented its dominance in consumer maps, it became what many thought was Esri's biggest existential threat ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)). Instead, the split was clean: Google aimed at consumers, while Esri held its base among power users in business and government. "He kind of created the industry," said John Hanke, who led Google's mapping efforts for six years — "products like Google Earth, Google Maps and Google Street View were built on the shoulders of what he created" ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)).

The power-user moat is concrete: UPS used Esri's maps to make routing more efficient, saving over $300 million a year; Walgreens uses the technology to site new stores and track the flu; and the Gates Foundation has used it in malaria and ebola campaigns in Africa ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)). When Google gave up on enterprise maps, it moved its customers to Esri ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)). Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates put the credit plainly: "One of the areas of technology that has gone further than I ever expected is mapping... And we have Jack Dangermond to thank, in large part, for his pioneering efforts of almost 50 years" ([Forbes India](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/cross-border/the-godfather-of-digital-cartography/42843/1)).
