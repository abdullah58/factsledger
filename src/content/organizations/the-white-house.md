## Overview

The [White House](/places/white-house/) is the official residence of the president of the United States — "the President's private home," in the words of the White House's own archived history — and the president's workplace, a complex that includes the West Wing offices. It stands at 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue in Washington, D.C., a site chosen by George Washington together with city planner Pierre L'Enfant, and has been a symbol of the presidency for more than two centuries ([Bush White House archive](http://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/history/facts.html)).

## Building a presidency's home

In 1792 a competition to find a builder for the "President's House" drew nine proposals; Irish-born architect James Hoban won a gold medal for his practical and handsome design. The cornerstone was laid that October. Washington oversaw the construction but never lived in the house — President John Adams and his wife Abigail became its first residents in 1800, when it was nearly complete ([Bush White House archive](http://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/history/facts.html)).

## Fire, rebuilding, and the name

The building survived a fire set by the British in 1814 during the War of 1812, and a second fire in the West Wing in 1929 while Herbert Hoover was president. During much of Harry S. Truman's presidency the interior — everything except the third floor — was completely gutted and renovated while the Trumans lived across Pennsylvania Avenue at Blair House; the exterior stone walls are the original ones. The house has been known as the "President's Palace," the "President's House," and the "Executive Mansion" — it was Theodore Roosevelt who officially gave it the name "White House" in 1901 ([Bush White House archive](http://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/history/facts.html)).

Today the Residence holds 132 rooms, 35 bathrooms, and 6 levels, with 412 doors, 147 windows, 28 fireplaces, 8 staircases, and 3 elevators. Thomas Jefferson opened the house for public tours, and it has stayed open except during wartime — per the archive, it is the only private residence of a head of state open to the public free of charge ([Bush White House archive](http://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/history/facts.html)).

## September 2026: the press-credential fight

On September 18, 2026, Donald Trump announced on social media that he was revoking the White House press credentials of [CNN](/organizations/cnn/), MS NOW, and [Politico](/organizations/politico/), accusing their reporters of publishing inaccurate stories and threatening national security ([USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/2026/09/24/trump-white-house-ban-cnn-ms-now-politico/91915510007/)). The outlets sued two days later. On September 24, U.S. District Judge [Timothy Kelly](/people/timothy-kelly/) — appointed by Trump in 2017 — issued a 14-day temporary restraining order requiring the White House to "immediately return, reinstate, and restore" the passes, finding the revocation "likely violated their constitutional due process rights." Kelly said he was bound "full stop" by the 1977 Sherrill v. Knight precedent, under which the White House cannot arbitrarily deny press passes based on the content of reporters' coverage, and he rejected the Justice Department's national-security justification, noting that nothing in the pre-suit record suggested the ban was motivated by it. The department had cited the outlets' stories on missile stockpiles, White House ballroom construction, and RNC convention turnout. Kelly had also ruled in 2018 against Trump suspending a CNN reporter's hard pass ([USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/2026/09/24/trump-white-house-ban-cnn-ms-now-politico/91915510007/), [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/politico-denied-access-white-house-after-judges-order-news-outlet-says-2026-09-24/)).

The restoration itself was chaotic: reporters were initially turned away on the morning of September 24 despite the overnight order, forcing the outlets to seek an emergency hearing, and credentials were only restored around midday. White House press operations director Micah Stopperich filed that reinstatement began at 7:25 a.m. and badges were dropped at the entry gate around 9:55 a.m. The TV press pool suspended its pooled coverage in solidarity with the banned outlets ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/politico-denied-access-white-house-after-judges-order-news-outlet-says-2026-09-24/), [USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/2026/09/24/trump-white-house-ban-cnn-ms-now-politico/91915510007/)).

The fight did not end with restoration. On September 28 the outlets asked the court for a preliminary injunction keeping their passes active until a final decision, writing: "The White House's words and actions leave little doubt that it will immediately reinstitute a complete ban of CNN, MS NOW, and POLITICO if allowed the opportunity." As of September 29 the White House was still blocking CNN from traveling on Air Force One, and Trump had posted "Before" and "After" pictures on Truth Social that appeared to float replacing the main press briefing room with a swimming pool. The outlets' lead attorney, Theodore Boutrous, called Kelly's ruling "a strong ruling vindicating freedom of the press, due process and the rule of law" ([USA Today](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/2026/09/29/cnn-msnow-politico-seek-longer-term-white-house-access/91923628007/), [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/politico-denied-access-white-house-after-judges-order-news-outlet-says-2026-09-24/)).
