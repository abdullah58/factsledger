## Overview

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) is an independent agency of the United States government that insures deposits at American banks and savings associations up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each ownership category. Created in 1933 during the Great Depression, it also supervises banks and takes over failed ones — a role tested most recently by the spring 2023 failures of Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, and First Republic Bank ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)).

The FDIC's stated mission is to maintain stability and public confidence in the nation's financial system, and it pursues that mission through three functions: insuring deposits, examining and supervising financial institutions for safety and soundness and consumer protection, and resolving failed banks ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)). It is headquartered in Washington, DC, with regional and field offices around the country, and it is managed by a Board of Directors of up to five members, including the Comptroller of the Currency and the Director of the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](/organizations/consumer-financial-protection-bureau/), with no more than three from the same political party ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)).

The scale of what it backstops is large: at December 31, 2025, the FDIC insured deposits at 4,336 institutions, with an estimated $10,822 billion in insured deposits and a Deposit Insurance Fund balance of $153.9 billion, a reserve ratio of 1.42 percent ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/quarterly-banking-profile/statistics-glance-historical-trends-fourth-quarter-2025.pdf)). Since deposit insurance began on January 1, 1934, the FDIC states that no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds as a result of a failure ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)).

## From the bank runs of 1933 to a permanent guarantee

The FDIC was created in 1933 in response to the thousands of bank failures of the 1920s and early 1930s, when bank runs forced institutions to suspend operations and depositors lost their savings ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)). Congress had considered some 150 proposals for a national deposit insurance system between 1886 and 1933 before President Franklin Roosevelt signed the Banking Act of 1933 into law on June 16, 1933, creating the corporation ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery)). Insurance began on January 1, 1934, protecting up to $2,500 per depositor — at its start guaranteeing $11 billion in deposits — and Congress raised the limit to $5,000 effective July 1, 1934 ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery)). The first insurance payment went to Lydia Lobsiger on July 5, 1934, restoring her $1,250 in life savings after the Fon du Lac State Bank in East Peoria, Illinois, failed ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery)). The Banking Act of 1935 then made deposit insurance permanent ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery)).

The coverage limit rose in steps with the economy: $10,000 in 1950, $20,000 in 1969, $40,000 in 1974, and $100,000 in 1980, where it stayed for 28 years ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery)). During the 2008 financial crisis, the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act temporarily raised it to $250,000, and the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act made $250,000 permanent on July 21, 2010 ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery)). The limit applies per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category, so a depositor with accounts in different ownership categories can hold more than $250,000 of insured deposits at a single bank ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)).

## Supervision, funding, and the insurance fund

The FDIC is the primary federal regulator of banks chartered by states that do not join the Federal Reserve System, and it serves as backup supervisor for the remaining insured banks and savings associations ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)). It directly supervises and examines over 2,700 banks and savings associations for safety and soundness — 2,738 at the end of 2025 — and also examines banks for compliance with consumer protection laws, including the Community Reinvestment Act, the Truth in Lending Act, and the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com), [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/quarterly-banking-profile/statistics-glance-historical-trends-fourth-quarter-2025.pdf)). Its work extends beyond bank safety: the FDIC's national survey found 4.5 percent of U.S. households lacked a bank or credit union account in 2021, the lowest unbanked rate since the survey began in 2009 ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery)).

The agency receives no Congressional appropriations; it is funded by assessments that banks and savings associations pay for deposit insurance coverage ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)). Those assessments flow into the Deposit Insurance Fund, which is backed by the U.S. Treasury and pays the costs of resolving failed banks ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)). The fund stood at $153.9 billion on December 31, 2025, up $3.7 billion in the fourth quarter on $3.0 billion of assessment revenue and $1.2 billion of interest earned, with a reserve ratio of 1.42 percent — above the 1.35 percent statutory minimum set by Dodd-Frank ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/news/speeches/2026/fdic-quarterly-banking-profile-fourth-quarter-2025)). The FDIC adopted a restoration plan on September 15, 2020, to return the reserve ratio to 1.35 percent, the statutory minimum, by September 2028 as required by law ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/quarterly-banking-profile/fdic-quarterly-2025-volume-19-number-1)). The ratio had previously fallen as low as negative 0.39 percent in 2009, when the fund itself went negative during the financial crisis ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/quarterly-banking-profile/statistics-glance-historical-trends-fourth-quarter-2025.pdf)).

The industry the fund covers keeps consolidating: the number of FDIC-insured institutions fell from 8,833 in 2005 to 4,336 at the end of 2025 ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/quarterly-banking-profile/statistics-glance-historical-trends-fourth-quarter-2025.pdf)). By the FDIC's own count, 60 banks were on its Problem Bank List at the end of 2025 — 1.4 percent of all banks, within the normal non-crisis range of 1 to 2 percent — and no banks failed in the fourth quarter of 2025 ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/news/speeches/2026/fdic-quarterly-banking-profile-fourth-quarter-2025)).

## Resolving failed banks — and the spring of 2023

When a bank fails it does not enter bankruptcy; its chartering authority closes it and the FDIC takes it into receivership, usually selling the deposits and loans to another institution, whose customers the failed bank's depositors automatically become ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html), [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)). Since the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation Improvement Act of 1991, the FDIC must choose the resolution least costly to the Deposit Insurance Fund — a reform adopted after 1980s forbearance and open-bank assistance had inflated failure costs ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)). The statute's only escape from least-cost resolution is the systemic risk exception, which requires a written recommendation by at least two-thirds of both the FDIC Board and the Federal Reserve Board, a determination by the Secretary of the [Treasury](/organizations/us-department-of-the-treasury/) in consultation with the President, documentation of the decision, a Government Accountability Office review, notice to Congress within three days, and a report from the failed bank's regulator — with any losses repaid through a special assessment on banks ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)).

The exception has been invoked six times: five during the 2008–2009 crisis and once in 2023 ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)). In 2008 it underwrote open-bank assistance for [Citigroup](/organizations/citigroup/) in the form of a partial guarantee of $306 billion of assets — a guarantee that never paid out — and a similar $118 billion guarantee was offered to Bank of America but never finalized ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)). The FDIC also initially accepted Citigroup's offer to acquire Wachovia under the exception, then rejected it for a competing offer from [Wells Fargo](/organizations/wells-fargo/) that required no FDIC assistance ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)). The Dodd-Frank Act later limited the exception to receiverships, ruling out its use for broad programs open to healthy banks ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)).

The exception's first use inside a receivership came in March 2023. Silicon Valley Bank was closed on March 10 by California's regulator, and Signature Bank was closed on March 12 by New York's; on March 12 the Treasury, the Federal Reserve, and the FDIC jointly announced that all depositors of both banks would be fully protected, with access to all their money from Monday, March 13, and that no losses would be borne by the taxpayer ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery), [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20230312b.htm)). Shareholders and certain unsecured debtholders were not protected, and senior management was removed ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20230312b.htm)). The two banks held an estimated $231.1 billion in uninsured deposits combined, and the FDIC estimated that guaranteeing them would cost the fund $16.7 billion, to be recovered through a special assessment levied on the 110 banks with over $5 billion in uninsured deposits ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)). The FDIC created a bridge bank for Silicon Valley Bank, then agreed on March 26 to sell all its deposits and loans to First-Citizens Bank & Trust, which bought about $72 billion of assets at a $16.5 billion discount; the FDIC estimated that failure's cost to the fund at about $20 billion ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/index.php/news/press-releases/2023/pr23023.html)). Flagstar Bank assumed Signature Bank's deposits on March 19 ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery)).

Seven weeks later came the contrast that defines the modern rule. On May 1, 2023, California regulators closed First Republic Bank, and the FDIC sold it to [JPMorgan Chase](/organizations/jpmorgan-chase/) in a purchase and assumption agreement covering all deposits and substantially all assets — with no invocation of the systemic risk exception ([ABA Banking Journal](http://bankingjournal.aba.com/2023/05/jpmorgan-to-assume-deposits-assets-of-first-republic-bank/)). First Republic held about $229.1 billion in total assets and $103.9 billion in deposits; JPMorgan assumed $92 billion in deposits, including all uninsured deposits, and acquired $173 billion in loans and $30 billion in securities, with the FDIC estimating the cost to the fund at about $13 billion ([FDIC](http://business.cch.com/BFLD/FDIC-PR-34-2023-05012023.pdf), [ABA Banking Journal](http://bankingjournal.aba.com/2023/05/jpmorgan-to-assume-deposits-assets-of-first-republic-bank/)). The FDIC counts First Republic, Silicon Valley Bank, and Signature Bank as the second, third, and fourth largest bank failures in its history, respectively — behind only Washington Mutual, which failed in 2008 with $307 billion in assets and was acquired by JPMorgan Chase at no cost to the fund ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/90years?source=govdelivery&utm_medium=email&utm_source=govdelivery)). Failed banks held $552.5 billion in assets in 2023, the largest annual total in the FDIC's 2005–2025 historical table ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/quarterly-banking-profile/statistics-glance-historical-trends-fourth-quarter-2025.pdf)).

The 2023 interventions revived the moral-hazard debate the exception was designed to contain: the Congressional Research Service notes that protecting uninsured depositors signals that banks and their large depositors need be less concerned about risk-taking, and that using the exception at two mid-sized banks supports concerns that some institutions are seen as "too big to fail" ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)). Congress responded in part through Section 905 of P.L. 119-101, which requires a failed bank's regulator to report to Congress on its supervision of the bank and expands the Government Accountability Office's review when the exception is invoked ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)).

## What the insurance does not cover

FDIC insurance covers deposits only; it does not insure securities, mutual funds, or other investments that banks and savings associations may offer ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/about/what-we-do?ref=broadbandbreakfast.com)). Amounts above the $250,000 limit are not guaranteed, though uninsured depositors may recover part of their funds from the failed bank's assets — and in the 2023 cases, only the systemic risk exception, not the standard insurance, protected them ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.EveryCRSReport.com/reports/IF12378.html)). The boundary also shapes the fintech era: the coverage attaches to deposits at insured banks, so a financial-technology brand on an account is not itself the insured party. [BlueVine](/organizations/bluevine/), for example, announced its business checking account in October 2019 with banking services provided by Coastal Community Bank, Member FDIC, making accounts eligible for FDIC deposit insurance up to $250,000 ([BlueVine](https://www.bluevine.com/newsroom/introducing-bluevine-business-banking)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not state a single final cost for the 2023 bank failures, because the published estimates measure different things at different dates: the FDIC's March 2023 estimate put Silicon Valley Bank's cost to the fund at about $20 billion, the Congressional Research Service puts the cost of guaranteeing uninsured deposits at SVB and Signature under the systemic risk exception at $16.7 billion, and the FDIC's historical table puts total 2023 estimated fund losses at $19.021 billion. It does not state the FDIC's current chair, whose currency as of November 2026 was not verified. It does not claim the Deposit Insurance Fund figures are current beyond December 31, 2025, and it does not state when the special assessment for the March 2023 failures ends. The 2008–2013 failure count of more than 500 banks and the savings-and-loan crisis figures are the FDIC's own historical totals, not independently recounted here.
