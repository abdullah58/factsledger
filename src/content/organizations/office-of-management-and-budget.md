The Office of Management and Budget opened 2025 with one of the most aggressive acts of its century-long existence — and then, within 72 hours, nearly un-did it. On January 27, OMB issued a memorandum pausing all federal financial assistance that might conflict with the new president's executive orders, effective at 5 p.m. the next day ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). On January 28, a federal court administratively stayed it. On January 29, OMB rescinded it — and the White House press secretary immediately declared that the rescission was "NOT a rescission of the federal funding freeze," prompting a judge to impose a temporary restraining order anyway ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). Generic profiles of OMB describe a budget shop; the verified record of its 2025 role — the legal machinery of the freeze, the rescission-that-wasn't, and the February restraining order on foreign aid — is the part competitors have not covered.

## The century-old budget office

OMB is a component of the Executive Office of the President with a cluster of statutory duties relating to executive-branch operations. As the Congressional Research Service puts it, it also acts on the president's behalf in "preparing the President's annual budget proposal, overseeing executive branch agencies, and helping steer the President's policy actions and agenda" ([CRS via Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS21665)).

The office traces its origin to 1921, when it was established as the Bureau of the Budget within the Treasury Department by the Budget and Accounting Act, 1921 — the first comprehensive national budget system, which called on the Bureau to assemble, correlate, and recommend changes to the executive branch's appropriations requests ([CRS via Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS21665)). Reorganization Plan No. 1 of 1939 transferred the bureau to the newly created Executive Office of the President, and Reorganization Plan No. 2 of 1970 re-designated it as the Office of Management and Budget under President Nixon ([CRS via Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS21665)). Concern about the office's accountability led Congress in 1974 to make the director and deputy director subject to Senate confirmation ([CRS via Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS21665)).

Congress also embedded four statutory offices inside OMB, each created by its own law: the Office of Federal Procurement Policy (1974), which guides government-wide acquisition; the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (Paperwork Reduction Act of 1980), which reviews regulations, information policy, statistical policy, and privacy; the Office of Federal Financial Management (Chief Financial Officers Act of 1990); and the Office of Electronic Government (E-Government Act of 2002), which leads on internet-based technologies ([CRS via Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS21665)). Below leadership, the office is organized around resource management offices — clusters of career program examiners that examine agency budget requests and develop funding recommendations — and OMB-wide support offices including the Budget Review Division and the Legislative Reference Division ([CRS via Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS21665)).

Successive administrations have characterized the office's mission in their own terms. The George W. Bush version emphasized assisting the president in overseeing budget preparation and supervising its administration; the Obama version called OMB the "implementation and enforcement arm of Presidential policy," carried out through five critical processes including budget development and execution, management, regulatory review, legislative clearance, and executive-order clearance; the Biden version restated those five functions ([CRS via Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS21665)). The money is modest for an office of its reach: FY2022 actual obligations of $116 million in salaries and expenses — 79 percent of it compensation and benefits — plus an information-technology oversight account, with about 47 percent of the core account going to the resource management offices ([CRS via Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/RS21665)).

## January 27, 2025: the freeze memorandum

OMB's most consequential recent act began as a two-page memo. On January 27, 2025, the office issued Memorandum M-25-13, titled "Temporary Pause of Agency Grant, Loan, and Other Financial Assistance Programs," directing every federal agency to "temporarily pause all activities related to obligation or disbursement of all Federal financial assistance, and other relevant agency activities that may be implicated by [President Trump's] executive orders, including, but not limited to, financial assistance for foreign aid, nongovernmental organizations, DEI, woke gender ideology, and the green new deal" — effective at 5 p.m. EST on January 28 ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)).

The memo's machinery was detailed. Agencies were to identify and review all federal financial assistance programs against the administration's policies, and each program's oversight was to be assigned to a "senior political appointee" to ensure it conformed to "Administration priorities" ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). Agencies had to review pending notices of funding opportunity for consistency with those priorities and, where the law allowed, modify or withdraw announcements and cancel awards that conflicted with them ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). The memo explicitly echoed 2 C.F.R. § 200.340 — the termination provision of OMB's own Uniform Guidance for federal financial assistance — which permits agencies to terminate an award if it no longer effectuates "program goals or agency priorities" ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)).

A day later, on January 28, OMB issued a clarification memo narrowing the pause: it was "not across the board" but "expressly limited to programs, projects, and activities implicated by the President's Executive Orders, such as ending DEI, the green new deal, and funding nongovernmental organizations that undermine the national interest" ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). It exempted Medicare, Social Security, mandatory programs like Medicaid and SNAP, and funds for small businesses, farmers, Pell grants, Head Start, and rental assistance ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). The executive orders behind the pause included "Unleashing American Energy," which directed agencies to immediately pause disbursement of funds appropriated through the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 and the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, and "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid," which ordered an immediate pause of new obligations and disbursements of development assistance funds pending review ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)).

## The rescission that wasn't

The pause did not survive the week — at least on paper. Lawsuits were filed immediately, and on January 28 the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia issued an "administrative stay" enjoining implementation of M-25-13 with respect to the disbursement of federal funds under all open awards ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). On January 29, OMB rescinded the memo; the rescission said questions about implementing the president's executive orders should go to each agency's general counsel ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)).

Then the White House press secretary declared the rescission meant nothing: it was "NOT a rescission of the federal funding freeze," and the president's executive orders "on federal funding remain in full force and effect, and will be rigorously implemented" ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). Because of that statement, the U.S. District Court judge in Rhode Island said he was inclined to grant the state attorneys general's request for a temporary restraining order despite OMB's rescission ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). A state attorneys general lawsuit had been filed there on January 28, the same day nonprofit and small-business groups challenged the pause in the District of Columbia under the Administrative Procedure Act ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)).

## February 13, 2025: the foreign-aid restraining order

The foreign-aid portion of the freeze then produced the order the queue entry refers to. On February 13, 2025, U.S. District Judge Amir Ali in Washington issued a temporary restraining order lifting the [State Department](https://factsledger.com/organizations/us-department-of-state/)'s blanket suspension of funds to the U.S. Agency for International Development, in a suit brought by the AIDS Vaccine Advocacy Coalition and the Journalism Development Network ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/daily-tax-report/judge-says-trump-administration-didnt-follow-his-funding-order)). In his written ruling, Ali said that unless he lifted the freeze, "the scale of the enormous harm that has already occurred will almost certainly increase" ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/daily-tax-report/judge-says-trump-administration-didnt-follow-his-funding-order)).

The order did not settle the matter. In a later ruling, Ali found that the State Department had "continued their blanket suspension of funds" to USAID despite what he called a "clear" temporary restraining order — the latest instance of the government failing to follow a court directive on spending ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/daily-tax-report/judge-says-trump-administration-didnt-follow-his-funding-order)). He declined, however, to hold the department in contempt as the nonprofits had requested, and he rejected the government's argument that its order contained a loophole permitting the review-era suspension to continue: he said he was "not inviting defendants to continue the suspension while they reviewed contracts and legal authorities to come up with a new, post-hoc rationalization for the en masse suspension" ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/daily-tax-report/judge-says-trump-administration-didnt-follow-his-funding-order)).

## The legal question: impoundment

Underlying the freeze was a constitutional fight over who controls appropriated money. OMB's January 28 clarification memo stated that the pause "is not an impoundment under the Impoundment Control Act," adding that "temporary pauses are a necessary part of program implementation that have been ordered by past presidents to ensure that programs are being executed and funds spent in accordance with a new President's policies and do not constitute impoundments" ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). The framework is the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974: an "impoundment" is any action or inaction by a federal officer that precludes the obligation or expenditure of budget authority, and the Constitution delegates the power of the purse to Congress — the president cannot unilaterally withhold funds from obligation ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)). The Act permits the president to temporarily withhold funds through a "deferral" — but not beyond the end of the fiscal year, and not for policy reasons — and to seek permanent cancellation through a "rescission" proposed in a special message that Congress must enact within 45 calendar days of continuous session, after which any withheld funds must be released ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/-/media/files/insights/alerts/2025/01/gt-alert_omb-memo-pauses-federal-financial-assistance-legal-challenges-and-considerations-for-recipients-and-subrecipients.pdf?rev=eb92a6930c3b44be86e9ff33fe8ad797)).

## Leadership: Vought's return

The office entered this fight under a director with both history and a stated philosophy. On February 6, 2025, the Senate confirmed Russell Vought as OMB director by 53-47, with all 53 Republicans voting in favor — the 13th member of President Trump's second-term cabinet to be confirmed ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/02/10/russell-vought-confirmed-as-director-of-the-office-of-management-and-budget/)). Trump had announced the nomination on November 22, 2024; the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee advanced it 8-7 after a January 15 hearing, and the Senate Budget Committee advanced it 11-0 with Democrats abstaining ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/02/10/russell-vought-confirmed-as-director-of-the-office-of-management-and-budget/)).

Vought had held the office before: deputy director from February 2018 to January 2019, then director confirmed July 20, 2020, leaving office on January 20, 2021 ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/02/10/russell-vought-confirmed-as-director-of-the-office-of-management-and-budget/)). In between, in 2021, he founded the Center for Renewing America, an organization supporting Trump's policies ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/02/10/russell-vought-confirmed-as-director-of-the-office-of-management-and-budget/)). His background runs through the Hill and the Republican budget establishment: a legislative assistant to former Senator Phil Gramm, then policy director for the House Republican Conference and executive director and budget director of the Republican Study Committee, with a B.A. from Wheaton College and a J.D. from George Washington University Law School ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/02/10/russell-vought-confirmed-as-director-of-the-office-of-management-and-budget/)). In the second Trump term, the OMB director holds Cabinet rank ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2025/02/10/russell-vought-confirmed-as-director-of-the-office-of-management-and-budget/)).
