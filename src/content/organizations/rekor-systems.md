## Overview

Rekor Systems, Inc. (Nasdaq: REKR) is a Columbia, Maryland-based company that builds artificial-intelligence vehicle identification and management systems for public safety and transportation markets in the United States, Canada, and internationally ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)). Led by president and CEO Robert A. Berman, its product line includes Rekor One, a roadway intelligence engine; Rekor Scout, a public-safety platform; and OpenALPR, AI-based vehicle recognition software that works through virtually any IP camera, hosted locally or in the cloud ([Security Sales & Integration](https://www.securitysales.com/news/rekor-systems-alpr-nokia-smart-city-offerings/97362/)). The software identifies license plates in real time along with vehicle color, make, model, and body type, drawing on a license-plate database covering 60 countries, and is used for law enforcement, traffic monitoring, public and private security, transportation, and logistics — as well as tolling, car counting, and parking management ([Security Sales & Integration](https://www.securitysales.com/news/rekor-systems-alpr-nokia-smart-city-offerings/97362/)).

## From Novume to Rekor: the OpenALPR bet

Rekor Systems did not start under that name. It was formerly Novume Solutions, Inc., trading on Nasdaq as NVMM, and completed a name change to Rekor Systems, Inc. on April 30, 2019, switching its ticker to REKR — a move that followed its acquisition of the assets of OpenALPR Technology and a deliberate pivot toward automatic license plate recognition and vehicle recognition systems ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)). The company had been pitching ALPR to investors as a major market opportunity since at least 2018 ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)).

The technical premise of OpenALPR was software over hardware: rather than requiring dedicated proprietary cameras, its AI-based recognition could run on virtually any IP camera, hosted locally or in the cloud ([Security Sales & Integration](https://www.securitysales.com/news/rekor-systems-alpr-nokia-smart-city-offerings/97362/)). That pitch won the company a notable early endorsement when Nokia selected Rekor to provide ALPR solutions for its smart-city offerings, to be offered to Nokia's worldwide customer base for law enforcement, traffic monitoring, and public and private security uses ([Security Sales & Integration](https://www.securitysales.com/news/rekor-systems-alpr-nokia-smart-city-offerings/97362/)).

## The UVED gamble and the class action

Rekor's biggest commercial bet was not selling cameras to police departments but a revenue-sharing model with state governments. Under an uninsured vehicle enforcement diversion (UVED) partnership with the State of Oklahoma, Rekor received compensation and commission fees in exchange for using its technology to scan vehicle license plates and compare them against a database to identify vehicles without auto insurance ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)). Management commentary presented the Oklahoma deal as lucrative and as the first stepping-stone to similar deals with other states ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)).

The bet unraveled in public in May 2021. On May 10, a bill authorizing a state UVED program in Texas was excluded from the Texas Legislature's Daily House Calendar and left pending in a state committee — and because that day was the deadline for the bill to move out of committee, news sources reported significant market speculation that it was dead ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)). On a post-market earnings call that same day, Berman indicated that Rekor might not secure a UVED agreement with Texas ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)). Rekor's stock fell $5.20 per share, or 27.5%, to close at $13.71 on May 10, 2021 ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)).

Then, on May 26, 2021, private investor Western Edge published a report titled "Rekor Systems: Lackluster Growth Runway And Exaggerated Insurance Scheme Raise Substantial Downside Risk," which alleged, among other things, that global competitors were "miles ahead" of Rekor in ALPR development, that management's potential revenue guidance could be overstated by up to 80%, that the Oklahoma UVED revenue prospects were significantly overstated, and that municipalities were unlikely to adopt legislation allowing Rekor's ALPR services because of state and local privacy laws, public concerns, and antipathy toward targeting lower-income communities that cannot afford auto insurance ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)). The stock fell $0.44 per share, or 3.93%, to close at $10.77 that day ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)).

A securities class action followed, filed on behalf of those who purchased Rekor securities between April 12, 2019 and May 25, 2021 ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)). According to the law firm's summary, the complaint alleged that Rekor's ALPR technology and UVED business were outclassed by global competitors with dominant market share; that states were unlikely to pass legislation authorizing deals like the Oklahoma UVED partnership because of privacy laws and public concerns; that the UVED partnership was less profitable than presented because of enrollment impediments and partnership costs; and that Rekor had therefore overstated its potential revenues, profitability, and overall ALPR- and UVED-related business prospects ([Kessler Topaz Meltzer & Check](https://www.ktmc.com/new-cases/rekor-systems-inc-rekr-f-k-a-novume-solutions-inc-nvmm/)). These are the complaint's allegations as summarized by the plaintiffs' firm — not court findings.

## License-plate readers in the privacy debate

Rekor's technology sits at the center of the national debate over automated license plate readers. An independent local-news window into how its systems actually operate comes from Greenwich, Connecticut, where the police department operates 13 fixed cameras manufactured by Rekor at town entry and exit points — a "chokepoint model" — alongside 27 vehicle-mounted readers built into Axon in-car camera systems ([Greenwich Beacon](https://greenwichbeacon.com/articles/greenwich-police-chief-explains-plate-reader-safeguards-before-oct-1-muox8l1y)). Police Chief James Heavey has addressed public confusion head-on: Greenwich does not use equipment from Flock Safety, the vendor used by 38% of Connecticut police departments, and the cameras do not identify drivers, use facial recognition, track individuals in real time, or feed a national database ([Greenwich Beacon](https://greenwichbeacon.com/articles/greenwich-police-chief-explains-plate-reader-safeguards-before-oct-1-muox8l1y)).

The department operates under Connecticut's new ALPR law, most provisions of which took effect October 1, 2026 ([Greenwich Beacon](https://greenwichbeacon.com/articles/greenwich-police-chief-explains-plate-reader-safeguards-before-oct-1-muox8l1y)). Under the law, departments must delete plate data after 21 days unless it is part of an active criminal investigation, and the state's Trust Act bars using plate reader data to investigate suspected immigration violations ([Greenwich Beacon](https://greenwichbeacon.com/articles/greenwich-police-chief-explains-plate-reader-safeguards-before-oct-1-muox8l1y)). Heavey said all plate data stays with the Greenwich Police Department — it is not shared with any federal agency and is not held by a third-party vendor — and the law requires quarterly audits, gives residents the right to sue for misuse, and subjects violating officers to criminal charges and permanent decertification ([Greenwich Beacon](https://greenwichbeacon.com/articles/greenwich-police-chief-explains-plate-reader-safeguards-before-oct-1-muox8l1y)). He cited specific cases in which the cameras helped: locating missing dementia patients, helping apprehend a suspect who allegedly attempted to assault a middle schooler, and solving robberies ([Greenwich Beacon](https://greenwichbeacon.com/articles/greenwich-police-chief-explains-plate-reader-safeguards-before-oct-1-muox8l1y)).
