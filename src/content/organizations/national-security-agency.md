## Overview

In April 2025 the National Security Agency lost both of its top leaders in a single day — and then went nearly a year without a Senate-confirmed director. President Donald Trump fired Air Force Gen. Tim Haugh, who also headed U.S. [Cyber Command](https://factsledger.com/organizations/us-cyber-command/), and his civilian deputy Wendy Noble, without the White House or the [Pentagon](https://factsledger.com/organizations/pentagon/) giving a public reason; far-right activist Laura Loomer publicly claimed she had urged the move ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)). Lt. Gen. William Hartman ran the agency in an acting capacity until the Senate confirmed Lt. Gen. Joshua Rudd on March 10, 2026, in a 71–29 vote that also promoted him to four-star general ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-senate-confirms-national-security-agency-director-ending-long-vacancy/)). Beneath that leadership drama sits the agency's permanent brief: the NSA "monitors, collects and processes information and data for global intelligence and counterintelligence purposes," while Cyber Command conducts the Pentagon's offensive and defensive cyber operations ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-senate-confirms-national-security-agency-director-ending-long-vacancy/)).

## The purge and the long vacancy

Senior military leaders were informed on Thursday, April 3, 2025, that Haugh had been fired — with no advance notice about the removal of a four-star general with a 33-year career in intelligence and cyber operations, according to officials who spoke anonymously to discuss personnel decisions ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)). The NSA notified congressional leadership and top national-security lawmakers of the firing late the previous Wednesday but gave no reasons; Noble was reassigned to the office of the defense undersecretary for intelligence ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)).

The firings drew immediate congressional fire. Rep. Jim Himes, the ranking member of the House Intelligence Committee, wrote to Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth demanding an explanation, calling it "a deeply troubling breach of the norms that safeguard our national security apparatus from political pressure and conspiracy theories" ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)). Sen. Jack Reed said Trump had "given a priceless gift to China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea by purging competence from our national security leadership," while Sen. Mark Warner asked how firing Haugh made America safer at a moment of "unprecedented cyber threats" ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)). Warner added a pointed contrast: the administration had fired the "nonpartisan, experienced leader" of the NSA while taking no action against officials who had discussed military strike plans on the commercial Signal messaging app ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)).

Loomer, meanwhile, took credit on X, writing that she had raised concerns with Trump about Haugh's ties to Gen. Mark Milley and the Biden administration and questioning the NSA chief's loyalty — "Given the fact that the NSA is arguably the most powerful intel agency in the world, we cannot allow for a Biden nominee to hold that position," she wrote ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)). The AP noted the timeline did not quite fit her claim: Milley, an Army officer, retired in September 2023, a few months before Haugh took over the NSA in February 2024, and Haugh had been confirmed for the job in a unanimous Senate vote in December 2023 ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)). Haugh's firing also set off a 60-day clock — unless he moved to another three- or four-star job within 60 days, he would automatically revert to two-star rank, and since a new job would require a Trump nomination, he was expected to retire ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)). One curious coda: a month before his dismissal, Haugh had met with Elon Musk, whose Department of Government Efficiency was slashing personnel and budgets across federal agencies; the NSA said the meeting was meant to keep both organizations "aligned" with the new administration's priorities ([Associated Press](https://japannews.yomiuri.co.jp/news-services/ap/20250405-247155/)).

## What the new director promised

Trump nominated Lt. Gen. Joshua Rudd — then the No. 2 at the military's Indo-Pacific Command — for the dual-hat position in December 2025 ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-senate-confirms-national-security-agency-director-ending-long-vacancy/)). Testifying before the Senate Intelligence Committee on January 29, 2026, Rudd told lawmakers he supports Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act, the contentious spying power that lets U.S. agencies gather the communications of foreigners abroad without a court warrant ([Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/01/trump-nominee-lead-nsa-commits-backing-controversial-spying-law/411054/)). Critics argue the authority, which can incidentally sweep up Americans' communications, effectively bypasses Fourth Amendment safeguards; the law had been reauthorized under President Joe Biden two years earlier and was set to expire in April 2026 ([Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/01/trump-nominee-lead-nsa-commits-backing-controversial-spying-law/411054/)). "What I've experienced in my career is that this provides the warfighter, the decision maker, [with] the ability to have critical insight into threats that enables decision making," Rudd said, adding that he knew the law had "saved lives here in the homeland" ([Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/01/trump-nominee-lead-nsa-commits-backing-controversial-spying-law/411054/)). Pressed by Sen. Ron Wyden — a privacy hawk who backs requiring a warrant for searches of 702 data involving U.S. persons — Rudd said a warrant mandate was "a topic that I need to look into," while voicing "supreme confidence that the men and women of the NSA are committed to protecting civil liberties and privacy of American citizens" ([Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/01/trump-nominee-lead-nsa-commits-backing-controversial-spying-law/411054/)).

Rudd also committed to using NSA resources to keep lawmakers informed about foreign threats to U.S. elections: "Any foreign threat to the electoral process should be viewed as a national security concern" ([Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/01/trump-nominee-lead-nsa-commits-backing-controversial-spying-law/411054/)). The pledge landed as the administration was closing or scaling down other election-threat trackers, including the ODNI's Foreign Malign Influence Center and the FBI's Foreign Influence Task Force ([Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/01/trump-nominee-lead-nsa-commits-backing-controversial-spying-law/411054/)). Rudd's résumé is heavy on special operations and joint command in the Indo-Pacific — experience some former officials consider relevant to cyber operations involving Beijing, though others flagged his lack of cybersecurity leadership ([Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/01/trump-nominee-lead-nsa-commits-backing-controversial-spying-law/411054/)).

The Senate confirmed him 71–29 on March 10, 2026. Sen. Tom Cotton, the Intelligence Committee chairman, called Rudd "a war hero with a lifetime of service" and "the right choice to lead the protection of our nation from cyberattacks by Iran, Russia, and China," while criticizing Democrats for moving slowly; Wyden, who had blocked efforts to fast-track the nomination, said in a letter to Trump that he did not consider Rudd qualified ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-senate-confirms-national-security-agency-director-ending-long-vacancy/)). Rudd took over an agency dealing with declining morale and significant workforce cuts driven by the administration's push to shed government spending ([Nextgov/FCW](https://www.nextgov.com/people/2026/01/trump-nominee-lead-nsa-commits-backing-controversial-spying-law/411054/)).

## The public defender: Salt Typhoon

While the leadership fight played out, the NSA was also operating in public as a cyber defender. On August 27, 2025, it joined the FBI, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and the Defense Department's Cyber Crime Center — plus counterparts in the U.K., Canada, Australia, Germany, Italy and Japan — in a joint advisory warning that the Chinese state-backed "Salt Typhoon" hacking campaign had targeted more than 200 U.S. companies across 80 countries ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/08/27/fbi-allies-warn-salt-typhoon-cyber-campaign-targeted-200-us-companies-80-countries/)). The advisory described "one of the most pervasive state-sponsored espionage operations discovered to date," focused on network infrastructure — routers, virtual private networks and other edge devices — where intrusions can bypass conventional endpoint defenses ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/08/27/fbi-allies-warn-salt-typhoon-cyber-campaign-targeted-200-us-companies-80-countries/)).

The campaign was not new: a 2024 wave against U.S. telecommunications companies had exposed sensitive information affecting more than a million users, including senior political figures, and Salt Typhoon was later found to have sat inside a U.S. National Guard network for months, quietly extracting credentials, diagrams and configuration data ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/08/27/fbi-allies-warn-salt-typhoon-cyber-campaign-targeted-200-us-companies-80-countries/)). In an unusual step, the U.S. and its allies publicly named three Chinese companies they said were involved — Sichuan Juxinhe Network Technology, Beijing Huanyu Tianqiong Information Technology and Sichuan Zhixin Ruijie Network Technology — alleging they provided cyber products and services to China's Ministry of State Security and the People's Liberation Army ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/08/27/fbi-allies-warn-salt-typhoon-cyber-campaign-targeted-200-us-companies-80-countries/)). Active since at least 2019 (also tracked as Earth Estrie and Ghost Emperor), the group maintains long-term persistence by compromising network-level devices before collecting intelligence at scale; organizations were told to assume compromise may already have occurred ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/08/27/fbi-allies-warn-salt-typhoon-cyber-campaign-targeted-200-us-companies-80-countries/)).

## Built in secret

The NSA was officially founded on November 4, 1952, when President Harry S. Truman's directive let the Defense Department consolidate its scattered cryptological services — though it traces its lineage to the U.S. Army Signal Intelligence Service ([Defense Media Network](https://www.defensemedianetwork.com/stories/national-security-agency-60th-anniversary-photos/)). Its first director was Lt. Gen. Ralph J. Canine, who served from 1952 to 1956, and its chief cryptologist in 1952 was William F. Friedman, one of the founding figures of American codebreaking ([Defense Media Network](https://www.defensemedianetwork.com/stories/national-security-agency-60th-anniversary-photos/)). Fort Meade was chosen for the headquarters for a Cold War reason: it was far enough from Washington, D.C., to survive a nuclear strike, yet close enough that employees would not have to move their families out of the D.C. area; the nine-story headquarters building opened there in 1963 ([Defense Media Network](https://www.defensemedianetwork.com/stories/national-security-agency-60th-anniversary-photos/)).

The secrecy was the point — the agency's existence was classified for years. The first great public exposure came in October 1975, when Sen. Frank Church of Idaho opened hearings on intelligence abuses, focusing on an agency that was picking up the communications of Americans, including Vietnam War protestors and civil rights leaders ([NPR](https://www.publicradioeast.org/us/2013-07-05/nsas-reach-leads-to-calls-for-updated-eavesdropping-laws)). Church charged that the NSA was violating the law by listening in on Americans' phone calls; what emerged were new congressional intelligence oversight committees and a secret court that would have to issue warrants for the NSA's foreign-intelligence work ([NPR](https://www.publicradioeast.org/us/2013-07-05/nsas-reach-leads-to-calls-for-updated-eavesdropping-laws)).

The leaked material described bulk collection of telephone metadata — the numbers dialed, dates, times and durations, but not names or content — approved by the secret court created after the Church hearings, with the data eventually residing at a massive new NSA facility in Utah ([NPR](https://www.publicradioeast.org/us/2013-07-05/nsas-reach-leads-to-calls-for-updated-eavesdropping-laws)). After September 11, 2001, the Bush administration had gone further, collecting data on email traffic to and from Americans without a court order; Congress was told days after it started, and no court orders were issued for the first two and a half years ([NPR](https://www.publicradioeast.org/us/2013-07-05/nsas-reach-leads-to-calls-for-updated-eavesdropping-laws)).
