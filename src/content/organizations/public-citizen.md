## The dozen complaints nobody answered

In September 2026, Public Citizen sent the Government Accountability Office and the Office of Special Counsel a complaint about a 30-second television ad boosting Donald Trump that carried the disclaimer "Paid for by the U.S. government" ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/white-house-spending-taxpayer-dollars-for-propaganda-purposes/)). The group alleged the ad was conceived, produced, and distributed by White House staff using government resources for partisan purposes, and asked OSC to treat it as a Hatch Act violation by White House staff ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/white-house-spending-taxpayer-dollars-for-propaganda-purposes/)).

The complaint carried a striking claim that leading profiles of Public Citizen never mention: the organization said it had "filed a dozen Hatch Act complaints with OSC over the past year and, to our knowledge, all have been ignored by the agency," and that OSC's non-enforcement had "cleared the way for on-going violations of the Hatch Act" ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/white-house-spending-taxpayer-dollars-for-propaganda-purposes/)).

One of those complaints had targeted the attorney general himself. In August 2026, Public Citizen asked OSC to investigate [Todd Blanche](/people/todd-blanche/) over an August 14 speech at a Long Island campaign rally for Republican gubernatorial candidate [Bruce Blakeman](/people/bruce-blakeman/), attended by police officers ([Government Executive](https://www.govexec.com/oversight/2026/08/attorney-general-violated-hatch-act-ethics-rules-nonprofit-says/415499/)). The group argued Blanche is classified as a "further restricted employee" under the Hatch Act, for whom active participation in partisan campaigns is illegal, and that his appearance alongside Trump constituted an active endorsement of the candidate ([Government Executive](https://www.govexec.com/oversight/2026/08/attorney-general-violated-hatch-act-ethics-rules-nonprofit-says/415499/)).

The Justice Department pushed back hard. A DOJ spokesperson called it "a politically-motivated complaint designed to weaponize the Hatch Act against the Attorney General for his support of law enforcement," said it was "baseless," and said it would not stop DOJ's work with law enforcement partners ([Government Executive](https://www.govexec.com/oversight/2026/08/attorney-general-violated-hatch-act-ethics-rules-nonprofit-says/415499/)). An OSC spokesperson told Government Executive the agency had no comment ([Government Executive](https://www.govexec.com/oversight/2026/08/attorney-general-violated-hatch-act-ethics-rules-nonprofit-says/415499/)).

Public Citizen's own announcement was characteristically blunt. "Blanche's blatantly partisan campaigning on behalf of a Republican candidate is yet another reason why he is unfit to be Attorney General," said co-president Lisa Gilbert ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/news/new-complaint-alleges-blanche-violated-hatch-act/)). Government-affairs lobbyist Craig Holman added that OSC should "immediately investigate this egregious violation of the Hatch Act" ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/news/new-complaint-alleges-blanche-violated-hatch-act/)).

## Born from a car-company settlement

Public Citizen was founded in 1971 by the consumer advocate and lawyer Ralph Nader ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/ralph-nader)). Its seed money came from Nader's courtroom victory over General Motors: after the automaker hired detectives to investigate his personal life in retaliation for his 1965 book *Unsafe at Any Speed*, GM was forced to apologize publicly and settle Nader's lawsuit for $425,000 ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/ralph-nader)). Nader used those proceeds to assemble the team of students and volunteers who exposed consumer abuses and government corruption — the young attorneys who came to be known as "Nader's Raiders" ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/ralph-nader)).

The Raiders' investigations were aggressive and consequential. Their probe of the Federal Trade Commission forced the agency to reorganize, and their exposure of corruption in the Food and Drug Administration and the Interstate Commerce Commission prompted similar reforms ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/ralph-nader)). Nader formally organized the network as the nongovernmental organization Public Citizen in 1971 ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/ralph-nader)). He later resigned from the organization in 1980 and went on to found dozens of other nonprofits ([EBSCO](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/biography/ralph-nader)).

## The litigation machine

The organization's most distinctive institutional feature is its litigating arm. In 1972, Nader and attorney Alan Morrison established the Public Citizen Litigation Group ([George Washington University](http://www.gwu.edu/~magazine/archive/2009_law_summer/feature_pdf/feature_morrison_09lawsum.pdf)). Morrison directed the group for more than 25 years, building it into one of the few public-interest practices with a serious Supreme Court presence ([George Washington University](http://www.gwu.edu/~magazine/archive/2009_law_summer/feature_pdf/feature_morrison_09lawsum.pdf)).

The numbers are unusual for a nonprofit of this size. Testifying before the Senate Committee on Rules and Administration in March 2000, Morrison said he had personally argued 16 times before the Supreme Court and that the Litigation Group as a whole had argued 40 cases, while participating in hundreds of others ([Stanford Law School](http://campaignfinance.law.stanford.edu/2012/09/testimony-of-allan-b-morrison.pdf)). During Morrison's long tenure the group also filed more than 300 Freedom of Information Act lawsuits, using court orders to force the government to release records and improve its procedures for preserving and delivering information to requesters — work its chroniclers describe as among its most important ([Satoegakuen / Omiya Law Review](https://satoegakuen.ac.jp/ols/ols-sc/ols-lawreview/No.4/No.4-morrison1.pdf)).

Public Citizen describes itself as a national nonprofit membership organization with more than 400,000 members and supporters, organized into five divisions and active "in every public forum: Congress, the courts, governmental agencies and the media" ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/wp-content/uploads/corporate-enforcement-public-citizen-report-july-2018.pdf)). Its reports name Congress Watch — its congressional lobbying arm — and the Litigation Group among those divisions ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/wp-content/uploads/corporate-enforcement-public-citizen-report-july-2018.pdf)). The organization represents consumer interests through lobbying, litigation, administrative advocacy, research, and public education, covering consumer rights, product safety, financial regulation, worker safety, health care, campaign finance, government ethics, fair trade, climate change, and corporate accountability ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/wp-content/uploads/corporate-enforcement-public-citizen-report-july-2018.pdf)).

## The 2026 watchdog campaign

The 2026 filings fit a long-standing pattern. Public Citizen was built to force ethics enforcement from the outside — through complaints, FOIA suits, and publicity — and its recent work shows that machinery aimed squarely at the second Trump administration.

The September 2026 ad complaint laid out its case in detail. The ad, first reported by a CBS News Miami reporter who saw it air on September 23, 2026, opened with Trump declaring "Together we will defeat communism, socialism, and Marxism, in America" and included UFC president Dana White calling Trump "the toughest, most resilient person that I've ever met" ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/white-house-spending-taxpayer-dollars-for-propaganda-purposes/)). Public Citizen argued that appropriations laws — including the one Trump himself had recently signed — prohibit spending taxpayer dollars for "publicity or propaganda" purposes, and that the ad was "simple propaganda devoid of a useful government function designed to influence the upcoming elections" ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/white-house-spending-taxpayer-dollars-for-propaganda-purposes/)).

The letter was signed by co-presidents Robert Weissman and Lisa Gilbert, with Craig Holman as government-affairs lobbyist ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/white-house-spending-taxpayer-dollars-for-propaganda-purposes/)). It concluded with a line that captures the organization's posture toward the enforcement agencies it petitions: "It Is Time to Enforce the Law" ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/white-house-spending-taxpayer-dollars-for-propaganda-purposes/)).
