## Overview

[Leicester](/places/leicester/) Royal Infirmary (LRI) is the main emergency hospital for the city of Leicester: an NHS teaching hospital at Infirmary Square, run by University Hospitals of Leicester NHS Trust, with an accident and emergency department open 24 hours a day, every day ([University Hospitals of Leicester NHS Trust](https://www.leicestershospitals.nhs.uk/aboutus/our-hospitals/leicester-royal-infirmary/); [Care Quality Commission](https://www.cqc.org.uk/location/RWEAA)). Founded in 1771 as the Leicester Infirmary with just 40 beds, it has been treating the city's sick and injured for 250 years.

Its latest inspection, published in June 2024, rated it Good for being Effective and Caring, but Requires improvement for being Safe, Responsive and Well-led ([Care Quality Commission](https://www.cqc.org.uk/location/RWEAA)).

The hospital's story is the story of Leicester itself: a voluntary charity hospital founded on donations from the city's rich, later absorbed into a national system, and now serving a city where, in 2020, 35% of the population lived in the most deprived fifth of neighbourhoods nationally, with tuberculosis rates far above the national average ([University of Leicester](http://le.ac.uk/-/media/uol/docs/academic-departments/medicine/leicester-medical-society/winning-essay-2022-from-rebecca-clough-lms.pdf)). It is also the hospital at the centre of one of the defining medical-regulation cases of the 2010s, the Bawa-Garba case, which changed how the profession thinks about individual blame versus system failure ([GPonline](https://www.gponline.com/dr-hadiza-bawa-garba-restored-unrestricted-medical-practice/article/1721266)).

## Two hundred and fifty years: from voluntary infirmary to NHS teaching hospital

The Leicester Infirmary was the first hospital to open in Leicester, founded in 1771 by Dr William Watts, who raised the £2,200 needed to build it through fundraising and donations from the rich of the city ([University of Leicester](http://le.ac.uk/-/media/uol/docs/academic-departments/medicine/leicester-medical-society/winning-essay-2022-from-rebecca-clough-lms.pdf)). When it opened its 40 beds to the public, medicine was almost unrecognisable: there was no running water in the infirmary or in the city itself, women would not be allowed to become doctors for another 130 years, and anaesthesia and antiseptics would not arrive in surgery until the mid-19th century ([University of Leicester](http://le.ac.uk/-/media/uol/docs/academic-departments/medicine/leicester-medical-society/winning-essay-2022-from-rebecca-clough-lms.pdf)). The hospital's founding idea — care for those in need, paid for by philanthropy — has outlasted every change in its funding and governance since, and the essay marking its 250th anniversary notes the parallel with the charities working within University Hospitals of Leicester today ([University of Leicester](http://le.ac.uk/-/media/uol/docs/academic-departments/medicine/leicester-medical-society/winning-essay-2022-from-rebecca-clough-lms.pdf)).

The hospital is also a teaching hospital, and its corridors carry the next generation of doctors: the 250th-anniversary essay was written by a fourth-year medical student at Leicester Medical School who described learning the hospital's history while walking those same corridors ([University of Leicester](http://le.ac.uk/-/media/uol/docs/academic-departments/medicine/leicester-medical-society/winning-essay-2022-from-rebecca-clough-lms.pdf)). That teaching role sits alongside a research role — Leicester is described in the same essay as a centre for ground-breaking research, attracting funding for clinical trials and laboratory studies ([University of Leicester](http://le.ac.uk/-/media/uol/docs/academic-departments/medicine/leicester-medical-society/winning-essay-2022-from-rebecca-clough-lms.pdf)). The hospital's emergency role has been visible nationally as well: after the [Barrow upon Soar](/places/barrow-upon-soar/) derailment in February 2008, the seriously injured train driver was taken to Leicester Royal Infirmary with deep lacerations to his legs ([Barrow upon Soar rail crash](/events/barrow-upon-soar-rail-crash/); [New Civil Engineer](https://www.newcivilengineer.com/archive/lorry-driver-blamed-for-collapsing-bridge-onto-midland-mainline-01-02-2008/)).

## The modern hospital: emergency care, ratings and services

The most visible recent change at the Infirmary is its emergency department. The new department began as a project in 2015, received its first patients in April 2017, and was officially opened by the Princess Royal on 7 March 2018 ([The Crown Chronicles](https://thecrownchronicles.co.uk/royal-news/other-royals/princess-anne-promotes-women-in-space-and-engineering-in-leicester/)). The contractor, Tilbury Douglas, puts the project value at £33.3 million with completion in April 2018, and describes a department designed for up to 270,000 patients a year — against an old building that catered for 100,000 a year but was dealing with 180,000 ([Tilbury Douglas](https://www.tilburydouglas.co.uk/projects/leicester-royal-infirmary-hospital-emergency-department/)). It was the first major investment of University Hospitals of Leicester's five-year, £320 million strategic plan, and the contractor calls it the UK's first frailty-friendly emergency department, designed with Age UK, Vista, Loughborough University and others: rubber flooring to reduce falls, pattern-free floors to avoid distressing people with dementia, clear colour demarcation between zones, lower trolleys, and purpose-built mental health rooms ([Tilbury Douglas](https://www.tilburydouglas.co.uk/projects/leicester-royal-infirmary-hospital-emergency-department/)). The department is single-storey, with separate adult and children's areas, CT and MRI scanning and an integrated mental health unit ([Tilbury Douglas](https://www.tilburydouglas.co.uk/projects/leicester-royal-infirmary-hospital-emergency-department/)).

The regulator's picture is more mixed. The Care Quality Commission's report published on 14 June 2024 rated the hospital Good for being Effective and Caring, but Requires improvement for being Safe, Responsive and Well-led ([Care Quality Commission](https://www.cqc.org.uk/location/RWEAA)). At service level, surgery, critical care and end-of-life care were rated Good, while medical care, services for children and young people, diagnostic imaging, maternity and urgent and emergency services were all rated Requires improvement ([Care Quality Commission](https://www.cqc.org.uk/location/RWEAA)). The hospital's registered specialisms span maternity and midwifery, family planning, surgical and diagnostic procedures, care for adults and children, dementia, learning disabilities, mental health conditions and substance misuse ([Care Quality Commission](https://www.cqc.org.uk/location/RWEAA)). The overall message of the ratings is that the hospital's clinical care is judged good, but its safety, responsiveness and leadership need improvement — a split that echoes the wider debate about NHS pressure, in which overstretched departments struggle to deliver timely care even when the care itself is good.

## The Bawa-Garba case and what it changed

The most consequential event in the Infirmary's recent history was the death of six-year-old Jack Adcock there in 2011. Junior doctor Hadiza Bawa-Garba was convicted of manslaughter over his death, and the medical tribunal recommended a one-year suspension ([GPonline](https://www.gponline.com/dr-hadiza-bawa-garba-restored-unrestricted-medical-practice/article/1721266)). The General Medical Council then challenged that sanction in the High Court, won, and had her struck off in January 2018 — a move that prompted the then health secretary Jeremy Hunt to say he was deeply concerned about its implications for patient safety ([GPonline](https://www.gponline.com/dr-hadiza-bawa-garba-restored-unrestricted-medical-practice/article/1721266)). Later in 2018, the Court of Appeal overturned the High Court ruling and reinstated her, and in July 2021 a tribunal ruled that her fitness to practise was no longer impaired and that she was safe to practise without restriction ([GPonline](https://www.gponline.com/dr-hadiza-bawa-garba-restored-unrestricted-medical-practice/article/1721266)).

The case became a lightning rod because the death had occurred in the context of wider system failures beyond her control: supporters described a paediatrician in training covering six wards across four floors, responding to numerous emergencies without a functioning IT system and without a consultant present, just back from 14 months of maternity leave ([GPonline](https://www.gponline.com/dr-hadiza-bawa-garba-restored-unrestricted-medical-practice/article/1721266)). The fallout reshaped regulation: the 2018 Williams review, commissioned after the case, recommended that the GMC be stripped of its power to appeal tribunal decisions, a recommendation the government accepted ([GPonline](https://www.gponline.com/dr-hadiza-bawa-garba-restored-unrestricted-medical-practice/article/1721266)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Where is Leicester Royal Infirmary?**
At Infirmary Square, Leicester, LE1 5WW. Its switchboard is 0300 303 1573, a 24-hour service, and its accident and emergency department is open 24 hours a day, every day ([University Hospitals of Leicester NHS Trust](https://www.leicestershospitals.nhs.uk/aboutus/our-hospitals/leicester-royal-infirmary/)). The trust's website gives the Infirmary Square address and the switchboard as the first point of contact for visitors.

**Who runs Leicester Royal Infirmary?**
University Hospitals of Leicester NHS Trust runs the hospital, which is a National Health Service teaching hospital. The Care Quality Commission records the trust as the operator of the service, and it is the body the regulator holds responsible for the hospital's ratings and services ([Care Quality Commission](https://www.cqc.org.uk/location/RWEAA)).

**How old is Leicester Royal Infirmary?**
It was founded in 1771 as the Leicester Infirmary with 40 beds, after Dr William Watts raised £2,200 through fundraising and donations. The founding fundraising came from the city's wealthy residents, and the hospital opened without running water. It marked its 250th anniversary in 2021 ([University of Leicester](http://le.ac.uk/-/media/uol/docs/academic-departments/medicine/leicester-medical-society/winning-essay-2022-from-rebecca-clough-lms.pdf)).

**What is the hospital's CQC rating?**
In the report published 14 June 2024, the hospital was rated Good for being Effective and Caring, and Requires improvement for being Safe, Responsive and Well-led. Surgery, critical care and end-of-life care were Good; medical care, maternity, children's services, diagnostic imaging and urgent and emergency services were Requires improvement ([Care Quality Commission](https://www.cqc.org.uk/location/RWEAA)).

**What was the Bawa-Garba case?**
Dr Hadiza Bawa-Garba was convicted of manslaughter after the death of six-year-old Jack Adcock at the Infirmary in 2011. The Court of Appeal reinstated her in 2018 after the GMC had her struck off, and a 2021 tribunal found her fitness to practise no longer impaired. The case led to the Williams review of gross negligence manslaughter in medicine ([GPonline](https://www.gponline.com/dr-hadiza-bawa-garba-restored-unrestricted-medical-practice/article/1721266)).
