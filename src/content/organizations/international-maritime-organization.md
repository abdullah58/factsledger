## Overview

The International Maritime Organization (IMO) is the [United Nations](/organizations/united-nations/) specialized agency that sets the global rules for international shipping — covering maritime safety, security, and the prevention of pollution from ships. Headquartered in [London](/places/london/), [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/), it has 176 member states and works through an Assembly of all members, a 40-member Council, and five committees. Its conventions, above all SOLAS and MARPOL, govern how ships are built and operated — and its emissions-pricing attempt stalled in 2025 ([Devex](https://www.devex.com/organizations/international-maritime-organization-imo-22314)).

The organization began in 1948 as the Inter-Governmental Maritime Consultative Organization (IMCO), took a decade to come into existence, and took its present name in 1982 ([United Nations Dag Hammarskjold Library](https://research.un.org/c.php?g=756034&p=5435544)). Its Secretary-General is Arsenio Dominguez of Panama, whose term began on 1 January 2024, succeeding Kitack Lim of the Republic of Korea ([Devex](https://www.devex.com/organizations/international-maritime-organization-imo-22314)). The IMO's remit today spans safety, environmental protection, legal matters, technical cooperation, maritime security, and the efficiency of shipping ([Max Planck Encyclopedia of European Private Law](https://max-eup2012.mpipriv.de/index.php/International_Maritime_Organization_(IMO))).

## From IMCO to IMO: a decade to exist, then a new name

An international conference in Geneva adopted the Convention establishing the organization on 6 March 1948, but the new body took a decade to come into existence. The delay came from a dispute over its proper role: the Convention's economic objectives — removing discriminatory action and unfair restrictive practices in shipping — were rejected by the main shipping nations, and entry into force waited until states tacitly understood those objectives would not be pursued. The Convention entered into force on 17 March 1958, the organization held its first session in London in January 1959, and that same year it became a specialized agency of the United Nations. It began life as the Inter-Governmental Maritime Consultative Organization (IMCO) and took its present name, the International Maritime Organization, in 1982. Its first Secretary-General was Ove Nielsen of Denmark, who served from 1959 to 1961 ([Devex](https://www.devex.com/organizations/international-maritime-organization-imo-22314); [Max Planck Encyclopedia of European Private Law](https://max-eup2012.mpipriv.de/index.php/International_Maritime_Organization_(IMO)); [United Nations Dag Hammarskjold Library](https://research.un.org/c.php?g=756034&p=5435544)).

Later Secretaries-General include Chandrika Prasad Srivastava of India (1974–1989), Efthimios Mitropoulos of Greece (2004–2011), Koji Sekimizu of Japan (2012–2015), and Kitack Lim of the Republic of Korea (2016–2023) ([United Nations Dag Hammarskjold Library](https://research.un.org/c.php?g=756034&p=5435544)).

## How the IMO makes rules the world fleet must follow

The IMO's Assembly, made up of all member states, meets every two years and approves the work programme and budget. Between sessions, a 40-member Council elected by the Assembly acts as the executive organ, supervising the organization's work. The detailed rulemaking happens in five committees — Maritime Safety, Marine Environment Protection, Legal, Technical Cooperation, and Facilitation — supported by a Secretariat of around 300 international staff in London led by a Secretary-General appointed for a maximum of two four-year terms. Conventions enter into force once states representing a set share of the world fleet have ratified them. Amendments to SOLAS move faster under "tacit acceptance": they are deemed accepted after two years unless more than a third of contracting governments, or governments holding at least half the world's merchant tonnage, object — and they enter into force six months later ([United Nations Dag Hammarskjold Library](https://research.un.org/c.php?g=756034&p=5435544); [Devex](https://www.devex.com/organizations/international-maritime-organization-imo-22314); [Max Planck Encyclopedia of European Private Law](https://max-eup2012.mpipriv.de/index.php/International_Maritime_Organization_(IMO)); [SOLAS Article VIII](http://imorules.com/GUID-512C2496-C578-4346-A3EE-E39C621A3319.html)).

The IMO's authority is written into other treaties: whenever the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) refers to the "competent international organization," the reference is to the IMO ([Max Planck Encyclopedia of European Private Law](https://max-eup2012.mpipriv.de/index.php/International_Maritime_Organization_(IMO))). Sixty-six intergovernmental organizations hold observer status with the IMO, and 85 international non-governmental organizations hold consultative status ([Devex](https://www.devex.com/organizations/international-maritime-organization-imo-22314)).

## The conventions that run world shipping

The IMO's best-known instruments are the safety and pollution conventions: the [International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS)](/events/solas-convention/), 1974, and the [International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships (MARPOL 73/78)](/events/marpol-convention/). Around them sits a web of codes and liability treaties — some binding, some guidance — covering dangerous goods, ship management, oil-pollution compensation, and the removal of wrecks. By 2012 the organization had produced about 50 conventions and more than 800 codes and recommendations. One specialist code shows how far the web reaches: the [INF Code](/events/inf-code/), adopted voluntarily in 1993 and mandatory from January 2001, sets ship-design rules for carrying irradiated nuclear fuel, plutonium, and high-level radioactive waste, complementing the package-safety regulations of the [International Atomic Energy Agency](/organizations/international-atomic-energy-agency/). Its ships are sorted into three classes, INF 1, INF 2, and INF 3, by the aggregate radioactivity they may carry ([Max Planck Encyclopedia of European Private Law](https://max-eup2012.mpipriv.de/index.php/International_Maritime_Organization_(IMO)); [World Nuclear Transport Institute](https://www.wnti.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/The-INF-Code-and-purpose-built-vessels.pdf)). The [World Nuclear Transport Institute](/organizations/world-nuclear-transport-institute/)'s fact sheet for purpose-built vessels sets out the INF Code's ship-design requirements, including a 55°C cap on average cargo-space temperature ([World Nuclear Transport Institute](https://www.wnti.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/The-INF-Code-and-purpose-built-vessels.pdf)).

## The net-zero fight: a framework approved, then stalled

In 2023 the IMO adopted a revised greenhouse-gas strategy aiming for net-zero emissions from international shipping by or around 2050, with interim checkpoints for 2030 and 2040. In April 2025, at the 83rd session of the Marine Environment Protection Committee, member states approved draft rules — a new Chapter 5 of MARPOL Annex VI — that would require ships over 5,000 gross tonnage, responsible for about 85 percent of shipping's carbon dioxide, to cut their fuel's greenhouse-gas intensity over time, buying remedial units when they overshoot and earning surplus units when they beat the standard, with an IMO Net-Zero Fund collecting the payments. But formal adoption, scheduled for October 2025, was postponed for a year by 57 votes to 49 at an extraordinary committee session after opposition led by the United States — and in September 2026, President Donald Trump used a United Nations General Assembly speech to denounce the framework as a global carbon tax ([SAFETY4SEA](https://safety4sea.com/trump-targets-imos-net-zero-framework-at-united-nations-general-assembly/); [Cruise & Ferry](https://www.cruiseandferry.net/articles/imo-member-states-approve-new-measures-for-net-zero-framework)).

The delay is already biting. Singapore's marine biofuel sales fell 47% in January–September 2026 compared with the same period in 2025 ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/shippers-demand-lower-carbon-marine-fuels-cools-imo-rules-hang-balance-2026-10-16/)) — a sign that regulatory uncertainty is chilling investment in cleaner fuels, since shipowners will not pay for greener fuel without a clear IMO decision. "I don't expect the biofuel demand to increase so much until we have an IMO decision," Jens Maul Jorgensen of dry bulk shipowner Oldendorff Carriers told Reuters ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/shippers-demand-lower-carbon-marine-fuels-cools-imo-rules-hang-balance-2026-10-16/)). Meanwhile regional rules are filling the gap: international shipping is already subject to the EU Emissions Trading System, and FuelEU Maritime has introduced greenhouse-gas intensity requirements for ships calling at EU ports ([SAFETY4SEA](https://safety4sea.com/trump-targets-imos-net-zero-framework-at-united-nations-general-assembly/)). IMO member nations were set to meet by early November 2026 on formal adoption ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/shippers-demand-lower-carbon-marine-fuels-cools-imo-rules-hang-balance-2026-10-16/)).

## FAQ

**What does the IMO do?**
The IMO develops and maintains the global regulatory framework for international shipping. Its remit covers maritime safety, environmental protection, legal matters, technical cooperation, maritime security, and shipping efficiency. It drafts conventions such as SOLAS and MARPOL, which member states bring into force by ratifying them ([Max Planck Encyclopedia of European Private Law](https://max-eup2012.mpipriv.de/index.php/International_Maritime_Organization_(IMO))).

**When was the IMO founded?**
The founding Convention was adopted in Geneva on 6 March 1948, but the organization only came into existence when the Convention entered into force on 17 March 1958. It held its first session in January 1959, became a UN specialized agency that year, and changed its name from IMCO to IMO in 1982 ([United Nations Dag Hammarskjold Library](https://research.un.org/c.php?g=756034&p=5435544)).

**How many members does the IMO have?**
The IMO currently has 176 member states. Its Assembly includes all member states and meets every two years, while a 40-member Council elected by the Assembly supervises the organization between sessions. Sixty-six intergovernmental organizations hold observer status and 85 NGOs hold consultative status ([Devex](https://www.devex.com/organizations/international-maritime-organization-imo-22314)).

**How do IMO rules become binding?**
Conventions enter into force after states holding a set fleet share ratify them. SOLAS amendments use tacit acceptance: they are deemed accepted after two years unless over a third of contracting governments, or governments holding at least half the world's merchant tonnage, object — effective six months later ([Max Planck Encyclopedia of European Private Law](https://max-eup2012.mpipriv.de/index.php/International_Maritime_Organization_(IMO)); [SOLAS Article VIII](http://imorules.com/GUID-512C2496-C578-4346-A3EE-E39C621A3319.html)).

**What happened to the IMO's net-zero framework?**
Member states approved the draft Net-Zero Framework in April 2025, but postponed formal adoption for a year in October 2025 by 57 votes to 49 after United States opposition. As of October 2026, member nations were set to meet by early November on formal adoption, while the delay had already cooled demand for lower-carbon marine fuels ([SAFETY4SEA](https://safety4sea.com/trump-targets-imos-net-zero-framework-at-united-nations-general-assembly/); [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/shippers-demand-lower-carbon-marine-fuels-cools-imo-rules-hang-balance-2026-10-16/)).
