## Overview

BasicNeeds began because its founder was turned down. Chris Underhill had spent
eleven years leading Action on Disability and Development (ADD), where he
repeatedly tried to persuade the trustees to include mentally ill people in its
programmes — but they insisted on focusing solely on physical disabilities
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)). In 1996 he left
ADD to become chief executive of Practical Action, growing its budget from £8
million to £13 million, and then walked away after four years to start from
scratch yet again, founding BasicNeeds in 1999
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)). The decision
traced back decades: while living in Malawi, Underhill had watched people with
schizophrenia caged and poked with sticks outside a hospital in Lilongwe, and
decided then that he would one day act for people with mental illnesses
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)).

The organisation's legal foundation is a Declaration of Trust dated 18 November
1999, recorded by the UK Charity Commission, which registered it as charity
number 1079599
([UK Charity Commission](https://register-of-charities.charitycommission.gov.uk/en/charity-search/-/charity-details/3967856/governing-document)).
Most published accounts date the start of its operations to 2000
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)). From the
outset, BasicNeeds paired two things most mental-health efforts kept separate:
access to community-based treatment and the means to earn a living
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)).

## The Model for Mental Health and Development

Underhill created the Model for Mental Health and Development, and it was field
tested by D. M. Naidu of Bangalore, India — the first trials ran in Karnataka,
then in other Indian states, and eventually across 15 low-income countries
([Solutions Journalism Network interview, 2024](https://sjn-static.s3.amazonaws.com/solutions-insights-lab/Complete_Interview+with+Chris+Underhill+(BasicNeeds+and+Elders+Council+for+Social+Entrepreneurs).pdf)).
As documented in a 2012 peer-reviewed case study in PLOS Medicine, the model has
five components — capacity building, community mental health, livelihoods,
research, and management — and works through user empowerment, community
development, health-systems strengthening and policy influencing
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)). Its stated
vision is that "the basic needs of all people with mental illness or epilepsy
throughout the world are satisfied and their basic rights are recognized and
respected"
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)).

A deliberate operating principle ran through the work: BasicNeeds never provided
medical treatment itself. Diagnosis, medication and clinical care were always
the state's job; the organisation worked with governments so they provided
treatment, while it supplied the social side — livelihoods, self-help groups,
stigma reduction — "like a social prescription in the modern parlance," as
Underhill put it
([Solutions Journalism Network interview, 2024](https://sjn-static.s3.amazonaws.com/solutions-insights-lab/Complete_Interview+with+Chris+Underhill+(BasicNeeds+and+Elders+Council+for+Social+Entrepreneurs).pdf)).
In practice this meant mental-health camps — regular clinics where people from
surrounding areas could see a psychiatrist, access medication, and join group
therapy or advocacy sessions, often minutes from home instead of a day's travel
to a capital-city hospital
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)). Local volunteers,
including stabilised former patients in Sri Lanka, were trained to support
follow-up care and free professionals for complex cases
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)).

The model kept evolving. In 2022 the successor BasicNeeds Network launched an
updated version built on four Foundations — Human Rights, Recovery, Integration
and Quality — and four Elements: psychosocial support, community development,
livelihoods and system strengthening
([BasicNeeds Network Impact Report 2023](https://www.cbmuk.org.uk/app/uploads/2024/11/CBM_BNN-Impact-Report_Nov-8.pdf)).

## Scale, countries and the Nepal case study

By 2011 BasicNeeds was running programmes in 98 districts across 11 countries —
Ghana, Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania, India, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Lao PDR and Vietnam,
with new programmes starting in China and the UK — working with 55 local
partners and reaching 39,518 affected individuals
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)). A year
later, its own account put the footprint at 12 countries and more than 85,000
people reached, about 60 percent living with mental illness and 40 percent with
epilepsy
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)). When Underhill
left in 2016 after 16 years, he said the programme reached 850,000 people
across 15 countries — scale he noted as unusual in a field where most NGOs work
out of a single clinic
([Solutions Journalism Network interview, 2024](https://sjn-static.s3.amazonaws.com/solutions-insights-lab/Complete_Interview+with+Chris+Underhill+(BasicNeeds+and+Elders+Council+for+Social+Entrepreneurs).pdf)).
A specialist NGO directory separately listed programmes in Ghana, Uganda,
Tanzania, Kenya, China, India, Sri Lanka, Pakistan, Vietnam, Lao PDR, Nepal and
the UK, with offices in the UK, India, the USA and Australia
([China Development Brief](https://chinadevelopmentbrief.org/ngos/basic-needs/)).

The best-documented programme is Nepal, the subject of the 2012 PLOS Medicine
case study. Nepal was the first country where BasicNeeds set up no country
office at all, operating instead through a direct partnership with an
independent local NGO, the Livelihoods Education and Development Society (LEADS)
of Pokhara — a prototype for the social-franchise approach the organisation
wanted to scale
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)). Funded by
the UK Department for International Development, the four-year programme (May
2010 to March 2014) ran in the remote Baglung and Myagdi districts, where a
baseline study found no government mental-health services and no
mental-health-trained staff; families seeking treatment had been paying an
estimated 25,000 Nepalese rupees (about US$312) a year
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)).

Mental-health camps began at the two district hospitals in August 2010, with
the chief psychiatrist from Pokhara's Western Regional Hospital attending, and
follow-up clinics were pushed out to village health posts, with LEADS supplying
staff with mobile-phone SIM cards to stay in contact with the psychiatrist on
clinic days
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)). By March
2011, 311 people had registered (134 men, 177 women); 269 were reported to show
improvement, and diagnoses were made by qualified psychiatrists using WHO
ICD-10 criteria
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)). On the
livelihoods side, 31 people were prioritised for support — skills training or
cash grants — and by an October 2011 evaluation all 31 were earning, through
tea and grocery shops, chicken and goat rearing, tailoring and embroidery; the
six who had earned before the programme saw incomes rise between 17 and 108
percent
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)). A Nepalese
pharmaceutical company supplied free medicines for up to 200 camp attendees,
with no financial relationship to the programme
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)).

Barriers were frank: villages almost entirely inaccessible by road, existing
self-help groups resisting the admission of mentally ill members, and political
instability delaying engagement with the government
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)). Elsewhere,
the model adapted to local politics — in Uganda, advocacy groups began lobbying
district officials directly for better treatment services; in Ghana, user groups
came together as the registered national Mental Health Society of Ghana
(MEHSOG); in Lao PDR, services ran through primary care in nine districts of
the Vientiane capital region
([PLOS Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3393669/)).

## Policy influence and advocacy

BasicNeeds treated data and user organisation as policy tools. From the start,
Underhill insisted on capturing data on every person the organisation worked
with — in a field where governments would not fund what they could not measure
— building one of the largest datasets of its kind
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)). In Ghana, the
organisation helped form 239 user-led groups, which grew into a registered
national user association with its own secretariat
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)).

The combination moved policy. Underhill worked with the World Health
Organization on its mhGAP Intervention Guide, and BasicNeeds country offices
played significant roles in national mental-health policy reforms in Uganda,
Kenya, Tanzania, Sri Lanka and Lao PDR
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)). In Uganda, the
organisation brought together 300 users and carers from self-help groups to
review the draft mental-health bill and policy — a process that culminated in
the country's National Strategy on Mental, Neurological, and Substance Abuse
Disorders Policy
([Ashoka](https://www.ashoka.org/de-de/fellow/chris-underhill)).

## Funding shortfall, merger and the BasicNeeds Network

By 2017 the money had run short. BasicNeeds, with an annual income of about
£1.6 million, approached [CBM UK](/organizations/cbm-uk/) — income roughly £6 million — and proposed a
merger to prevent its programmes, then running in 14 countries, from closing
([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/basicneeds-merged-christian-disability-charity/management/article/1440733)).
On 20 July 2017 it became a wholly-owned subsidiary of CBM UK; five UK-based
staff joined the CBM team, three UK roles and one IT role in Sri Lanka were
lost, and no jobs or funding were cut from the overseas programmes
([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/basicneeds-merged-christian-disability-charity/management/article/1440733)).
Both charities stayed registered with the Charity Commission, with CBM UK's
trustees taking over BasicNeeds' governance, and the merged operation was based
at CBM UK's Cambridge headquarters — BasicNeeds had formerly been based in
Leamington Spa, Warwickshire
([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/basicneeds-merged-christian-disability-charity/management/article/1440733)).
BasicNeeds chief executive Adrian Sell continued to oversee the programmes and
joined CBM UK's leadership
([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/basicneeds-merged-christian-disability-charity/management/article/1440733)).
CBM UK described BasicNeeds as "the largest organisation working globally to
improve the lives of people affected by mental health problems," and said the
merger would create a new strong voice on mental health in development
([CBM UK](https://www.cbmuk.org.uk/news-and-blogs/tackling-mental-health-worlds-poorest-places-cbm-uk-merges-basicneeds-uk/?form=secure-donate)).

The story did not end at the merger. Over time, country programmes such as
those in Vietnam, Ghana and Kenya became independently registered organisations
in their own countries, and in November 2022 they formed the BasicNeeds
Network — a collective of community mental-health practitioners using the
BasicNeeds Model, supported by CBM UK and CBM Global
([BasicNeeds Network Impact Report 2023](https://www.cbmuk.org.uk/app/uploads/2024/11/CBM_BNN-Impact-Report_Nov-8.pdf)).
The Network's first consolidated report, for 2023, counted nine member
organisations and affiliated individuals across ten countries — India, Ghana,
Uganda, Kenya, Vietnam, Lao PDR, Nigeria, Pakistan, the USA and the UK —
reporting 58,696 people supported, 456 active peer and self-help groups with
13,259 members, 92,526 people attending face-to-face awareness events, 3,120
frontline health workers and teachers given specialist training, and 4,196
people supported in earning a living
([BasicNeeds Network Impact Report 2023](https://www.cbmuk.org.uk/app/uploads/2024/11/CBM_BNN-Impact-Report_Nov-8.pdf)).
