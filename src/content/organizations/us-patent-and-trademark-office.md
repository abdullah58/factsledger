## Overview

The United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) is the federal agency that grants U.S. patents and registers U.S. trademarks, operating inside the Department of Commerce and funded by the fees its users pay rather than by taxpayers. It traces its history to the first Patent Act of 1790, became a separate office under the Patent Act of 1836, and took its current name in 1975. ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/history/freedom250/americas-innovative-history); [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/features/anniversary-first-patent))

The office is headquartered in Alexandria, Virginia, and is led by the Under Secretary of Commerce for Intellectual Property and Director of the USPTO, John A. Squires, who was confirmed by the Senate in September 2025 ([Goodwin](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/watch-out-section-101-the-uspto-has-a-9767361/); [U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/e5a21495-b7b0-4962-650e-5e18213b112e/2026-09-29-PM_Testimony_Squires_ede844c2-3240-403d-95a7-0686d085cbf0.pdf)). It is a large operation: at the end of fiscal year 2023 it employed 13,452 federal staff, and in that year it received 594,143 utility patent applications and 737,018 trademark applications, collecting $4.04 billion in fees ([USPTO Agency Financial Report](https://www.uspto.gov/sites/default/files/documents/USPTOFY23AFR.pdf)). In older usage, and in much journalism, it is still called "the Patent Office" or "the U.S. Patent Office" — the name it carried until 1975 ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/241.html)).

## Two centuries of paper, fire and reinvention

The patent system is written into the Constitution itself: Article I, Section 8, Clause 8 gives Congress the power to promote the progress of science and useful arts by securing exclusive rights to authors and inventors for limited times ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/history/freedom250/americas-innovative-history)). Congress built on that foundation with the first Patent Act on 10 April 1790, creating a Patent Board of the Secretary of State, the Secretary of War and the Attorney General ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/241.html)). Thomas Jefferson, then Secretary of State, led that first board and is sometimes called the "first patent examiner" ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/history/freedom250/americas-innovative-history)). The first patent followed within months: on 31 July 1790, Samuel Hopkins received a patent for improvements in making potash and pearl ash, signed by President George Washington ([GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/features/anniversary-first-patent)).

The modern office was born out of disaster. The Patent Act of 1836, signed on 4 July of that year, made the Patent Office a separate organization, required patents to be examined, and created a corps of professional examiners ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/241.html)). On 15 December 1836, a fire tore through the office, then housed in Blodgett's Hotel in [Washington, D.C.](/places/district-of-columbia/), destroying most records of the nation's first 46 years of patents ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/history/freedom250/americas-innovative-history)). Of around 10,000 early patents, only about 2,800 were ever recovered; the rest survive, if at all, as reconstructed "X-patents" numbered with an X suffix ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/history/freedom250/americas-innovative-history); [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/features/anniversary-first-patent)). The numbering system the 1836 Act created is still in use: Patent No. 1 went to John Ruggles on 13 July 1836 for a traction wheel for steam locomotives ([GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/features/anniversary-first-patent)).

The office moved with the government around it: to the Department of the Interior in 1849, then to the Department of Commerce in 1925, where it remains ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/241.html)). Its first dedicated building, opened in 1840, doubled as one of Washington's first public museums, exhibiting patent models alongside artifacts of the founding era ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/history/freedom250/americas-innovative-history)). Abraham Lincoln is the only U.S. president to be granted a patent, for a method of buoying vessels over shoals ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/history/freedom250/americas-innovative-history)). On the trademark side, the first federal registration issued on 25 October 1870 to the Averill Chemical Paint Company, and the Lanham Act of 5 July 1946 remains the primary federal trademark statute ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/history/freedom250/americas-innovative-history)). The Patent Office Name Change Act of 2 January 1975 finally added "Trademark" to the name ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/241.html)).

## What the office does today

The USPTO runs two business lines: patents and trademarks. On the patent side it examines applications for utility, plant and reissue patents, and its examiners apply four statutory requirements — the invention must be patentable subject matter, new, non-obvious, and described clearly enough that someone else could make or use it once the patent expires ([Colorado Public Radio](https://www.cpr.org/2024/08/23/interview-rocky-mountain-patent-office-molly-kocialski-cyndi-bray/)). It receives a little over 630,000 patent applications a year and issues about 300,000 patents ([Colorado Public Radio](https://www.cpr.org/2024/08/23/interview-rocky-mountain-patent-office-molly-kocialski-cyndi-bray/)). On the trademark side it examines applications for federal registration; in fiscal year 2023 it received 737,018 trademark applications ([USPTO Agency Financial Report](https://www.uspto.gov/sites/default/files/documents/USPTOFY23AFR.pdf)).

The biggest structural change in modern times came with the Leahy-Smith America Invents Act, signed on 16 September 2011 and described as the most significant update to U.S. patent law in over 50 years ([Corporate Counsel Business Journal](https://ccbjournal.com/articles/america-invents-act-makes-historic-changes-us-patent-law)). It moved the United States from first-to-invent to first-inventor-to-file, effective for applications with priority claims on or after 16 March 2013, and created the Patent Trial and Appeal Board (PTAB) to hear appeals from examiners and to run two new ways of challenging issued patents — post-grant review and inter partes review, both effective 16 September 2012 ([Corporate Counsel Business Journal](https://ccbjournal.com/articles/america-invents-act-makes-historic-changes-us-patent-law)). The PTAB's ex parte appeal inventory has since fallen to roughly 1,800 cases, with maximum pendency of about nine months ([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/e5a21495-b7b0-4962-650e-5e18213b112e/2026-09-29-PM_Testimony_Squires_ede844c2-3240-403d-95a7-0686d085cbf0.pdf)).

The office is unusual among federal agencies because it operates solely on fees collected from its users, not on taxpayer dollars — so much so that during the last government shutdown it stayed open on its fee reserves ([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/e5a21495-b7b0-4962-650e-5e18213b112e/2026-09-29-PM_Testimony_Squires_ede844c2-3240-403d-95a7-0686d085cbf0.pdf)). Its authority under Section 10 of the America Invents Act to set and adjust those fees is currently extended only through 11 December 2026 ([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/e5a21495-b7b0-4962-650e-5e18213b112e/2026-09-29-PM_Testimony_Squires_ede844c2-3240-403d-95a7-0686d085cbf0.pdf)). The economic stakes are large: the office's own report on 2024 data found that IP-intensive industries accounted for approximately $11.4 trillion, or 44%, of private-sector U.S. GDP, directly employing 49.6 million American workers and supporting 16.2 million more through supply chains ([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/e5a21495-b7b0-4962-650e-5e18213b112e/2026-09-29-PM_Testimony_Squires_ede844c2-3240-403d-95a7-0686d085cbf0.pdf)) — a figure that makes the quality of its examination an economic question, not just a legal one.

## The office as referee: reexamination and the JPEG patent

Granting patents is only half the job; the office also corrects its own mistakes. Anyone can ask it to reexamine an issued patent in light of prior art, and the office can cancel claims it finds should never have been granted. The best-known modern example involves the JPEG image standard and [Asure Software](/organizations/asure-software/), founded in 1985 as Forgent Networks. Forgent asserted U.S. Patent No. 4,698,672 — covering a technique for compressing data by replacing frequently appearing code strings with shorter ones — against the JPEG standard, suing about 30 companies and collecting about $105 million in licensing revenue ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/180138/jpeg.html)).

In May 2006, after the [Public Patent Foundation](/organizations/public-patent-foundation/) asked for reexamination and submitted prior art it said made the patent invalid, the USPTO rejected 19 of the patent's 47 claims, including the broadest ones — among them claim 1, "a method for processing digital signals … to reduce the amount of data utilized" ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/180138/jpeg.html)). Forgent said it was pleased the office had upheld the majority of its claims and promised to "vigorously defend" the rejected ones, but the episode showed the reexamination system working as a safety valve: a patent that had extracted nine-figure royalties could still be cut back when earlier inventions came to light ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/180138/jpeg.html)). That corrective role is the same one the PTAB now performs at larger scale through inter partes review ([Corporate Counsel Business Journal](https://ccbjournal.com/articles/america-invents-act-makes-historic-changes-us-patent-law)).

## The Squires era: backlog, AI and eligibility

John A. Squires took over an office with a long-standing complaint — delay — and made patent eligibility under 35 U.S.C. § 101 his signature issue, arguing that Section 101 "should not be misused as a blunt instrument to exclude entire technological fields" ([Goodwin](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/watch-out-section-101-the-uspto-has-a-9767361/)). On his first full day after confirmation he signed two patents into issuance, one in distributed-ledger technology and one in medical diagnostics, a deliberate signal about fields where eligibility uncertainty affects investment ([Goodwin](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/watch-out-section-101-the-uspto-has-a-9767361/)). Days later, on 26 September 2025, the PTAB's Appeals Review Panel decided *Ex parte Desjardins*, vacating a Section 101 rejection of Google DeepMind claims for a method of training machine-learning models to reduce "catastrophic forgetting," and finding the claims directed to a technological improvement ([Goodwin](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/watch-out-section-101-the-uspto-has-a-9767361/)).

On artificial intelligence and inventorship, the office reversed course within two years. Its February 2024 guidance had applied the *Pannu* joint-inventorship factors to AI-assisted inventions, requiring a natural person to have made a "significant contribution" to conception ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=09e9f62e-6785-4b3c-a085-68839a2ea9e9)). On 28 November 2025 the office rescinded that guidance in its entirety and replaced it, treating AI systems as instruments used by human inventors — like laboratory equipment — and reaffirming that only natural persons can be inventors, following *Thaler v. Vidal* ([Federal Register](https://federalregister.gov/d/2025-21457); [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=09e9f62e-6785-4b3c-a085-68839a2ea9e9)). On 11 March 2026 Squires issued further guidance clarifying that a digital design may qualify for design patent protection when it satisfies the statutory requirements ([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/e5a21495-b7b0-4962-650e-5e18213b112e/2026-09-29-PM_Testimony_Squires_ede844c2-3240-403d-95a7-0686d085cbf0.pdf)).

The backlog is also moving. ## Frequently asked questions

**What does the USPTO do?**
The USPTO grants U.S. patents and registers U.S. trademarks, and advises the President, through the Secretary of Commerce, on intellectual-property policy. It examines patent applications for novelty, non-obviousness and other statutory requirements, and runs the Patent Trial and Appeal Board, which hears appeals and challenges to issued patents ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/history/freedom250/americas-innovative-history); [Corporate Counsel Business Journal](https://ccbjournal.com/articles/america-invents-act-makes-historic-changes-us-patent-law)).

**Is the USPTO the same as the U.S. Patent Office?**
Yes. "The U.S. Patent Office" was the office's name until 2 January 1975, when the Patent Office Name Change Act renamed it the Patent and Trademark Office to reflect its trademark work. The older name still appears in journalism and in older court and agency documents ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/241.html); [GovInfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/features/anniversary-first-patent)).

**How long does it take to get a U.S. patent?**
The office's target is a first office action within 14 months of filing, though recently it has run closer to 16 months. The queue is improving: in fiscal year 2026 output exceeded filings for the first time in nearly a decade, and the unexamined inventory fell by about 84,000 applications ([Colorado Public Radio](https://www.cpr.org/2024/08/23/interview-rocky-mountain-patent-office-molly-kocialski-cyndi-bray/); [U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/e5a21495-b7b0-4962-650e-5e18213b112e/2026-09-29-PM_Testimony_Squires_ede844c2-3240-403d-95a7-0686d085cbf0.pdf)).

**Can an AI system be named as an inventor on a patent?**
No. Only natural persons can be inventors under the Patent Act, as the Federal Circuit held in *Thaler v. Vidal*. The USPTO's revised guidance of 28 November 2025 treats AI as a tool assisting human inventors, and the key question is whether a natural person conceived the invention ([Federal Register](https://federalregister.gov/d/2025-21457); [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=09e9f62e-6785-4b3c-a085-68839a2ea9e9)).

**How is the USPTO funded?**
It is funded by the fees its users pay for patent and trademark services, not by taxpayer dollars — it collected $4.04 billion in fees in fiscal year 2023. Its authority to set and adjust those fees is currently extended only through 11 December 2026 ([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/e5a21495-b7b0-4962-650e-5e18213b112e/2026-09-29-PM_Testimony_Squires_ede844c2-3240-403d-95a7-0686d085cbf0.pdf); [USPTO Agency Financial Report](https://www.uspto.gov/sites/default/files/documents/USPTOFY23AFR.pdf)).
