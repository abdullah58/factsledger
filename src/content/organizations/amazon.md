## Overview

Amazon spent 2026 becoming two contradictory things at once: the biggest patron in AI history and the whistleblower that got its own AI investment shut down by the federal government. In April, the company deepened its Anthropic partnership to $13 billion committed — with up to $20 billion more on the table — while Anthropic pledged more than $100 billion to [Amazon Web Services](https://factsledger.com/organizations/amazon-web-services/) over the next decade ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-amazon-compute)). Then in June, CEO Andy Jassy reportedly picked up the phone to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and told him Amazon's own researchers had jailbroken Anthropic's Fable 5 model to extract information usable in cyberattacks — setting off the federal order that took Anthropic's two flagship models offline for everyone ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-ceo-reportedly-raised-anthropic-fable-concerns-prior-to-u-s-order-forcing-models-offline/)). And in August, the FTC and 22 states sued Amazon over an alleged seven-year scheme to secretly rig its advertising auctions, extracting tens of billions of dollars from more than a million advertisers ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)). The everything-store, three decades in, is now the company that funds frontier AI, polices it, and stands accused of taxing the merchants on its own shelves.

## The patron that turned whistleblower

Amazon's relationship with Anthropic began in 2023 and grew in tranches: an initial $1.25 billion, then $2.75 billion more in early 2024, then a $4 billion add-on — $8 billion in total before 2026, as Amazon itself confirmed through Anthropic's announcement ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-amazon-compute)). The April 2026 agreement was the escalation: $5 billion invested immediately, up to $20 billion more available down the road, with Anthropic committing more than $100 billion over ten years to AWS technologies and securing up to 5 gigawatts of capacity — spanning Amazon's custom Trainium2 through Trainium4 chips and its Graviton processors ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-amazon-compute)). More than a million Trainium2 chips were already training and serving Claude, over 100,000 customers were running Claude on Amazon Bedrock, and Anthropic said its run-rate revenue had passed $30 billion, up from about $9 billion at the end of 2025 ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-amazon-compute)). The two companies had jointly built Project Rainier, one of the largest compute clusters in the world, and Amazon's CEO praised Anthropic's decade-long commitment to Trainium as proof of progress on custom silicon ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-amazon-compute)).

Less than two months later, that same partnership detonated. According to reporting relayed by GeekWire's Todd Bishop, The Information first reported calls between Jassy and senior administration officials, and The Wall Street Journal reported that Jassy told Bessent and others that Amazon researchers had used Anthropic's Fable 5 to obtain information that could be used in cyberattacks ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-ceo-reportedly-raised-anthropic-fable-concerns-prior-to-u-s-order-forcing-models-offline/)). Amazon shared those findings with officials; its spokesperson told GeekWire it was "not uncommon for governments to seek our counsel on potential security risks," while adding that the company does not share details of such discussions ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-ceo-reportedly-raised-anthropic-fable-concerns-prior-to-u-s-order-forcing-models-offline/)). The administration's directive, issued on a Friday afternoon, cited a jailbreak method for Fable 5 — a general-use version of the more powerful Mythos 5 — and suspended access for any foreign national, which forced Anthropic to disable both models for all users since it could not instantly verify every user's citizenship ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-ceo-reportedly-raised-anthropic-fable-concerns-prior-to-u-s-order-forcing-models-offline/)).

As GeekWire put it, the situation put Amazon "in an unusual and potentially awkward position with Anthropic, in which it has invested $13 billion since 2023, with plans to put in as much as $20 billion more" ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-ceo-reportedly-raised-anthropic-fable-concerns-prior-to-u-s-order-forcing-models-offline/)). Awkward is an understatement: Amazon was simultaneously Anthropic's largest investor, its primary cloud host, its chip supplier, its distribution channel through Bedrock, and — via its own Nova models — a direct competitor. Anthropic pushed back, saying the vulnerabilities were "relatively simple," findable through other publicly available models including OpenAI's GPT-5.5, and warning that applying the standard industry-wide "would essentially halt all new model deployments for all frontier model providers" ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-ceo-reportedly-raised-anthropic-fable-concerns-prior-to-u-s-order-forcing-models-offline/)). Independent experts questioned the severity too: GreyNoise Intelligence founder Andrew Morris told the Journal the report showed Fable could surface bugs in at least four software programs but was "still a long way from dangerous cybersecurity information" ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-ceo-reportedly-raised-anthropic-fable-concerns-prior-to-u-s-order-forcing-models-offline/)). Fable 5 remained unavailable as of mid-June 2026, and the episode followed an earlier 2026 clash in which the Pentagon designated Anthropic's models a supply-chain risk after the two sides disagreed over uses like mass domestic surveillance and lethal autonomous weapons ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-ceo-reportedly-raised-anthropic-fable-concerns-prior-to-u-s-order-forcing-models-offline/)).

## The ad-auction lawsuit

On August 31, 2026, the [Federal Trade Commission](https://factsledger.com/organizations/federal-trade-commission/) and the attorneys general of 22 states — Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Florida, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maryland, Nebraska, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, Vermont and Washington — sued Amazon in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)). The complaint alleges that for more than seven years, Amazon "covertly and substantially" inflated what over a million brands and sellers paid for search-advertising placements, likely extracting tens of billions of dollars — including from more than 500,000 small and medium-sized businesses ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)).

The mechanism, as alleged, is the kind of thing that makes auction theorists wince. Amazon told advertisers for years that it ran "second price" auctions, where the winner pays "one cent more than the next highest bidder" — the generalized second-price model that has been the digital-advertising industry standard ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)). In practice, the complaint says, Amazon since 2019 had been adding an undisclosed "soft reserve price" — a surcharge one internal document described as hidden in the auction — converting the nominally second-price auction into a first-price one, where winners simply pay their own bid ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)). For Sponsored Products ads, the share of auctions in which advertisers paid their own full bid rose from 30–40% in 2021 to 70% in 2022 and about 80% in 2024 ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)). The complaint quotes the Amazon Ads executive explaining internally that the price "isn't set by an actual bidder" but is a "proxy 2nd price that we calculate," and another document referencing an "invented auction participant" — which the FTC characterizes as essentially shill bids ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)). A 2024 discussion among senior executives, including the head of Amazon Ads and the company's chief digital economist, described the "clever non-transparent way to charge first price" as an "incredibly effective way to drive revenue," while internal documents warned that disclosure would cause "irrevocable damage to advertiser trust" and a "downward spiral" of bid-lowering ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)). The commission vote authorizing the complaint was 2–0 ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)).

The politics of the case are part of the story. FTC Chairman Andrew N. Ferguson — serving under President Trump — declared: "The FTC under President Trump won't allow this deception to continue," adding that the higher ad costs were "largely passed on to American consumers" ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-states-sue-amazon-over-secret-ad-surcharge-scheme)). Amazon has rejected the allegations; the profile treats the complaint's claims as allegations, not findings, and Amazon's position as the company's stated view.

## The bookstore that ate the world

The 2026 drama sits on top of the familiar origin story. On July 5, 1994, Princeton graduate [Jeff Bezos](https://factsledger.com/people/jeff-bezos/) founded Cadabra — a twist on the magic word "Abracadabra" — as an online bookstore run from his garage in Bellevue, Washington, later changing the name to Amazon ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/july-5/amazon-is-founded-by-jeff-bezos)). The website launched in 1995 on the still-young world wide web; Amazon went public two years later ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/july-5/amazon-is-founded-by-jeff-bezos)). Music and videos followed in 1999, third-party sellers in 2000, Prime in 2005 as a fast-shipping subscription, and the first Kindle e-reader in 2007 ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/july-5/amazon-is-founded-by-jeff-bezos)). The pivotal launch was 2006's Amazon Web Services, built to sell cloud computing — which, as of 2024, provided a majority of Amazon's operating profits ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/july-5/amazon-is-founded-by-jeff-bezos)). Expansion continued through acquisitions, most notably the $13.7 billion purchase of Whole Foods in 2017 ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/july-5/amazon-is-founded-by-jeff-bezos)). In 2021, Bezos stepped down as CEO to become executive chairman, handing the top job to Jassy ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/july-5/amazon-is-founded-by-jeff-bezos)). Bezos once said Amazon's success rested on three ideas it had "stuck with for 18 years": "Put the customer first. Invent. And be patient" ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/july-5/amazon-is-founded-by-jeff-bezos)). In 2026, the customer-first company was answering to the FTC, the invent company was building frontier AI chips, and the patient company was discovering that patience cuts both ways.

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not present the FTC's ad-auction allegations as proven — the lawsuit was filed in August 2026 and no court has ruled. It does not assert the exact date of Jassy's call to Bessent, since reporting differs (Fortune said June 11; other accounts point to June 12); the profile says mid-June 2026 and notes the directive landed on Friday, June 12. It does not claim Amazon's Anthropic stake beyond the announced commitments ($8 billion previously, $5 billion in April 2026, up to $20 billion more available). It does not cover the reported Melania Trump documentary deal or AWS's 2026 Middle East outages, which were not read in full for this profile. And it treats Amazon's response to the FTC suit as the company's stated position, not an adjudicated outcome.
