## The court where "en banc" is a lottery

In federal appeals courts, "en banc" is Latin for the whole bench —
every active judge deciding the case together. Except in one circuit. The
Ninth Circuit, with too many judges to fit around a single argument table,
alone among the federal appellate courts decides en banc cases with just
eleven of its active judges: the chief judge plus ten drawn by lot
([Harvard Law & Policy Review](http://journals.law.harvard.edu/lpr/2019/09/25/divide-and-conquer-how-the-democrats-can-maintain-control-of-the-ninth-circuit/)).
Congress authorized the procedure in 1978 for any circuit with fifteen or
more active judges; the Ninth Circuit adopted it by local rule in August
1980 and no other circuit has ever followed
([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/sites/default/files/2012/9CirInnv.pdf)).

The lottery has consequences that would be impossible under full en banc.
In 2019, a limited en banc panel upheld the Trump administration's
abortion-funding rules in California v. Azar by a 7–4 vote — with seven
Republican appointees and four Democratic appointees on the panel, even
though 15 of the 26 active judges at the time were Democratic appointees.
All votes fell on party lines
([Harvard Law & Policy Review](http://journals.law.harvard.edu/lpr/2019/09/25/divide-and-conquer-how-the-democrats-can-maintain-control-of-the-ninth-circuit/)).
Critics call it minority rule: as one Ninth Circuit judge put it in
Senate testimony, the procedure "presents the quite real possibility
that some of the circuit's most important or divisive issues will be
decided by a substantial minority of judges, who do not represent the
true views of our court"
([Harvard Law & Policy Review](http://journals.law.harvard.edu/lpr/2019/09/25/divide-and-conquer-how-the-democrats-can-maintain-control-of-the-ninth-circuit/)).

The circuit knows the objection and keeps the procedure anyway. The
Federal Judicial Center's evaluation found that between 1980 and 1984,
the new rule cut the average time from submission to decision nearly in
half — from 349 days to 175 — and all but one active judge called the
limited panel a satisfactory substitute for the full bench
([Federal Judicial Center](https://www.fjc.gov/sites/default/files/2012/9CirInnv.pdf)).
Every active judge still votes on whether to take a case en banc, and any
judge can request a rehearing before the full court — but Chief Judge
Sidney Thomas told the Senate the circuit had never once used that
option
([Harvard Law & Policy Review](http://journals.law.harvard.edu/lpr/2019/09/25/divide-and-conquer-how-the-democrats-can-maintain-control-of-the-ninth-circuit/)).
The court even experimented with a more representative 15-judge panel;
both the judges and the bar found it too unwieldy
([Harvard Law & Policy Review](http://journals.law.harvard.edu/lpr/2019/09/25/divide-and-conquer-how-the-democrats-can-maintain-control-of-the-ninth-circuit/)).

## The VA order that the en banc court itself erased

The limited en banc procedure is not an abstraction — it decided the
fate of one of the court's most dramatic orders. In May 2011, a
three-judge panel in Veterans for Common Sense v. Shinseki found the
[Department of Veterans Affairs](https://factsledger.com/organizations/us-department-of-veterans-affairs/)' mental-health system
guilty of "unchecked incompetence": the average disability claim took
more than four years, and 18 veterans were dying by suicide each day,
according to Senior Judge Procter Hug Jr.'s opinion. The panel ordered
an immediate overhaul of the VA's system
([WEKU (NPR)](https://www.weku.org/weku-news/2011-05-11/u-s-appeals-court-says-va-care-unchecked-incompetence)).
A year later, the en banc court undid it. On rehearing, the court held
that 38 U.S.C. § 511 barred the district court from hearing claims about
how the VA administers benefits — affirmed in part, reversed in part,
and remanded with instructions to dismiss
([Willamette Law Online](https://wlo.willamette.edu/9thcir/2012/05/veterans-for-common-sense-v-shinseki.html)).

## The biggest, busiest, most split-proposed circuit

Created in 1891 and headquartered in San Francisco, the Ninth Circuit
covers nine states plus Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands — more than
40 percent of the U.S. landmass — and hears more than 11,000 cases a
year, with 29 authorized judgeships (more than twice the circuit
average) and 56 percent of the nation's immigration cases
([U.S. Senate](https://www.crapo.senate.gov/media/newsroom/crapo-leads-effort-to-fix-ninth-circuits-out-of-control-caseload)).
Its decisions affect roughly one in five Americans
([Harvard Law & Policy Review](http://journals.law.harvard.edu/lpr/2019/09/25/divide-and-conquer-how-the-democrats-can-maintain-control-of-the-ninth-circuit/)).
The size has fueled split proposals for decades: the latest Senate
effort would carve seven states — Alaska, Arizona, Idaho, Montana,
Nevada, Oregon, and Washington — into a new Twelfth Circuit
([U.S. Senate](https://www.crapo.senate.gov/media/newsroom/crapo-leads-effort-to-fix-ninth-circuits-out-of-control-caseload)).
Thirty-four Ninth Circuit judges wrote the Senate Judiciary Committee in
2017 opposing the split
([Harvard Law & Policy Review](http://journals.law.harvard.edu/lpr/2019/09/25/divide-and-conquer-how-the-democrats-can-maintain-control-of-the-ninth-circuit/)).

The bench also attracts unusual cross-partisan defenders. When President
Obama nominated Berkeley professor Goodwin Liu to the circuit in 2010,
[[Richard Painter](/people/richard-painter/)](https://factsledger.com/people/richard-painter/) — a
conservative Republican who had served as President George W. Bush's
chief ethics lawyer — wrote a Politico op-ed making the case for Liu.
Senators Dianne Feinstein and Chris Coons quoted Painter's editorial on
the Senate floor, and the San Francisco Chronicle cited his support in an
editorial condemning the filibuster that sank the nomination
([University of Minnesota Law School](https://law.umn.edu/news/2011-05-19-prof-painter-writes-op-ed-case-goodwin-liu)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim the limited en banc lottery is unconstitutional
or invalid — it is a statutorily authorized procedure no court has
struck down, and every active judge participates in the vote to take
cases en banc. It does not assert the Ninth Circuit's current active-judge
headcount (vacancies change the number; 29 is the authorized strength).
It does not rule on the merits of splitting the circuit, and it treats the
Harvard Law & Policy Review's ideological math about limited panels as
analysis, not fact.
