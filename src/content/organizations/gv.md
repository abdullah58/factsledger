## Overview

GV is the venture capital arm of [Alphabet](/organizations/alphabet/), the parent company of [Google](/organizations/google/). Founded as Google Ventures in 2009 by Bill Maris, it invests its parent's money independently — from seed rounds to growth-stage deals — across consumer technology, enterprise software, life sciences and frontier technology, and it has backed some of the best-known startups of the past fifteen years, including [Uber](/organizations/uber/), Nest, Slack and GitLab ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/ai/venture-goog-munichiello-hulme-gv/)).

This profile is about Alphabet's GV, formerly Google Ventures. It is not any other company or product using the initials "GV", and it is not CapitalG — Alphabet's separate growth-stage fund, launched as Google Capital in 2014. The distinction matters because GV's defining feature is its independence: Alphabet is its sole limited partner, yet GV makes its own investment decisions and has repeatedly backed companies that compete directly with Alphabet's own products ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/ai/venture-goog-munichiello-hulme-gv/)).

As of July 2025, GV had $10 billion in assets under management and a 21-partner team led by chief executive and managing partner David Krane, headquartered in Silicon Valley with offices in Cambridge, Massachusetts, New York and [London](/places/london/) ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/ai/venture-goog-munichiello-hulme-gv/)).

## Founding and the independence model

Google launched Google Ventures on March 31, 2009, with a $100 million commitment for its first year, at the depth of the financial crisis — the founders' announcement called the downturn an "ideal time" to back entrepreneurs ([TechCrunch](/organizations/techcrunch/)). Bill Maris and Rich Miner, two Google executives, ran the fund as managing partners, with David Drummond, Google's senior vice president of corporate development and chief legal officer, overseeing it ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2009/04/04/the-google-ventures-cheat-sheet/)). Google was the fund's sole limited partner, and all of Google's smaller-scale venture investing was routed through it; larger strategic deals stayed with Google's corporate development team ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2009/04/04/the-google-ventures-cheat-sheet/)).

From the start, the pitch to founders was that GV would invest for financial return, not for Google's strategic benefit. Maris and Miner promised a "one-way mirror" policy — GV could look into Google, but Google could not look into portfolio companies without asking — and said the overriding investment criterion would be return on investment ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2009/04/04/the-google-ventures-cheat-sheet/)). The first two portfolio investments were Pixazza, an image-advertising startup, and Silver Spring Networks, a smart-grid company ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2009/04/04/the-google-ventures-cheat-sheet/)).

The model scaled fast. By 2013 the fund was investing $300 million a year, and it had made more than 300 investments by the end of 2015 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/08/22/google-ventures-puts-258m-into-uber-its-largest-deal-ever/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/12/07/google-ventures-rethinks-its-international-investment-strategy/)). Its early bets ranged from ride-services company Uber and genetics-testing firm 23andMe to commercial drones and 3D printing ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/google-ventures-ceo-bill-maris-quits)).

## The Uber bet and the landmark exits

GV's defining early deal came in August 2013, when it led Uber's $258 million Series C alongside TPG Growth — the largest check GV had ever written, equal to about 86% of its $300 million annual fund ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/08/22/google-ventures-puts-258m-into-uber-its-largest-deal-ever/)). The round valued Uber at about $3.4 billion before the money and $3.76 billion after it, and was led by GV general partner David Krane; a meeting with Google chief executive Larry Page sealed the deal, and Google's David Drummond joined Uber's board ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/08/22/google-ventures-puts-258m-into-uber-its-largest-deal-ever/)). GV counts Uber among its largest exits ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/ai/venture-goog-munichiello-hulme-gv/)).

The second landmark exit arrived even sooner. In January 2014, Google agreed to buy Nest Labs — the smart-thermostat maker GV had backed — for $3.2 billion in cash ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1288776/000128877614000003/a9911132013.htm)). The Nest deal illustrated the awkward side of GV's position: only six of its roughly 320 investments had ever been acquired by Google ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/google-ventures-ceo-bill-maris-quits)). Its investment in online retailer Jet.com also paid off when Walmart agreed to acquire the company for about $3 billion in 2016 ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/google-ventures-ceo-bill-maris-quits)).

## Rebrand, succession, and going global

GV opened a London office in July 2014, setting aside an initial $100 million to invest in startups across Europe, but the European arm stayed quiet, making only a handful of investments in its first year ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/12/07/google-ventures-rethinks-its-international-investment-strategy/)). In December 2015, Maris told the *Financial Times* that the fund would abandon separate regional funds and pool its investments into a single global vehicle from January 2016, giving it more flexibility to back the best founders regardless of location ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/12/07/google-ventures-rethinks-its-international-investment-strategy/)).

The same month, Google Ventures rebranded as GV, in part to put clearer distance between the fund and its parent — by then reorganised as Alphabet — and to counter the perception of a conflict of interest ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/12/07/google-ventures-rethinks-its-international-investment-strategy/)). Maris was blunt about the accusation that GV was a secret arm of Google: "There is not a single case that supports that mythology. The company gets no information from us, we're a shareholder just like any other" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/12/07/google-ventures-rethinks-its-international-investment-strategy/)). The rebrand also coincided with a strategic shift: GV dialled down seed investing and turned toward more mature companies, making just 34 investments in 2015 compared with 63 in 2014, according to CB Insights ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/google-ventures-ceo-bill-maris-quits)).

Leadership changed the following year. In August 2016, Maris stepped down as chief executive after close to eight years running the firm he had founded, to take a break with his family and "tackle something new"; managing partner David Krane replaced him ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/google-ventures-ceo-bill-maris-quits)). Krane had been at Google for nearly 17 years, starting as the company's first communications director ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/google-ventures-ceo-bill-maris-quits)). A visible marker of the Krane era came in October 2020, when GV promoted Terri Burns to partner — making her the firm's first Black woman partner and its youngest-ever partner ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/30/new-gv-partner-terri-burns-has-a-simple-investment-thesis-gen-z/)). Burns had joined GV in 2017 as a principal after product roles at Twitter and Venmo, and built her investing thesis around Gen Z founders and mobile consumer companies ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/30/new-gv-partner-terri-burns-has-a-simple-investment-thesis-gen-z/)).

## The operating model: Design Sprints

What set GV apart from a conventional venture fund was not only its capital but an in-house team of designers, engineers, marketers and recruiters who worked directly with portfolio companies. Its best-known tool was the Design Sprint, a five-day process developed by GV design partner Jake Knapp, drawing on methods from IDEO and Stanford's d.school ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/10/23/inside-a-google-ventures-design-sprint/)). Day one is spent understanding the problem, day two sketching solutions, day three deciding which idea is best for the user, day four building a prototype, and day five testing it with real users — compressing work that would normally take a startup six months into a single week ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/10/23/inside-a-google-ventures-design-sprint/)).

[CircleUp](/organizations/circleup/) is the worked example — and the reason GV's relationship with it runs deeper than a single cheque. GV participated in CircleUp's $7.5 million Series A in May 2013, a round led by [Union Square Ventures](/organizations/union-square-ventures/) ([AllThingsD](http://allthingsd.com/20130507/circleup-raises-7-5m-led-by-union-square-ventures/)). Months later, GV's Design Studio ran a sprint with CircleUp, the crowdfunding platform for consumer brands, and founder [Ryan Caldbeck](/people/ryan-caldbeck/) said the process taught his team to think differently about design: "Ninety percent of what we used came from the process we went through with the GV Design Studio," and the sprint produced a complete redesign of CircleUp's core page for evaluating private companies ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/10/23/inside-a-google-ventures-design-sprint/)). The Design Studio ran 27 design sprints and 28 user-research sprints for portfolio companies in 2013 alone ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/10/23/inside-a-google-ventures-design-sprint/)).

## The AI-native turn

Since 2023, GV's investing has tilted decisively toward artificial intelligence. In December 2023 it hired Michael McBride as a general partner to back AI and open-source startups; McBride had spent five and a half years as chief revenue officer at GitLab, itself a former GV portfolio company ([Global Venturing](https://globalventuring.com/corporate/corporate-venturer/mcbride-joins-gv-as-a-general-partner/)). GV invests about $1 billion a year and had managed about $8 billion at that date, with more than 400 active portfolio companies ([Global Venturing](https://globalventuring.com/corporate/corporate-venturer/mcbride-joins-gv-as-a-general-partner/)).

The AI bets that followed show the independence model operating at full stretch. In 2024 GV led the Series C in AI legal-tech startup Harvey at a $1.5 billion valuation; the company has since raised further rounds at a $5 billion valuation ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/ai/venture-goog-munichiello-hulme-gv/)). GV co-led OpenEvidence's Series B at a $3.5 billion valuation and Lawhive's $40 million Series A, and invested in Thinking Machines Lab's $2 billion seed round at a $12 billion valuation — a company led by Mira Murati, formerly [OpenAI](/organizations/openai/)'s chief technology officer, that competes directly with Alphabet's Gemini ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/ai/venture-goog-munichiello-hulme-gv/)). At the infrastructure layer it led a $30 million seed round in Modular, which is building a universal compiler to rival Nvidia's CUDA ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/ai/venture-goog-munichiello-hulme-gv/)). GV's own site now organises its investing around AI applications, healthcare and AI drug discovery, developer tools and security, and AI infrastructure ([GV](https://www.gv.com/)).

The throughline from 2009 is unchanged: one limited partner funding a team that invests as if it had no parent — backing Slack against Google's own product, and Thinking Machines Lab against Gemini today. As managing partner Dave Munichiello put it, GV can have "30 people going in 30 different directions, investing in 30 different things" without driving consensus ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/ai/venture-goog-munichiello-hulme-gv/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is GV?**
GV is the venture capital arm of Alphabet Inc., Google's parent company. Founded as Google Ventures in 2009 and renamed GV in 2015, it invests Alphabet's money in startups from seed to growth stage, making its own decisions independently of Google's product businesses.

**Who founded GV?**
Bill Maris founded Google Ventures in 2009 and ran it as chief executive for close to eight years, alongside co-managing partner Rich Miner. Maris stepped down in August 2016 and was succeeded by managing partner David Krane, who remains chief executive.

**What are GV's most famous investments?**
Its landmark bets include leading Uber's $258 million Series C in 2013, backing Nest before Google acquired it for $3.2 billion in 2014, and early investments in Slack and GitLab. More recent bets include Harvey, OpenEvidence, Thinking Machines Lab and Modular.

**How is GV different from CapitalG?**
Both are Alphabet investment vehicles, but GV is the venture arm: it invests from seed to growth stage with Alphabet as its sole limited partner. CapitalG (formerly Google Capital, launched in 2014) is the separate growth-stage fund for later, larger rounds.

**Did GV invest in CircleUp?**
Yes. GV participated in CircleUp's $7.5 million Series A in May 2013, led by Union Square Ventures. GV's Design Studio also ran a five-day Design Sprint with CircleUp later that year, redesigning the platform's core page for evaluating private companies.
