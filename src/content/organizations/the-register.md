## Overview

The Register is a British technology news website founded in [London](/places/london/) in 1994 by journalists Mike Magee and John Lettice. Known to readers as "El Reg", it covers enterprise technology in a tabloid voice under the motto "Biting the hand that feeds IT", and says it reaches roughly 40 million readers worldwide ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/profile/about_the_register)).

It is the flagship title of the Situation Publishing family, with journalists in America, Europe, Asia-Pacific and Britain — "the sun never sets on El Reg" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/profile/contact); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/profile/about_the_register)). Its readers are technology professionals, IT decision makers, C-suite executives and software developers, and its coverage spans hardware, software, AI and cloud services, plus off-duty articles on space, electronics, tech culture and feats of engineering ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/profile/about_the_register)). What sets it apart is not just what it covers but how: it was built to be the irreverent, unofficial voice of the IT industry, and its scoops have repeatedly forced the industry's biggest companies to admit things they would rather have kept quiet ([SemiAccurate](https://www.semiaccurate.com/2024/08/12/industry-pioneer-mike-magee-has-passed-away/)).

## From a pub idea to a profitable vulture

The Register began, by its co-founder's own account, in a pub. Mike Magee covered computer chips and John Lettice covered software, and one day they decided to start an email newsletter because the chip industry alone was worth about $200bn a year and "nobody else seems to be doing much about it" ([Press Gazette](http://pressgazette.co.uk/archive-content/how-online-journalism-got-its-uk-start/)). The early years were painful: the two worked on the newsletter in their spare time over a dial-up connection, though the subscriber list grew quickly once they posted about it on Usenet and CIX ([Press Gazette](http://pressgazette.co.uk/archive-content/how-online-journalism-got-its-uk-start/)).

The newsletter made no money, and Lettice was thinking of quitting when, in early 1997, Drew Cullen — an editor at VNU — joined them and found investment from Linus Birtles ([Press Gazette](http://pressgazette.co.uk/archive-content/how-online-journalism-got-its-uk-start/)). For a time the site made money selling news feeds to other publications, but that market collapsed just as it hired more staff, and it pivoted to advertising instead ([Press Gazette](http://pressgazette.co.uk/archive-content/how-online-journalism-got-its-uk-start/)). Cullen later put the cost of the journey bluntly: "We spent well over £1m before we became profitable in 2003" ([Press Gazette](http://pressgazette.co.uk/archive-content/how-online-journalism-got-its-uk-start/)).

In about 2001 the founders split, with Magee leaving to start the rival publication The Inquirer, which was later sold to VNU ([Press Gazette](http://pressgazette.co.uk/archive-content/how-online-journalism-got-its-uk-start/)). Magee died in August 2024; his obituary in the trade press remembered The Register of his era as "the only real source for industry news of an… err… unofficial nature" — "unique, irreverent, and a must read" ([SemiAccurate](https://www.semiaccurate.com/2024/08/12/industry-pioneer-mike-magee-has-passed-away/)).

## Scoops that moved markets and machines

The Register's most consequential reporting has come when a big company wanted a story buried. Its longest-running example is Forgent Networks' JPEG patent campaign. The Register reported Forgent's June 2002 announcement that it held the "sole and exclusive right" to license US Patent 4,698,672 — a compression patent filed by [Compression Labs](/organizations/compression-labs/) in October 1986, before Forgent acquired the company in 1997 — and that it had persuaded [Sony](/organizations/sony/) to pay $15m for a licence ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2004/04/23/jpeg-patent-holder-renews-royalty-offensive/799817)). The alarm was so widespread that the [ISO](/organizations/iso/) threatened to withdraw its standards blessing from JPEG, which it can only endorse royalty-free, yet Forgent claimed to have netted $90m in licensing royalties ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2004/04/23/jpeg-patent-holder-renews-royalty-offensive/799817)). In April 2004 the site reported Forgent's lawsuit against 30 companies that had rejected its terms, including [JVC](/organizations/jvc/), [Panasonic](/organizations/panasonic/), [Ricoh](/organizations/ricoh/), [Fuji](/organizations/fujifilm/), [Agfa](/organizations/agfa/), [Kodak](/organizations/kodak/), [IBM](/organizations/ibm/), [Dell](/organizations/dell/), [Apple](/organizations/apple/), [HP](/organizations/hp/), [Toshiba](/organizations/toshiba/), [Gateway](/organizations/gateway/), Xerox, Palm, [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/), Macromedia and JASC ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2004/04/23/jpeg-patent-holder-renews-royalty-offensive/799817)). That 2004 report is one of the sources behind this site's profile of [Asure Software](/organizations/asure-software/), the Austin company formerly known as Forgent Networks ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2004/04/23/jpeg-patent-holder-renews-royalty-offensive/799817)).

The same instinct — join the dots the vendor would not — produced the Atom C2000 scoop in February 2017. Cisco had warned that a clock-signal component in routing, optical networking, security and switch products sold before 16 November 2016 was unreliable and could fail after about 18 months, leaving hardware that "will stop functioning, will not boot, and is not recoverable" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/on-prem/2017/02/03/fyi-ticking-time-bomb-fault-will-brick-cisco-gear-after-18-months/1013644)). Cisco declined to name the supplier, but The Register identified the part as an [Intel](/organizations/intel/) Atom C2000 system-on-chip, pointing to Intel errata AVR54 in a January 2017 datasheet ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/on-prem/2017/02/03/fyi-ticking-time-bomb-fault-will-brick-cisco-gear-after-18-months/1013644)).

Its biggest scoop came in January 2018, when The Register broke the news of the Meltdown and Spectre chip flaws. Researchers at [Google](/organizations/google/)'s Project Zero and Graz University of Technology had found the flaws, and Intel and Google planned to disclose them the following week once fixes were ready — but Intel "was forced to disclose the problem Wednesday after British technology site The Register reported it, causing Intel's stock to fall" ([Associated Press](https://www.manufacturing.net/home/news/13120477/ap-explains-whos-affected-by-computer-chip-security-flaw)). Meltdown affected nearly every Intel processor made since the mid-1990s, while Spectre was verified in Intel, AMD and ARM chips, and patches followed for [Microsoft](/organizations/microsoft/) Windows, Apple macOS and Linux ([Associated Press](https://www.manufacturing.net/home/news/13120477/ap-explains-whos-affected-by-computer-chip-security-flaw)).

## Vulture Central: voice, stunts and criticism

The site's voice is its brand. Its journalists call themselves "vultures" and its newsroom "Vulture Central", and in 2001 readers competing to write a corporate anthem sang "we bite the hand that feeds IT" and "Vulture Central on my shirt / I'm proud of 'El Reg'" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/profile/contact); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/Print/2001/09/10/reg_corporate_anthem_competition_results/)). The same anthem entries repeatedly name-checked the BOFH column, the long-running sysadmin satire that became part of the site's identity ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/Print/2001/09/10/reg_corporate_anthem_competition_results/)).

That irreverence extends to stunts. In 2010 the site backed the Paper Aircraft Released Into Space (PARIS) project, in which a paper plane called Vulture 1 — a nod to the masthead — was carried by a helium balloon to about 89,591 feet, nearly 17 miles up, photographed the curve of the Earth and glided back down to be recovered intact ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/9521-world-paper-airplane-snaps-amazing-space-photos.html)). The plane was built by Register writer Lester Haines with Steve Daniels and John Oates from paper straws and stiff paper, with a 3-foot wingspan, as an answer to a Japanese proposal to throw paper planes from the International Space Station: "We thought we could do better, so we did" ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/9521-world-paper-airplane-snaps-amazing-space-photos.html)).

The same tone has drawn criticism. In October 2010, Guardian science correspondent Martin Robbins accused The Register of misrepresenting climate science, saying it had "seriously misrepresented" research by Professor Joanna Haigh of Imperial College London, published in Nature, on solar variation ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2010/10/12/guardian-super-blogger-flames-reg-boffinry-desk/978729)). The Register's headline had read "Much of recent global warming actually caused by Sun", based on Haigh's finding that over 2004–2007 increased solar output caused as much warming as human carbon emissions — and the site refused to amend the article, publishing a same-day response that read, in part, "we DON'T CARE what YOU THINK" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2010/10/12/guardian-super-blogger-flames-reg-boffinry-desk/978729)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is The Register?**
The Register is a British technology news website founded in London in 1994, covering enterprise technology, hardware, software, AI and cloud services for IT professionals ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/profile/about_the_register)). It is the flagship title of Situation Publishing and says it reaches roughly 40 million readers worldwide ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/profile/contact)).

**Who founded The Register?**
Journalists Mike Magee and John Lettice founded it as an email newsletter in 1994 — Magee covering computer chips, Lettice covering software — after deciding over a pub conversation that the $200bn-a-year chip industry deserved its own publication ([Press Gazette](http://pressgazette.co.uk/archive-content/how-online-journalism-got-its-uk-start/)). Magee left in about 2001 to found The Inquirer and died in August 2024 ([Press Gazette](http://pressgazette.co.uk/archive-content/how-online-journalism-got-its-uk-start/); [SemiAccurate](https://www.semiaccurate.com/2024/08/12/industry-pioneer-mike-magee-has-passed-away/)).

**Why is it called "El Reg", and what is the vulture?**
"El Reg" is readers' affectionate nickname for the site, and the vulture is its mascot — the newsroom calls itself "Vulture Central" and its journalists "vultures" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/profile/contact)). Readers sang "we bite the hand that feeds IT" in the site's own 2001 anthem competition, a motto that captures its sceptical, tabloid attitude to the industry it covers ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/Print/2001/09/10/reg_corporate_anthem_competition_results/)).

**What was The Register's biggest scoop?**
Its January 2018 report on the Meltdown and Spectre chip flaws, which forced Intel to disclose the problem days earlier than planned and sent its stock falling ([Associated Press](https://www.manufacturing.net/home/news/13120477/ap-explains-whos-affected-by-computer-chip-security-flaw)). Meltdown affected nearly every Intel processor since the mid-1990s, and Spectre hit Intel, AMD and ARM chips alike ([Associated Press](https://www.manufacturing.net/home/news/13120477/ap-explains-whos-affected-by-computer-chip-security-flaw)).

**Did The Register really fly a paper plane to the edge of space?**
Yes — its PARIS project released Vulture 1, a paper plane, from a helium balloon at about 89,591 feet on 28 October 2010, photographed the Earth's curve and recovered it intact ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/9521-world-paper-airplane-snaps-amazing-space-photos.html)). The £8,000 project was built by Lester Haines, Steve Daniels and John Oates as a riposte to a Japanese plan to throw paper planes from the ISS ([Space.com](https://www.space.com/9521-world-paper-airplane-snaps-amazing-space-photos.html)).
