## Overview

Cat Rock Capital Management LP is a long-term focused investment firm based in Connecticut, founded in 2015 by Alex Captain, a former Partner at Tiger Global Management ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/cat-rock-capital-releases-presentation-on-grubhub-and-just-eat-takeaway.com-2021-11)). It manages capital for pension funds, endowments, foundations and other institutional investors, and invests in a select number of high-quality companies with a long-term approach built on deep fundamental research ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/cat-rock-capital-releases-presentation-on-grubhub-and-just-eat-takeaway.com-2021-11)). The firm is best known publicly for two very different things: leading [Booksy](/organizations/booksy/)'s $70 million Series C in January 2021, and a multi-year activist campaign as a large shareholder in Just Eat Takeaway.com, where it pressed the company to sell Grubhub ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/27/booksy-raises-70m-war-chest-to-acquire-salon-appointment-apps-expand-internationally/), [Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/Cat-Rock-activist-investor-calls-just-eats-grubhub-purchase-a-capital-allocation-mistake/622621/)).

The combination is unusual enough to state plainly: Cat Rock is not a venture capital firm. Its normal business is concentrated investing in public companies, holding a small number of positions for years and engaging with management when it thinks strategy is destroying value. The Booksy investment — a private, growth-stage bet on a beauty-booking platform — is the exception in its public record, not the rule. Understanding the firm means understanding the public-markets activism first, and the Booksy lead as a single, deliberate departure from it.

## A concentrated, long-term model

Cat Rock's own description of itself is short and has barely changed across its releases: a long-term focused investment firm that "seeks to invest in a select number of high-quality companies, with a long-term approach that emphasizes deep fundamental research" ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/cat-rock-capital-releases-presentation-on-grubhub-and-just-eat-takeaway.com-2021-11)). The model is concentration plus patience: few positions, large stakes relative to the fund, and multi-year holding periods. That structure explains both halves of the firm's public record. A concentrated holder cannot quietly sell a position it disagrees with — the stake is too big to exit without moving the price — so disagreement becomes public engagement instead. And a firm that does deep fundamental research on a handful of companies is well placed to spot a private company, like Booksy, whose economics it already understands from studying its public peers.

Captain's background fits the model. Before founding Cat Rock in 2015, he was a Partner at Tiger Global Management, the investment firm known for concentrated, research-intensive bets on internet and technology companies ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/cat-rock-capital-releases-presentation-on-grubhub-and-just-eat-takeaway.com-2021-11)). Cat Rock's public record begins in food delivery, a sector Captain had studied for years: by November 2021 the firm said it had "spent over six years researching the online food delivery sector" and had been a shareholder of Just Eat Takeaway.com and its predecessor companies for over four years ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/cat-rock-capital-releases-presentation-on-grubhub-and-just-eat-takeaway.com-2021-11)). That depth of sector work — six years on one industry — is the practical meaning of the firm's "deep fundamental research" claim.

## The Just Eat campaign, 2018–2022

Cat Rock's engagement with Just Eat began as pressure to move faster. In December 2018, when it owned about 2% of the company, it urged Just Eat to consider selling non-core assets — including its minority stake in Brazilian startup iFood, which it said would free up $755 million — and to produce a three-year plan targeting at least 20% organic annual growth ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/just-eat-under-pressure-from-activist-investor/544592/)). Then in January 2020 it criticised the competition watchdog's investigation into Takeaway.com's £6 billion merger with Just Eat, calling the inquiry's apparent equivalence between that merger and Amazon's investment in Deliveroo a false one, and calling for a speedy review ([Business Insider](https://www.insider.co.uk/news/just-eats-captain-delivers-broadside-21348898)). The merger went ahead, and Cat Rock held stakes on both sides of it.

The campaign's sharpest phase began after Takeaway.com — now Just Eat Takeaway.com, or JET — bought Grubhub in June 2020. In October 2021, Cat Rock, by then holding approximately 14.0 million shares, about 6.5% of JET, published a presentation and letter urging the company to sell or spin off Grubhub by the end of the year ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/cat-rock-capital-releases-presentation-on-grubhub-and-just-eat-takeaway.com-2021-11)). Its argument was valuation, not operations: JET's stock had underperformed the MSCI World Index by 69% in the sixteen months since the Grubhub deal was announced, which Cat Rock said implied the market was assigning a negative €14 billion of enterprise value to the Grubhub acquisition — more than double its €6.5 billion purchase price. The firm argued Grubhub itself was valuable — over 32 million customers generating about $10 billion of sales, more than 300,000 restaurant partners, and a logistics network of about 200,000 couriers delivering across more than 4,000 US cities — and that its value would be realised only inside a US-focused owner ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/cat-rock-capital-releases-presentation-on-grubhub-and-just-eat-takeaway.com-2021-11)). It also put the US same-day delivery addressable market at $2.6 trillion, growing 79% a year since 2017 — Cat Rock's own calculation, not an independent market sizing ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/cat-rock-capital-releases-presentation-on-grubhub-and-just-eat-takeaway.com-2021-11)).

By April 2022 the tone had hardened into a governance fight. Cat Rock, now holding 14.8 million shares, published a letter advising shareholders to vote against JET's chief financial officer and the legacy supervisory board at the annual general meeting on May 4 ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/Cat-Rock-activist-investor-calls-just-eats-grubhub-purchase-a-capital-allocation-mistake/622621/)). The firm claimed that since JET bought Grubhub, its market value had declined 75%, a loss of over $17 billion, and alleged that JET leadership had misled shareholders about the profitability outlook before the votes that approved the deal — shareholders had been led to expect 2021 EBITDA close to a $429 million increase, the firm said, before JET disclosed in early 2021 that EBITDA would instead be a loss of $64 million to $85 million ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/Cat-Rock-activist-investor-calls-just-eats-grubhub-purchase-a-capital-allocation-mistake/622621/)). "JET management made a capital allocation mistake when it decided to buy Grubhub in June 2020 with minimal synergies only two months after tripling the Company's size with the Just Eat acquisition," Captain wrote in the letter ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/Cat-Rock-activist-investor-calls-just-eats-grubhub-purchase-a-capital-allocation-mistake/622621/)). The letter landed days after JET told shareholders it was considering a partial or full sale of Grubhub, as well as a strategic partnership ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/Cat-Rock-activist-investor-calls-just-eats-grubhub-purchase-a-capital-allocation-mistake/622621/)) — the outcome Cat Rock had demanded six months earlier.

## Leading Booksy's $70 million Series C

Three months before the Grubhub campaign went public, Cat Rock made its most visible private investment. In January 2021 it led Booksy's $70 million Series C, with [Sprints Capital](/organizations/sprints-capital/) participating and [OpenOcean](/organizations/openocean/), [Piton Capital](/organizations/piton-capital/), [VNV Global](/organizations/vnv-global/), [Enern](/organizations/enern/), [Kai Hansen](/people/kai-hansen/), Zach Coelius and Manta Ray Ventures also joining; the round took Booksy's total raised to $119 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/27/booksy-raises-70m-war-chest-to-acquire-salon-appointment-apps-expand-internationally/)). Captain's comment at the time framed the bet in the firm's usual language: "We are incredibly excited to invest in Booksy as it builds the leading global software platform for digitizing the beauty and wellness industry around the world" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/27/booksy-raises-70m-war-chest-to-acquire-salon-appointment-apps-expand-internationally/)). The funding was earmarked for North American expansion, new verticals and further acquisitions — including the roll-up of local booking players that Booksy had already begun with Lavito in 2018 and Versum in December 2020 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/27/booksy-raises-70m-war-chest-to-acquire-salon-appointment-apps-expand-internationally/)).

One discrepancy in the record is worth stating rather than smoothing over: TechCrunch reports the post-Series C total raised as $119 million, while Global Venturing reports "more than $116m" ([Global Venturing](https://globalventuring.com/blog/2021/01/29/booksy-slots-in-70m/)). The gap is not resolved in the sources, and it is the same discrepancy recorded in the Booksy profile. The round itself — $70 million, led by Cat Rock — is not in dispute.

For Cat Rock, the Booksy lead is best read as the firm's research model applied to a private company in a sector it understood: local-services marketplaces with network effects, the same structure as food delivery. A beauty-booking platform with 13 million consumers and a subscription model for salons is, analytically, a cousin of the delivery businesses Cat Rock had spent six years studying. The difference is that in Booksy the firm was a lead investor helping to fund expansion, while in JET it was an outside shareholder demanding a sale — two sides of the same conviction that capital allocation, not just operations, decides whether a marketplace business creates value.
