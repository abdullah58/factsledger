## Overview

The Senate most people picture — all 100 members solemnly debating — is rarely what the floor actually looks like. The Constitution requires a majority of senators, 51 when there are no vacancies, to do business, yet the Congressional Research Service notes it is "unusual for as many as 51 Senators to be present on the floor at the same time unless a roll call vote is in progress" — typically only a handful are there ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/96-452)). The chamber gets away with this through a standing fiction: it presumes a quorum is always present unless someone challenges it ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/96-452)). Any senator can "suggest the absence of a quorum," starting a roll call that is almost always cut short by unanimous consent before it finishes — a pause button senators use to negotiate off the record, wait for a colleague to reach the floor, or work through a disagreement ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/96-452)). Only a "live" quorum call, such as the one required before a cloture vote, actually summons senators to record their presence; if a majority still fails to answer, the Senate's options are to adjourn or to direct the Sergeant at Arms to compel attendance ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/96-452)).

A second unwritten lever sits in the Judiciary Committee: the blue slip. Since 1917 the committee's chairman has sent a blue form to each home-state senator of a judicial nominee, asking for approval or objection ([Senate Judiciary Committee](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/press/rep/commentary/grassley-op-ed-senate-democrats-are-trying-to-stall-trumps-nominations-by-rewriting-the-history-of-blue-slips)). How much a withheld slip matters has swung with each chairman: James Eastland imposed a strict no-hearing-without-two-positive-slips rule in 1956, Ted Kennedy reverted to case-by-case treatment in 1979, and Pat Leahy resurrected the strict version in 2001 ([Senate Judiciary Committee](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/press/rep/commentary/grassley-op-ed-senate-democrats-are-trying-to-stall-trumps-nominations-by-rewriting-the-history-of-blue-slips)). The practice's stated purpose, in Chairman Chuck Grassley's account, is to gather home-state insight and push the White House to consult before nominating — not, as he puts it, to hand a single senator a veto ([Senate Judiciary Committee](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/press/rep/commentary/grassley-op-ed-senate-democrats-are-trying-to-stall-trumps-nominations-by-rewriting-the-history-of-blue-slips)).

And the chamber keeps one fixture stocked with sugar. Since 1965, one desk on the Republican side — last row, on the aisle, beside the chamber's busiest entrance — has been kept full of candy ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/art-artifacts/decorative-art/furniture/senate-chamber-desks/candy-desk.htm)). The custom began when Senator George Murphy of California, newly arrived in the Senate and recovering from vocal cord surgery, kept hard candy lozenges in his drawer and shared them with colleagues; since he left in 1971, the senators assigned that spot have carried it on, with more than 19 keepers to date and each occupant recorded by the Secretary of the Senate's curatorial staff since 1985 ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/art-artifacts/decorative-art/furniture/senate-chamber-desks/candy-desk.htm)).

Beneath these quirks sits the institution the textbooks describe. The Senate is the 100-member upper chamber of Congress: two senators from each state, serving six-year terms, with roughly a third of the seats up for election every two years ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/origins-foundations/senate-and-constitution.htm); [U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/filibusters-cloture.htm)). It is the smaller, more deliberative half of the legislature by design — and it holds a set of powers the House does not: confirming the president's nominees, approving treaties, and trying impeachments ([U.S. Senate](https://www.Senate.gov/about/powers-procedures.htm)).

As of September 2026, Republicans hold the majority in the 119th Congress: 53 Republicans, 45 Democrats, and 2 independents — Bernie Sanders of Vermont and Angus King of Maine — who both caucus with the Democrats, making the working split 53–47 ([Financial Express](https://www.financialexpress.com/world-news/us-news/us-mid-term-elections-2026-why-democrats-need-to-flip-4-senate-seats-to-take-back-the-majority/4324950/)). Majority Leader John Thune of South Dakota runs the floor for the Republicans; Charles E. Schumer of New York leads the Democrats ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/senators/leadership.htm)).

## How the Senate is built

The framers wanted a check on the House's passions, so they built the Senate small and slow. Each state gets exactly two senators — a rule the Constitutional Convention approved unanimously — and each senator votes as an individual, not as part of a state bloc ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/origins-foundations/senate-and-constitution.htm)). The six-year term was adopted on June 26, 1787, by a 7–4 vote, and the first Senate divided its members into three classes by lot in 1789 so the whole chamber would never turn over at once ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/origins-foundations/senate-and-constitution.htm)).

Senators were not always chosen by voters. Until 1913, state legislatures picked them — a system that produced bribery scandals and seats left empty for years by deadlocked legislatures ([U.S. Senate](http://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/briefing/Direct_Election_Senators.htm)). The Seventeenth Amendment changed that: Connecticut's approval on April 8, 1913, supplied the required three-fourths majority of states, and 1914 brought the first elections in which every senator was chosen by popular vote ([U.S. Senate](http://www.senate.gov/artandhistory/history/common/briefing/Direct_Election_Senators.htm)).

The Constitution sets a low bar for entry and the voters do the rest: a senator must be at least 30 when taking office ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48535/R48535.1.pdf)). In the 119th Congress the youngest member was Jon Ossoff, born in 1987 and 37 when the Congress began, and the oldest was Chuck Grassley, born September 17, 1933 — who also serves as president pro tempore ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48535/R48535.1.pdf)). Since 1789, 2,018 people have served as senators out of 12,585 who have served in Congress overall ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48535/R48535.1.pdf)).

## What the Senate alone can do

Article II, Section 2 gives the Senate its most visible job: advice and consent. Ambassadors, Supreme Court justices, and other federal officers take office only after the Senate confirms them, and treaties need a two-thirds vote to be approved — though the president can also make executive agreements that skip the Senate entirely ([U.S. Senate](https://www.Senate.gov/about/powers-procedures.htm)).

The Senate also has the sole power to try impeachments. Since 1789 it has tried 20 federal officials, including three presidents ([U.S. Senate](https://www.Senate.gov/about/powers-procedures.htm)). Its disciplinary record runs long: Congress has declared war 11 times, the first in 1812 and the last during World War II; the Senate has censured 9 of its own members and expelled 15 ([U.S. Senate](https://www.Senate.gov/about/powers-procedures.htm)). It has been running investigations since 1792 ([U.S. Senate](https://www.Senate.gov/about/powers-procedures.htm)).

One constitutional quirk shapes every close vote: the vice president is the Senate's president and presiding officer, with the sole power to cast a tie-breaking vote, and presides over the counting of presidential electoral ballots ([U.S. Senate](http://www.senate.gov/about/officers-staff/vice-president.htm)). In the 119th Congress that is [J.D. Vance](/people/jd-vance/) ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/senators/leadership.htm)).

## How debate ends: the filibuster and cloture

For its first 128 years, the Senate had no way to force an end to debate — which is how the filibuster, a term dating to the 1850s, became the chamber's signature weapon ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/filibusters-cloture.htm)). In 1917 the Senate adopted Rule 22, letting two-thirds of senators vote to end debate and force a vote ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/filibusters-cloture.htm)). In 1975 it lowered that bar to three-fifths of all senators duly chosen and sworn — 60 votes in the full 100-member Senate, the number that still defines whether most legislation can move ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/filibusters-cloture.htm)).

The thresholds step up from there: treaties, impeachment convictions, and expulsions need two-thirds, and the vice president votes only when the chamber is evenly split ([U.S. Senate](https://www.Senate.Gov/about/powers-procedures/voting.htm)). Day to day, senators vote by roll call, voice vote, and unanimous consent ([U.S. Senate](https://www.Senate.gov/about/powers-procedures.htm)).

## The 119th Congress: who runs it and what it did

The 119th Congress convened on January 3, 2025, with John Thune taking over as majority leader from Mitch McConnell and Chuck Grassley becoming president pro tempore; John Barrasso serves as majority whip and Richard J. Durbin as Democratic whip ([U.S. Senate](https://www.senate.gov/senators/leadership.htm); [Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/PDF/R48535/R48535.1.pdf)).

The summer of 2026 was busy on the floor, and the Senate's own roll-call record tells the story in numbers. On August 7 the chamber confirmed an en bloc nomination package 51–47 and passed the Russia sanctions bill H.R. 5334 by 86–11 ([U.S. Senate](http://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_lists/vote_menu_119_2.htm)). The next day it passed continuing appropriations for fiscal year 2027 (H.R. 6500) by 90–6 — funding federal agencies into early December, about two months before the September 30 fiscal-year end — and confirmed [Todd Blanche](/people/todd-blanche/) as Attorney General by 50–49 ([U.S. Senate](http://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_lists/vote_menu_119_2.htm); [Associated Press](https://gmg-ksat-prod.cdn.arcpublishing.com/news/politics/2026/08/08/senate-approves-funding-bill-to-avoid-a-shutdown-before-the-election/?ref=%2Fpins%2Fp%2Fe4aeedea-216c-4c8c-a2b2-348f4c852679%2F)).

September brought a split result on digital assets: cloture on the digital-commodities bill H.R. 3633 failed 49–50 on September 15, while the Protect College Sports Act (S. 4668) — the student-athlete name/image/likeness bill — cleared cloture 74–25 on September 24 and passed 77–22 on September 28; three district judges were confirmed between September 15 and 23 ([U.S. Senate](http://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_lists/vote_menu_119_2.htm); [Senate Commerce Committee](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/press/rep/release/senate-overwhelmingly-passes-protect-college-sports-act/)). Thune used September floor time to press the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act, pointing to the 2025 GENIUS Act — which his office describes as "the first bill regulating digital assets ever to become law" — and a 49 percent rise in stablecoin market capitalization in 2025 ([Office of Senator John Thune](https://www.thune.senate.gov/news/press-releases/thune-on-the-clarity-act-regulatory-certainty-is-key-to-american-leadership-in-digital-assets/)).
