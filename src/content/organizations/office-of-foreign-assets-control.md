## Overview

The Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) is the office of the [U.S. Department of the Treasury](/organizations/us-department-of-the-treasury/) that administers and enforces U.S. economic sanctions against targeted foreign jurisdictions and regimes, as well as individuals and entities engaged in harmful activity such as terrorism, narcotics trafficking, and weapons-of-mass-destruction proliferation ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). It was formally created in December 1950, after China entered the Korean War, and its principal instrument is the Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons (SDN) List, whose thousands of entries U.S. persons are generally barred from dealing with ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). Sanctions most commonly work by barring targets from the U.S. financial system and from transactions with U.S. persons — a mechanism that reaches far beyond banks, as the technology company [Cloudflare](/organizations/cloudflare/) discovered when it self-disclosed possible violations to OFAC in 2019 ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477333/000095012319006180/filename1.htm)).

## History: from wartime asset controls to a sanctions agency

Treasury's involvement in sanctions predates OFAC by more than a century: its own history traces the practice to before the War of 1812, when Treasury Secretary Albert Gallatin administered sanctions against Great Britain over the harassment of American sailors ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). OFAC's direct predecessor was the Office of Foreign Funds Control, established at the start of World War II after the German invasion of Norway in 1940, whose initial purpose was to prevent Nazi Germany from seizing the foreign-exchange and securities holdings of occupied countries; after the [United States](/places/united-states/) entered the war it blocked enemy assets and prohibited trade and financial transactions with the Axis powers ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). OFAC itself was formally created in December 1950, following China's entry into the Korean War, when President Truman declared a national emergency and blocked all Chinese and North Korean assets subject to U.S. jurisdiction ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). Today OFAC sits within Treasury's Office of Terrorism and Financial Intelligence; Andrea Gacki was named its permanent director in September 2018 after serving as acting director since May of that year ([U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sm486)). An earlier Treasury announcement named Adam Szubin as director at a time when the office administered roughly 30 sanctions programs ([U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/hp39)).

## How OFAC sanctions work

OFAC administers dozens of sanctions programs, most promulgated under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), with the Trading with the Enemy Act underlying the Cuba program ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). Each program addresses different foreign-policy and national-security goals, and the prohibitions differ accordingly. OFAC describes five tools its programs employ, singly or in combination:

| Tool | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| List-based sanctions | Block the property of, or prohibit dealings with, specifically listed individuals and entities |
| Government or regime sanctions | Block the property of a foreign government or regime, or prohibit dealings with it |
| Jurisdiction-based sanctions | Prohibit dealings with an entire country or region, e.g. Cuba or North Korea |
| Sectoral sanctions | Target persons operating in identified sectors of a foreign economy, e.g. defense or finance |
| Secondary sanctions | Target non-U.S. persons, such as foreign banks, for dealings with blocked persons |

The principal list is the SDN List, which contains thousands of names of designated individuals and entities worldwide, plus identified blocked property such as aircraft and vessels; U.S. persons are generally prohibited from transactions with SDN-listed persons and must block any of their property in their possession or control ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). OFAC also maintains non-SDN lists that prohibit specific activities rather than blocking property outright. Because not every sanctioned person is separately listed, OFAC applies a "50 Percent Rule": entities owned 50 percent or more, directly or indirectly, by one or more blocked persons are themselves treated as blocked even if they never appear on a list ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)).

Compliance is mandatory for all persons subject to U.S. jurisdiction — U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents wherever they are, everyone and every entity within the United States, and U.S.-incorporated entities and their foreign branches — and some programs extend prohibitions to foreign entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). OFAC encourages a risk-based compliance program built on five components — management commitment, risk assessment, internal controls, testing and auditing, and training — with screening of customers, counterparties, and transactions as the most common control ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). When a match is confirmed, the required response is one of three actions: block (freeze) the property, reject the transaction, or proceed only if the activity is exempt or authorized by an OFAC license ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). Licenses come in two forms: general licenses, publicly issued and self-executing for all who meet their conditions, and specific licenses, granted case by case to named persons for named transactions ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). Blocking and rejected transactions must be reported to OFAC within 10 business days, and records must be kept for 10 years ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)).

## Enforcement in practice

The teeth of the system are civil penalties imposed on a strict-liability standard: in many cases a U.S. person can be held civilly liable for a sanctions violation even without knowledge or reason to know the activity was prohibited ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). As of January 15, 2025, the maximum civil monetary penalty for each apparent violation of IEEPA was $377,700, adjusted annually for inflation, and OFAC may bring an enforcement action for IEEPA- or TWEA-based violations within 10 years of the latest violation ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). The counterweight is voluntary self-disclosure: a company that discloses an apparent violation may receive a 50 percent reduction in the base amount of any proposed civil penalty ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)). The largest demonstration of the stakes came in 2014, when BNP Paribas pleaded guilty to conspiring to violate IEEPA and the Trading with the Enemy Act after admitting it had moved more than $8.8 billion through the U.S. financial system between 2004 and 2012 on behalf of Sudanese, Iranian, and Cuban sanctioned entities; total penalties reached $8.9736 billion, including a $963 million fine levied by OFAC itself ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/bnp-paribas-agrees-plead-guilty-conspiring-process-transactions-through-us-financial)).

The scale of the modern program is clearest in the Russia sanctions imposed after February 2022: by February 2023 OFAC had added over 2,500 Russia-related targets to the SDN List — about 2,400 individuals and entities, 115 vessels, and 19 aircraft — and over 80 percent of Russia's banking sector by assets was under U.S. sanctions, including the top ten Russian-owned banks ([U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1298/)). The same fact sheet records that all 450 members of the Russian State Duma and all 170 members of the Federation Council had been sanctioned, along with 47 Russian governors ([U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1298/)). Sanctions also end: on June 30, 2025, Executive Order 14312 revoked the six executive orders behind the comprehensive [Syria](/places/syria/) sanctions program, and OFAC removed 518 individuals and entities from the SDN List the same day, while designating 139 others affiliated with the former Assad regime under a new accountability program ([U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0183)). The technology sector's exposure surfaced in Cloudflare's 2019 IPO process, when the company told the [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](/organizations/us-securities-and-exchange-commission/) it had submitted a voluntary self-disclosure to OFAC on May 24, 2019, identifying a small number of user accounts involving services to parties from [Iran](/places/iran/) or Syria that may not have been covered by OFAC's general licenses for internet-based communications; it said it believed the services in those countries were otherwise authorized, that any payments were de minimis, and that it had terminated the accounts on discovery ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477333/000095012319006180/filename1.htm)). [Sudan](/places/sudan/), the letter noted, had not been under comprehensive U.S. sanctions since 2017 ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477333/000095012319006180/filename1.htm)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is OFAC?**
OFAC is the Office of Foreign Assets Control, the office of the U.S. Department of the Treasury that administers and enforces U.S. economic sanctions against targeted countries, regimes, and individuals and entities engaged in terrorism, narcotics trafficking, or weapons proliferation ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)).

**What is the SDN List?**
The Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List is OFAC's principal sanctions list, containing thousands of designated individuals, entities, and blocked property worldwide; U.S. persons are generally barred from dealing with anyone on it and must block their property ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)).

**Who has to follow OFAC sanctions?**
Everyone subject to U.S. jurisdiction — U.S. citizens and permanent residents anywhere, all persons and entities inside the United States, and U.S.-incorporated entities and their foreign branches — and, under some programs, foreign entities owned or controlled by U.S. persons ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)).

**Can a company be penalized if it did not know it was violating sanctions?**
Yes. OFAC civil penalties are generally strict-liability: a U.S. person can be liable even without knowledge of the violation, though a voluntary self-disclosure can cut the base penalty by 50 percent ([OFAC, U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/935656/download?inline=)).

**Did OFAC lift sanctions on Syria?**
Yes, in substance. Executive Order 14312 of June 30, 2025 revoked the comprehensive Syria sanctions program, and OFAC removed 518 individuals and entities from the SDN List — while keeping sanctions on Bashar al-Assad, his associates, and other destabilizing actors under a new program ([U.S. Department of the Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0183)).
