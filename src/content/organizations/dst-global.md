## Overview

DST Global is the late-stage internet investment firm Yuri Milner built from a 2009 Facebook bet that Silicon Valley first called absurdly overpriced: about 2% of the company for $200 million, taken without a board seat, more than two years before its IPO. That deal announced a new kind of investor — one that bought scale, not control — and DST repeated the formula at Twitter, Spotify, Alibaba, Flipkart and Wish, before returning to AI-era deals as a backer of [Anysphere](/organizations/anysphere/)'s $900 million Series C and $2.3 billion Series D ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/05/cursors-anysphere-nabs-9-9b-valuation-soars-past-500m-arr/); [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/biggest-funding-rounds-ai-defense-tech-anysphere/)). Its history is inseparable from its founder's: a Moscow physicist turned internet entrepreneur who first built Russia's Mail.ru, then split its international investing into a separate fund ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768); [ICIJ](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)).

## The playbook: late, large, and hands-off

Two mechanics made DST distinctive, and both are visible in its first two landmark deals. First, Milner refused the perks other investors treated as prerequisites — above all a board seat. He told Forbes the approach "sent a strong message that we are not seeking any influence over Facebook's operations," which let DST invest in competing companies without creating conflicts of interest ([ICIJ](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). Second, DST bought big chunks of secondary stock — shares sold by employees and early investors — rather than only funding the company itself. TechCrunch, reporting Twitter's 2011 round, called this "something of a DST speciality": by letting insiders cash out, DST reduced the internal pressure on a company to go public ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2011/08/01/twitter-confirms-significant-new-funding-round-led-by-dst/)). The Facebook maths vindicated the model fast. The initial $200 million stake, bought when the price was widely considered wildly overvalued, had swelled to about $1 billion by 2010, and DST's total $800 million Facebook investment was worth about $5 billion by 2011 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). Four days after Facebook's May 2012 IPO, a DST Global subsidiary sold more than 27 million shares for roughly $1 billion, according to SEC filings cited by the ICIJ ([ICIJ](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). Twitter followed the same script: a round led by DST Global in August 2011, reported at $800 million in total — $400 million of new money and $400 million to cash out employees and investors — at an $8 billion valuation, with Milner calling Twitter "one of the few companies that has truly changed the world" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2011/08/01/twitter-confirms-significant-new-funding-round-led-by-dst/)).

## A portfolio replicated across continents

What separates DST from a lucky two-deal firm is that it ran the same playbook across geographies. Milner had cofounded NetBridge in 1999, run Mail.ru as chief executive from 2001 to 2003, and formed Digital Sky Technologies in 2005, which took control of Mail.ru and invested about $1 billion in more than two dozen businesses by 2011 ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1930768)). In September 2010 the group rebranded: the operating wing became Mail.ru Group and listed in London, while the investment fund became DST Global ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/entrepreneur/yuri-milners-dst-global-raises-1-7b-for-its-fifth-fund-without-russian-money)). The fund sequence that followed — DST Global II in 2011 at about $1 billion, III in 2012, IV in 2014, and V in 2015 at $1.7 billion — carried the firm into Airbnb, Spotify, Twitter, Zalando and Rocket Internet, into Chinese e-commerce giants Alibaba and JD.com (then 360buy.com), and into India, where it led a $210 million round for Flipkart in 2014 ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/entrepreneur/yuri-milners-dst-global-raises-1-7b-for-its-fifth-fund-without-russian-money)). The Wish deal of June 2015, a $500 million round led by DST at a $3 billion valuation, showed the template still working a decade after the firm's origins: a fast-growing, later-stage private company, a very large cheque, and no demand for control ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/06/03/sources-dst-global-led-mobile-shopping-app-wishs-500m-round/)). Fund V also marked a quiet break: unlike earlier DST funds, it had no Russian backers, according to a source quoted by VentureBeat, and it was registered in the Cayman Islands ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/entrepreneur/yuri-milners-dst-global-raises-1-7b-for-its-fifth-fund-without-russian-money)).

## Scrutiny, divestment, and the return to AI-era deals

The firm's past caught up with it in November 2017, when the Paradise Papers investigation by the ICIJ, The New York Times and the BBC showed that VTB Bank, a Russian state-controlled bank, had directed $191 million into a DST Global vehicle used to buy a large Twitter stake in 2011, and that a Gazprom Investholding-funded offshore company, Kanton Services, had partnered with DST Global in the Facebook investment ([ICIJ](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). Milner's response, carried by the ICIJ and PitchBook, was that the investments were made on business merits, that VTB was a passive investor with no influence over which companies DST chose, that less than 5% of the firm's funding came from Russian government institutions, and that he had not known of the Gazprom link until investigators contacted him ([ICIJ](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/); [PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/beyond-twitter-and-facebook-a-closer-look-at-yuri-milners-investments)). The ICIJ itself stated there was no indication the Kremlin gained influence over Twitter or Facebook or received inside information from the investments, and noted DST had sold its Twitter stake before the United States sanctioned VTB in 2014, while Facebook emphasised DST had no voting rights or board seat and had sold all its Facebook holdings ([ICIJ](https://www.icij.org/investigations/paradise-papers/kremlin-owned-firms-linked-major-twitter-facebook-investments-icij/)). The episode did not end the firm: a decade later DST Global participated in Anysphere's $900 million Series C in June 2025 at a $9.9 billion valuation, led by Thrive Capital, and backed the $2.3 billion Series D in November 2025 at a $29.3 billion post-money valuation — the same late-stage, large-cheque posture, applied to the AI coding boom ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/05/cursors-anysphere-nabs-9-9b-valuation-soars-past-500m-arr/); [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/biggest-funding-rounds-ai-defense-tech-anysphere/)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not present the Twitter round's $800 million size or $8 billion valuation as company-confirmed figures: Twitter called the round only "significant," and the numbers are contemporary press reports. It does not treat Fund V's lack of Russian backers as independently confirmed: that rests on one unnamed source quoted by VentureBeat. It does not assert DST Global's current assets under management, its full office list, or the size of any fund after Fund V, none of which appear in the sources read in full. And it does not claim the Kremlin gained influence over Facebook or Twitter: the ICIJ investigation itself found no such indication, and Milner's account — passive investors, no control — is reported as his response, not as an independently verified fact.
