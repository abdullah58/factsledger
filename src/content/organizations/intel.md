Intel's story reads as two different companies. The first built the chips that powered the personal computer, grew from a July 1968 startup into the company that named the "Wintel" era, and for decades treated dominance as the natural order of things ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). The second is a company that missed the smartphone wave, watched rivals eat its lead, delayed its flagship Ohio factories to 2030, and in August 2025 ended up partly owned by the U.S. government after federal grants were converted into stock ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel); [AFP](https://techxplore.com/news/2025-03-intel-bn-chip-factory-ohio.html?deviceType=mobile)). The Ohio arc ties the two halves together: it began as a $20 billion bipartisan ribbon-cutting moment and became a case study in how hard it is to rebuild a chip industry on a deadline.

## Origins: the experienced founders

Intel was founded in July 1968 by American engineers Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). Unlike the archetypal Silicon Valley startup born in a garage, Intel opened its doors with $2.5 million in funding arranged by Arthur Rock, the financier who coined the term "venture capitalist" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). The name came from *int*egrated *el*ectronics, and the company set up in Santa Clara, California ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

The founders were middle-aged technologists with established reputations, not first-timers ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). Noyce had co-invented the silicon integrated circuit in 1959 while general manager of Fairchild Semiconductor ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). Moore had headed research and development at Fairchild, and in 1965 had made the prediction — later called Moore's law — that transistor counts on chips would double about every year, revised in 1975 to every two years ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). Immediately after founding Intel, Noyce and Moore recruited other Fairchild employees, including Hungarian-born businessman Andrew Grove, and the three men served as chairman and CEO in succession across the company's first three decades ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

## The memory chips and the 4004

Intel's first products were memory chips, including the 1101 — the world's first metal oxide semiconductor, which sold poorly — and its sibling the 1103, a one-kilobit DRAM that became the first chip to store a significant amount of information ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). Honeywell bought the 1103 in 1970 to replace core memory in its computers, and DRAMs quickly became the standard memory in computers worldwide ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

The bigger breakthrough came in 1971, when Intel engineers Ted Hoff, Federico Faggin, and Stan Mazor invented a general-purpose four-bit microprocessor — the 4004, one of the first single-chip microprocessors — under contract to the Japanese calculator maker Nippon Calculating Machine Corporation, which let Intel retain all rights to the technology ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). That same year Intel went public and introduced the EPROM chip, its most successful product line until 1985 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

Not everything worked: in 1972 management bought the digital watch company Microma, never understood the consumer business, and sold it in 1978 at a $15 million loss ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). By then, Intel was already shifting its attention from memory to microprocessors — the 8008 in 1972, the 8080 two years later, and the first 16-bit chip, the 8086, in 1978 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

## The "Wintel" decades

In 1981, IBM chose Intel's 16-bit 8088 to be the CPU in its first mass-produced personal computer, and Intel also supplied chips to makers of PC "clones" compatible with IBM's product ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). IBM had contracted a small Redmond firm, Microsoft, to provide the disk operating system for its PC, and the pairing of Windows software with Intel chips — dubbed "Wintel" machines — went on to dominate the market ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

The most important chip of the era was the 80386, a 32-bit processor released in 1985 that began Intel's commitment to backward compatibility across all future microprocessors ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). In 1993, Intel dropped number-based naming for the trademarked Pentium, the first Intel PC chip to use parallel "superscalar" processing, with 3.1 million transistors against its predecessor's 1.2 million ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). Combined with Microsoft's Windows 3.x, the much faster Pentium spurred a major expansion of the PC market ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

Dominance had its costs. The so-called Pentium flaw — an obscure segment of the chip's transistors performing division incorrectly — was discovered by engineers after release and initially kept quiet; when mathematician Thomas Nicely independently found it and IBM said it would not ship computers with the CPU, Intel was forced into a recall that cost $475 million ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). In 2009, the European Union fined Intel $1.45 billion for alleged monopolistic actions, and Intel paid AMD $1.25 billion the same year to settle a decades-long dispute in which AMD accused it of pressuring PC makers not to use its chips ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

By the end of the century, Intel or compatible chips were in every PC except Apple's Macintosh — and in 2005, Apple CEO Steve Jobs announced future Apple PCs would use Intel CPUs, closing even that gap ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

## The stumble

Even as Intel looked strong from the outside, strain was showing: smartphone makers bypassed its chips, manufacturing delays piled up, and AMD began regaining ground in the PC market ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). Paul Otellini succeeded Craig Barrett as CEO in 2005, Brian Krzanich took over in 2013, and CFO Bob Swan became CEO in 2019 — the year Intel ranked 43rd on the Fortune 500 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

In 2021, Pat Gelsinger, a former Intel executive and VMware CEO, returned as chief executive pledging to restore the company's technology leadership ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). The revival did not take: delays continued, competition intensified, and Gelsinger was forced out in December 2024 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). Intel reported a record $18.8 billion net loss for fiscal 2024 ([AFP](https://techxplore.com/news/2025-03-intel-bn-chip-factory-ohio.html?deviceType=mobile)). Since 2023 the company recorded losses exceeding $22 billion and planned to lay off more than 20,000 workers, while its market capitalization fell to roughly $108–110 billion — over 60 percent below its peak, and dwarfed by rival [NVIDIA](/organizations/nvidia/), which surpassed $4 trillion in July 2025 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

## The Ohio arc: from $20 billion announcement to 2030

On January 21, 2022, Intel announced it would invest $20 billion in two chip factories — fabs — on a 1,000-acre site in Licking County, just east of [Columbus](/places/city-of-columbus-ohio/), during a global chip shortage ([AP](https://techxplore.com/news/2022-01-intel-20b-ohio-chip-facility.amp)). The two plants were expected to create 3,000 company jobs — many highly skilled — plus 7,000 construction jobs, with tens of thousands more across suppliers and partners ([AP](https://techxplore.com/news/2022-01-intel-20b-ohio-chip-facility.amp)). Construction was to begin that year, with production coming online at the end of 2025 ([AP](https://techxplore.com/news/2022-01-intel-20b-ohio-chip-facility.amp)).

President Joe Biden used the announcement at the White House to push a $52 billion bill for the chip sector, with CEO Gelsinger, Commerce Secretary Gina Raimondo, and Ohio's two senators — Democrat Sherrod Brown and Republican Rob Portman — appearing together after Biden spoke ([AP](https://techxplore.com/news/2022-01-intel-20b-ohio-chip-facility.amp)). Intel executives made clear the size of the Ohio complex would depend on passage of the federal subsidies the Biden administration and Ohio lawmakers were seeking ([AP](https://techxplore.com/news/2022-01-intel-20b-ohio-chip-facility.amp)). Brown worked with local and state leaders and other members of Ohio's congressional delegation to make sure Intel chose Ohio, and was instrumental in moving the CHIPS Act through the Senate ([Ohio AFL-CIO](https://www.ohioaflcio.org/blog/senator-sherrod-brown-leads-efforts-to-pass-chips-act)). The CHIPS and Science Act became law in 2022, and Biden later finalized a $7.9 billion grant award to Intel ([AFP](https://techxplore.com/news/2025-03-intel-bn-chip-factory-ohio.html?deviceType=mobile)).

Then the timeline collapsed. In March 2025, Intel pushed Ohio production to 2030 — years behind the original end-of-2025 target ([AFP](https://techxplore.com/news/2025-03-intel-bn-chip-factory-ohio.html?deviceType=mobile)).

## The government becomes a shareholder

In March 2025 Intel named Lip-Bu Tan as CEO ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). In August that year, President Donald Trump publicly called for Tan's resignation, labeling him "highly conflicted" over his investment ties to Chinese tech companies; after Tan reaffirmed his commitment to U.S. interests in a letter to employees and met Trump at the White House, the president reversed himself and praised Tan as a respected leader ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

Days later came the move that redefined Intel's relationship with Washington: the U.S. government converted $11.1 billion in federal grants and incentives — primarily from the 2022 CHIPS and Science Act and related programs — into 433.3 million shares of nonvoting stock valued at $20.47 each, about a 10 percent stake, making the federal government one of Intel's largest shareholders with no voting rights or board representation ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)). Industry outlet Data Center Dynamics put the deal's total at $8.9 billion — $5.7 billion in unpaid CHIPS grants plus $3.2 billion from the Secure Enclave program — with a warrant for 5 percent more stock and no board seat ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/us-government-takes-10-stake-in-intel/)). Officials described the transaction as a strategic effort to bolster domestic semiconductor manufacturing rather than a bailout, though analysts debated whether government ownership threatened market independence and corporate governance ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Intel)).

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not claim Donald Trump personally co-sponsored the Ohio project with Sherrod Brown — Brown's role was the January 2022 announcement under the Biden administration and his work on the CHIPS Act; the Trump-era development was the August 2025 grant-to-equity conversion, described separately above.
- It does not claim the Ohio fabs are operational or have produced any chips — Intel pushed production to 2030.
- It does not claim Intel invented the integrated circuit alone — Britannica credits Noyce as co-inventor of the silicon integrated circuit in 1959.
- It does not quote a current share price or market value beyond the dated figures from the read sources.
- It does not claim the government stake was a bailout — officials described it as a strategic manufacturing investment, and analysts are divided.
