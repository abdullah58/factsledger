## Overview

*TV Guide* was supposed to tell you what was on television. What it ended up doing, fall after fall, was documenting what America was — and what it was willing to become. The magazine's Fall Preview issue, the showcase edition that arrived each autumn with the new TV season, became "a barometer of society," as journalist Steve Lipman's 2026 survey of seven decades of Fall Previews puts it — but a barometer held by particular hands [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). Syracuse professor Robert Thompson told Lipman that the magazine "reflected the people in charge" — "large middle- and upper-class 'very, very white' men" — who produced "a product that was 'very conservative and completely asexual,'" because "they didn't want to offend advertisers" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). No general history of *TV Guide* leads with this; they all lead with the founding date. The diary is the more important story.

## America's diary, one fall at a time

The first Fall Preview issue, in 1953, was 32 stapled-together pages measuring 5¼ by 8¼ inches and cost 15 cents. All the dozens of stars' faces were white — with one exception, Louise Beavers, star of "Beulah" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). In 1956, the magazine finally showed a Black face — "Two, actually. Both, of Louis Armstrong" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). The early issues were "crammed with profiles of well-known celebrities" and "with cigarette ads" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2).

Then the country's changes started arriving as programming notes. In 1968 "Julia," starring Diahann Carroll, premiered — depicting, as the magazine put it, "a contemporary Negro living in a white environment" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). In 1969, "Room 222" starred Lloyd Haynes as a Black teacher, and *TV Guide* wrote: "This is no longer a TV novelty" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). In 1981, "Love, Sidney" starred Tony Randall as a gay commercial artist — "Sidney's sexual orientation does not figure in the series' story lines" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). In 1997, when Ellen DeGeneres came out on her sitcom, *TV Guide* handled it "matter-of-factly": "Now that she's out of the closet, what's next?" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). In 2014, the magazine introduced "Transparent" by describing its lead as someone "whose decision to live truthfully as a woman inspires [his family members] to reevaluate their own happiness" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). A parallel timeline ran in the advertising columns: cigarette ads out in 2000, a NicoDerm patch ad in 2002, Salem cigarettes back in 2004, out again by 2016 [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2).

## The founding

The magazine itself was founded in 1953 by publisher Walter Annenberg [University of Pennsylvania](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/annenberg-school-for-communications-library-archives-celebrates-100-years-of-television). The inaugural edition, dated April 3–9, 1953, put a "chubby-cheeked, blue-eyed, consternated-looking" Desi Arnaz Jr. — the infant son of Lucille Ball and Desi Arnaz — on the cover under the headline "Lucy's $50,000,000 baby" [University of Pennsylvania](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/annenberg-school-for-communications-library-archives-celebrates-100-years-of-television). Annenberg's publisher's message inside the front cover explained the premise: "television's growing importance in our daily lives brought a need for accurate and complete station schedules printed in convenient form" [University of Pennsylvania](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/annenberg-school-for-communications-library-archives-celebrates-100-years-of-television). The first issue carried listings alongside a Walter Winchell photo series, a humor column on "three-D" films, and a review of "Robert Montgomery Presents" — the template of listings-plus-stories that held for decades [University of Pennsylvania](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/annenberg-school-for-communications-library-archives-celebrates-100-years-of-television).

The magazine's own body tracked the industry's expansion. In 1980 it began carrying listings for cable and pay-TV channels [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). In 1981 the Fall Preview was "for the first time, bound like the pages in a book, not stapled" at 5 by 7½ inches [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). By 1989, "in recognition of the growing number of programs offered on network and cable, TV Guide starts printing multiple Fall Preview issues" [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2). The small digest size it had kept for 52 years was retired in October 2005; in 2006 the magazine moved to a larger 8-by-10½-inch stapled format [Ultimate Classic Rock](http://ultimateclassicrock.com/unforgettable-tv-guide-covers/); [Medium](https://medium.com/@sl5738/u-s-history-as-reflected-in-the-pages-of-tv-guide-3f27286986a2).

## The $1 sale — and the patents that stayed behind

By the late 2000s the business was in free fall: circulation had dropped from a peak of about 20 million copies per week around 1980 to roughly 3.2 million, and "as cable channels proliferated, TV Guide magazine actually reduced the number of listings it carried — an admission, essentially, that TV listings had become unwieldy for print" [EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/tv-guide-mag-given-away-as-epgs-rule/?_ga=piddl_msgorder%3Dasc). On October 13, 2008, Macrovision sold the money-losing magazine to Beverly Hills equity fund OpenGate Capital for $1 and a $9.5 million loan [EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/tv-guide-mag-given-away-as-epgs-rule/?_ga=piddl_msgorder%3Dasc). The price told the story in reverse: Macrovision had paid $2.8 billion for the whole company earlier that year, and the company had changed hands for $9.2 billion in 2000 [EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/tv-guide-mag-given-away-as-epgs-rule/?_ga=piddl_msgorder%3Dasc). The $1 was for the shell — Macrovision kept TVGuide.com and the notorious EPG patent portfolio, because "the real value of the business... are patent licensing and the electronic delivery of listings to set top boxes, TV sets and other devices" [EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/tv-guide-mag-given-away-as-epgs-rule/?_ga=piddl_msgorder%3Dasc). OpenGate assumed roughly $85 million in subscription obligations [EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/tv-guide-mag-given-away-as-epgs-rule/?_ga=piddl_msgorder%3Dasc).

The turnaround nobody remembers came next. OpenGate's own account says it acquired the magazine from Rovi in late 2008 "at a time when the publication had been under performing for years and was facing deep challenges due to the rapid decline in the print advertising market" — and that within 12 months, cost realignment and editorial improvements "guided the business back to a state of continual profitability" [OpenGate Capital](https://opengatecapital.com/news/opengate-capital-completes-sale-of-tvguide-magazine-and-tv-insider-to-ntvb-media/). In early 2015 it launched TV Insider, a digital site for behind-the-scenes coverage [OpenGate Capital](https://opengatecapital.com/news/opengate-capital-completes-sale-of-tvguide-magazine-and-tv-insider-to-ntvb-media/).

On October 7, 2015, OpenGate sold *TV Guide Magazine* and TV Insider to NTVB Media — "the third change in ownership for the legendary TV and entertainment publication in a decade" [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/tv-guide-magazine-sold-to-ntvb-media/). The magazine, at 1.8 million paid copies, remained "one of the country's most successful print entertainment platforms, trailing only People"; NTVB's regional TV Weekly reached a million subscribers through partnerships with more than 160 newspapers, and the combined operation reached over 20 million consumers [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/tv-guide-magazine-sold-to-ntvb-media/). Then the digital brand moved again: in October 2022, Fandom — the fan platform founded by Wikipedia co-founder Jimmy Wales — acquired the TV Guide brand along with GameSpot, Metacritic and four others from Red Ventures for about $55 million [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/10/03/fandom-acquires-seven-brands-from-red-ventures-gamespot-tv-guide/).

## The reporting life

Beyond the listings, *TV Guide* was a working entertainment newsroom — one whose coverage other FactsLedger profiles already rely on. [Brett Butler](/people/brett-butler/)'s rise and fall was documented by the magazine in real time: a December 2000 report on her attempted ABC comeback pilot [TV Guide](https://www.tvguide.com/news/chance-brett-butler-46195/), a Televisionary column reconstructing the Chuck Lorre feud and the soda-can cancellation [TV Guide](https://www.tvguide.com/news/question-remember-66847/), and a wide-ranging 2011 interview on her addiction and recovery [TV Guide](https://www.tvguide.com/news/brett-butler-addiction-1030325/). [Bill Prady](/people/bill-prady/)'s *TV Guide* celebrity bio records the details his filmography skips: the 474 votes he received in California's 2003 recall election, the origin of Sheldon's "Soft Kitty" lullaby in his daughter's preschool teacher, and his start writing for Jim Henson's Muppets at age 22 [TV Guide](https://www.tvguide.com/celebrities/bill-prady/bio/3031001721/). The listings died; the reporting outlived them.
