## Overview

Toshiba Corporation is a [Japanese](/places/japan/) industrial conglomerate headquartered in Kawasaki, Kanagawa, whose roots go back to an 1875 telegraph-equipment shop and whose modern form dates to a 1939 merger ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). It invented NAND flash memory, once led the world in laptops and memory chips, and was taken private in December 2023 after an accounting scandal, a nuclear bankruptcy and the sale of its memory business ([Reuters](https://www.hl.co.uk/news/reuters-toshiba-delisted-after-74-years-faces-future-with-new-owners)).

The name itself is a contraction of its 1939 identity, Tokyo Shibaura Electric — **To**kyo **Shiba**ura Denki — and the company only officially adopted the Toshiba name in 1978 ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). Today it is a maker of batteries, chips, and nuclear and defence equipment, employing around 106,000 people as of December 2023, and is led by chief executive Taro Shimada, who stayed on after the buyout ([Reuters](https://www.hl.co.uk/news/reuters-toshiba-delisted-after-74-years-faces-future-with-new-owners)). Its story is unusual even by Japanese conglomerate standards: one of the country's oldest industrial names, an inventor of a technology that now sits in nearly every phone on earth, brought down in a decade by its own accounting, its nuclear bet and its activist shareholders — and then rebuilt as a private company.

## From telegraphs to a 1939 merger

Toshiba's two roots were both Japanese firsts. Hisashige Tanaka — called the "Edison of Japan" — established Tanaka Seizo-sho in 1875 as Japan's first telegraph equipment shop, while Hakunetsu-sha, founded in 1890 by Ichisuke Fujioka and Shoichi Miyoshi, was Japan's first maker of incandescent lamps ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). The Tanaka line became Shibaura Seisaku-sho in 1904 and developed Japan's first X-ray tubes in 1915; the lamp line became Tokyo Electric in 1899 ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). The two merged in 1939 to form Tokyo Shibaura Electric, and the company's shares were first listed on the Tokyo and Osaka exchanges in 1949, backed by the powerful Mitsui trading group ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)).

Post-war Toshiba collected firsts the way other companies collected patents: Japan's first broadcasting equipment in 1952, its first digital computers in 1954, and its first microwave ovens in 1959 ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). Under president Toshiwo Doko, who took charge in 1965, it controlled 63 subsidiaries and employed more than 100,000 people by 1967, ranking as Japan's largest electronics manufacturer ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). The 1980s made it a semiconductor power: in 1985 it won the global race to develop the first one-megabyte DRAM chip, by 1987 it was producing almost half of the world's one-megabyte chips, and in 1986 it passed Hitachi to become the second-largest semiconductor maker in the world, behind NEC ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). Laptop production began in 1986, and by 1991 Toshiba held more than a fifth of the laptop market ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)).

Success came with scandals. In 1987 Toshiba Machine, a subsidiary half-owned by Toshiba, was found to have sold submarine sound-deadening equipment to the Soviet Union, making Soviet submarines harder to detect and forcing NATO to modernise its anti-submarine equipment ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). Two subsidiary executives were arrested, four top-ranking officials resigned, Japan barred the subsidiary from exporting to the Soviet Union for a year, and on 1 July 1987 Toshiba's president Sugichiro Watari and chairman Shoichi Saba resigned after the U.S. Senate voted to ban imports of Toshiba products for three years ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). A decade later the company paid $1.1 billion in October 1999 to settle a U.S. class action over an allegedly faulty floppy disc drive in more than five million Toshiba laptops — denying liability, but settling because its president feared a jury verdict approaching $10 billion ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). The same year it posted its first net loss in 23 years, ¥13.9 billion ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)).

## The inventor of flash memory — and two format wars

Toshiba's most consequential invention came from one of its own engineers. Fujio Masuoka invented flash memory while working for Toshiba around 1980, presenting NOR flash in 1984 and NAND flash at the IEEE 1987 International Electron Devices Meeting in San Francisco; Toshiba commercially launched NAND flash the same year ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/storage/1987-toshiba-launches-nand-flash/)). The name "flash" was suggested by Masuoka's colleague Sho-ji Ariizumi, because erasing the memory reminded him of a camera flash ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/storage/1987-toshiba-launches-nand-flash/)). [Intel](/organizations/intel/) introduced the first commercial NOR-type flash chip in 1988, but NAND — the denser, cheaper variant that now stores data in phones, cameras and solid-state drives — was Toshiba's ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/storage/1987-toshiba-launches-nand-flash/)). By 2009 [Apple](/organizations/apple/) had signed a long-term agreement for Toshiba to keep supplying it with flash chips for the iPhone era ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/storage/1987-toshiba-launches-nand-flash/)).

Toshiba also played both sides of the great disc-format wars — winning one and losing the next. In 1992 it paid $500 million for a 5.6% stake in Time Warner Entertainment, and the Toshiba–Time Warner DVD format became the industry standard by the mid-1990s, beating a rival format from Sony and Philips; Toshiba introduced the first DVD players and DVD drives for computers in 1996 ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). Thirteen years later it lost the sequel. On 19 February 2008 Toshiba announced it would no longer develop, manufacture or market HD DVD players and recorders, after [Warner Bros.](/organizations/warner-bros/) went Blu-ray-exclusive in January and [Netflix](/organizations/netflix/), Best Buy and Walmart backed Blu-ray ([TWICE](https://www.twice.com/news/toshiba-pulls-plug-hd-dvd-20293)). It aimed to be out of the HD DVD business by the end of March 2008 while continuing product support and its commitment to standard DVD ([TWICE](https://www.twice.com/news/toshiba-pulls-plug-hd-dvd-20293)). President Atsutoshi Nishida put it plainly: "We carefully assessed the long-term impact of continuing the so-called 'next-generation format war' and concluded that a swift decision will best help the market develop" ([TWICE](https://www.twice.com/news/toshiba-pulls-plug-hd-dvd-20293)).

Toshiba was also caught in one of the tech industry's most notorious patent campaigns. On 23 April 2004 Forgent Networks — now [Asure Software](/organizations/asure-software/) — sued 30 companies that had rejected its royalty terms for U.S. Patent 4,698,672, covering compression techniques at the heart of the JPEG image format; Toshiba was named among the PC makers sued, alongside [IBM](/organizations/ibm/), [Dell](/organizations/dell/), Apple, [HP](/organizations/hp/) and [Gateway](/organizations/gateway/) ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2004/04/23/forgent_jpeg_suit/)). Forgent had acquired the patent's owner, [Compression Labs](/organizations/compression-labs/), in 1997, and had already persuaded [Sony](/organizations/sony/) to pay $15 million for a licence in 2002, claiming some $90 million in royalties by 2004 ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2004/04/23/forgent_jpeg_suit/)). The campaign collapsed when the [Public Patent Foundation](/organizations/public-patent-foundation/) got the patent rejected on prior-art grounds; Forgent settled its remaining cases in November 2006 for a reported $8 million from about 30 companies, having collected more than $110 million from over 60 companies in total ([BetaNews](http://betanews.com/article/forgent-settles-jpeg-patent-litigation/)). [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/) was also among the companies sued ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2004/04/23/forgent_jpeg_suit/)).

## Scandal, bankruptcy and going private

The decade from 2015 undid Toshiba as a public company. On 20 July 2015 an independent investigation committee reported that Toshiba had overstated its operating profits by ¥151.8 billion — about $1.22 billion — cumulatively over fiscal years 2008 to 2014, roughly triple the company's initial estimate, driven by managerial pressure to meet targets in the PC, visual products and semiconductor units ([EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/toshiba-ceo-resigns-over-accounting-scandal/?_ga)). Chief executive Hisao Tanaka resigned the next day, along with his predecessor as chief executive, vice chairman Norio Sasaki, and adviser Atsutoshi Nishida ([EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/toshiba-ceo-resigns-over-accounting-scandal/?_ga)). It was described as Japan's worst boardroom scandal since Olympus was found in 2011 to have covered up $1.7 billion in losses ([EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/toshiba-ceo-resigns-over-accounting-scandal/?_ga)).

The nuclear business finished the job. On 29 March 2017 Westinghouse Electric, Toshiba's U.S. nuclear unit, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy after cost overruns at its two American nuclear plant projects, Vogtle 3&4 and Virgil C. Summer 2&3 ([Power Technology](https://www.power-technology.com/comment/commentwestinghouse-bankruptcy-and-its-implications-for-the-nuclear-power-market-5826267/)). The filing listed $4.3 billion in assets against $9.4 billion in liabilities, and Westinghouse obtained $800 million in debtor-in-possession financing to keep operating ([Power Technology](https://www.power-technology.com/comment/commentwestinghouse-bankruptcy-and-its-implications-for-the-nuclear-power-market-5826267/)). Toshiba announced it was closing down its nuclear construction business ([Power Technology](https://www.power-technology.com/comment/commentwestinghouse-bankruptcy-and-its-implications-for-the-nuclear-power-market-5826267/)).

To survive, Toshiba sold its crown jewel. On 1 June 2018 it completed the sale of Toshiba Memory Corporation to a Bain Capital-led consortium — including Apple, SK Hynix, Dell and Seagate — for about ¥2.3 trillion, roughly $18 billion, transferring the shares to K.K. Pangea while retaining about 40% ownership ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2018/06/02/toshiba-memory-business-sale-complete/)). The memory business had been separated from Toshiba in April 2017, and on 1 October 2019 it was renamed Kioxia — a blend of the Japanese word *kioku* (memory) and the Greek word *axia* (value) — to mark its separation from the Toshiba group ([EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/toshiba-memory-re-brands-to-kioxia/?_ga)).

The end of the public era came on 20 December 2023, when Toshiba was delisted after 74 years on the Tokyo exchange and taken private by a consortium led by Japan Industrial Partners, including financial services firm Orix, utility Chubu Electric Power and chipmaker Rohm ([Reuters](https://www.hl.co.uk/news/reuters-toshiba-delisted-after-74-years-faces-future-with-new-owners)). The $14 billion takeover put Toshiba in domestic hands after protracted battles with overseas activist investors, and its shares ended their last trading day at ¥4,590 ([Reuters](https://www.hl.co.uk/news/reuters-toshiba-delisted-after-74-years-faces-future-with-new-owners)). Under the new owners, Toshiba announced a revitalization plan in May 2024: up to 4,000 domestic jobs cut — 6% of its domestic workforce — through early retirement for employees aged 50 and over, the consolidation of its Tokyo and Kawasaki head offices into one in Kawasaki, and a target of a 10% operating profit margin in fiscal 2026 ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/on-prem/2024/05/17/toshiba-to-shed-4000-jobs-as-part-of-revitalization-plan/937564)). The plan also folds four key subsidiaries in energy and infrastructure into "One Toshiba" and directs more resources into EV semiconductors, AI products, renewable energy equipment and quantum technology ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/on-prem/2024/05/17/toshiba-to-shed-4000-jobs-as-part-of-revitalization-plan/937564)). President Taro Shimada called the revamp "a necessary thing to do to ensure the company survives for the next 100 years" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/on-prem/2024/05/17/toshiba-to-shed-4000-jobs-as-part-of-revitalization-plan/937564)). The headquarters move followed: by late August 2025 the executive office and corporate planning divisions had relocated from Shibaura, Tokyo, to Kawasaki, Kanagawa, with the public relations department set to follow, to better align corporate planning with R&D and manufacturing ([CIJ Today](https://cijtoday.com/japanese-manufacturers-relocating-headquarters-central-tokyo/)).

**What is Toshiba?**
Toshiba Corporation is a Japanese industrial conglomerate headquartered in Kawasaki, Kanagawa, with roots in an 1875 telegraph-equipment shop and its modern form dating to a 1939 merger ([Reference for Business](http://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/71/Toshiba-Corporation.html)). It makes energy, infrastructure and electronic-device products, and was taken private in December 2023 after 74 years listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange ([Reuters](https://www.hl.co.uk/news/reuters-toshiba-delisted-after-74-years-faces-future-with-new-owners)).

**Did Toshiba invent flash memory?**
Yes. Fujio Masuoka invented flash memory while working for Toshiba around 1980, and Toshiba commercially launched NAND flash in 1987 after presenting it at the IEEE International Electron Devices Meeting ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/storage/1987-toshiba-launches-nand-flash/)). The name "flash" was suggested by Masuoka's colleague Sho-ji Ariizumi, because erasing the memory reminded him of a camera flash ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/storage/1987-toshiba-launches-nand-flash/)).

**Why was Toshiba delisted from the Tokyo Stock Exchange?**
Toshiba was delisted on 20 December 2023 after a $14 billion takeover by a consortium led by Japan Industrial Partners, ending 74 years as a public company ([Reuters](https://www.hl.co.uk/news/reuters-toshiba-delisted-after-74-years-faces-future-with-new-owners)). The buyout followed a decade of upheaval — the 2015 accounting scandal, the 2017 Westinghouse bankruptcy and battles with overseas activist investors ([Reuters](https://www.hl.co.uk/news/reuters-toshiba-delisted-after-74-years-faces-future-with-new-owners)).

**What happened to Toshiba's memory chip business?**
Toshiba sold it to a Bain Capital-led consortium for about $18 billion in June 2018, retaining about 40% ownership, to raise cash after the Westinghouse losses ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2018/06/02/toshiba-memory-business-sale-complete/)). The business, separated from Toshiba in April 2017, was renamed Kioxia Corporation on 1 October 2019 ([EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/toshiba-memory-re-brands-to-kioxia/?_ga)).

**Was Toshiba sued over the JPEG patent?**
Yes. On 23 April 2004 Forgent Networks — now Asure Software — sued 30 companies, including Toshiba, for refusing its royalty terms on U.S. Patent 4,698,672, covering JPEG compression ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2004/04/23/forgent_jpeg_suit/)). Forgent settled its final cases in November 2006 for about $8 million, after collecting more than $110 million from over 60 companies ([BetaNews](http://betanews.com/article/forgent-settles-jpeg-patent-litigation/)).
