## Overview

The Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (RCAP) was a Canadian royal commission established on 26 August 1991 to investigate the relationship between Indigenous peoples, the Government of [Canada](/places/canada/) and Canadian society as a whole. Over five years it heard more than 2,000 people, commissioned over 350 research studies and produced a five-volume, 4,000-page final report, released on 21 November 1996, containing 440 recommendations for restructuring that relationship — most of which were never fully implemented ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://development.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/royal-commission-on-aboriginal-peoples); [Library of Parliament](https://publications.gc.ca/Collection-R/LoPBdP/EB/prb9924-e.htm)).

This profile is about RCAP itself, not the later Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015), which investigated the residential schools system and issued 94 Calls to Action ([Prime Minister of Canada](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/statements/2021/11/21/statement-prime-minister-25th-anniversary-final-report-royal-commission)), and not the National Inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls (2016–2019), whose final report contained 231 Calls for Justice ([Crown-Indigenous Relations and Northern Affairs Canada](https://cirnac.gc.ca/eng/1663183221757/1663183241877)). RCAP's importance lies in its scope: it remains the most comprehensive official examination of Crown–Indigenous relations in Canada, and its ideas shaped reconciliation policy for decades even where its recommendations were shelved ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://development.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/royal-commission-on-aboriginal-peoples)).

## Origins, mandate and hearings

RCAP was created after the Oka Crisis of the summer of 1990. Its terms of reference were developed after consultations led by former Chief Justice Brian Dickson with Indigenous groups and leaders and federal and provincial politicians, and the commission was established by order-in-council on 26 August 1991, with a mandate to study the relationship between Indigenous peoples, the [Government of Canada](/organizations/government-of-canada/) and Canadian society as a whole, and sixteen areas identified for special attention ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://development.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/royal-commission-on-aboriginal-peoples); [Library and Archives Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/indigenous-history/royal-commission-aboriginal-peoples.html)). Seven commissioners were appointed, four Indigenous and three non-Indigenous: co-chairs Georges Erasmus, a former national chief of the Assembly of First Nations, and René Dussault, a Quebec Court of Appeal justice, alongside Viola Robinson, Mary Sillett, Paul Chartrand, Bertha Wilson — a former justice of the [Supreme Court of Canada](/organizations/supreme-court-of-canada/) — and Allan Blakeney, who resigned in April 1993 and was replaced by Peter Meekison ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://development.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/royal-commission-on-aboriginal-peoples)). Its 1993 research plan covered governance, land and economy, social and cultural issues, and the North, and the commission held 178 days of public hearings in 96 communities, with transcripts running from April 1992 to December 1993 ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/royal-commission-aboriginal-peoples-rcap/); [Library and Archives Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/indigenous-history/royal-commission-aboriginal-peoples.html)).

## The report and its recommendations

The final report was released on 21 November 1996 at a ceremony in Hull, Quebec: five volumes, 4,000 pages, 440 recommendations and a 20-year agenda for change ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://development.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/royal-commission-on-aboriginal-peoples); [Library of Parliament](https://publications.gc.ca/Collection-R/LoPBdP/EB/prb9924-e.htm)). Its central conclusion was blunt: the main policy direction pursued for more than 150 years, first by colonial then by Canadian governments, had been wrong, and only a complete restructuring — founded on recognising Aboriginal peoples as self-governing nations with a unique place in Canada — would fix it ([Library of Parliament](https://publications.gc.ca/Collection-R/LoPBdP/EB/prb9924-e.htm)). It traced the relationship through four stages, from contact and trade under the Royal Proclamation of 1763, through domination and assimilation in the 1800s, to a fourth stage of renewal it hoped to begin ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/royal-commission-aboriginal-peoples-rcap/)). Its flagship proposals were constitutional in scale: a new Royal Proclamation and companion legislation, an Aboriginal order of government subject to the Charter of Rights and Freedoms, replacement of the Department of Indian Affairs with two departments, an Aboriginal parliament, and an independent lands and treaties tribunal ([Library of Parliament](https://publications.gc.ca/Collection-R/LoPBdP/EB/prb9924-e.htm)). It also proposed three models of self-government — national, public and community-of-interest — and estimated that 60 to 80 Indigenous nations could be candidates for self-government ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/royal-commission-aboriginal-peoples-rcap/); [The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://development.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/royal-commission-on-aboriginal-peoples)).

## Response, legacy and what was never done

The government did not call the First Ministers Conference the report sought within six months; in December 1996 the Prime Minister said it needed time to study the recommendations, and the Assembly of First Nations answered with a national day of protest in April 1997 ([The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://development.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/royal-commission-on-aboriginal-peoples); [Library of Parliament](https://publications.gc.ca/Collection-R/LoPBdP/EB/prb9924-e.htm)). The formal response came on 7 January 1998 as *Gathering Strength: Canada's Aboriginal Action Plan*, with a Statement of Reconciliation expressing profound regret and a $350-million healing fund for the legacy of residential schools ([Library of Parliament](https://publications.gc.ca/Collection-R/LoPBdP/EB/prb9924-e.htm)). Its ideas nevertheless paved the way for the 2007 Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission and the 2008 apology, and in 2017 the government split the Department of Indian Affairs and Northern Development into two successor departments, as RCAP had recommended ([Prime Minister of Canada](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/statements/2021/11/21/statement-prime-minister-25th-anniversary-final-report-royal-commission)). The [United Nations](/organizations/united-nations/) Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act has since come into force, while the Aboriginal parliament and the new Royal Proclamation remain unbuilt ([Prime Minister of Canada](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/statements/2021/11/21/statement-prime-minister-25th-anniversary-final-report-royal-commission); [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/royal-commission-aboriginal-peoples-rcap/)).

The report called for spending to rise to $1.5 billion by Year 5 and $2 billion over the following 15 years ([Library of Parliament](https://publications.gc.ca/Collection-R/LoPBdP/EB/prb9924-e.htm)) — a scale of investment no government ever approached, which is why the report is now cited more as a research landmark than as a plan that was carried out.

## FAQ

### What was the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples?

It was a Canadian royal commission established on 26 August 1991 to investigate the relationship between Indigenous peoples, the Government of Canada and Canadian society. Over five years it heard more than 2,000 people and commissioned over 350 studies, publishing a five-volume, 4,000-page final report with 440 recommendations in November 1996.

### Who chaired the commission?

Georges Erasmus, a former national chief of the Assembly of First Nations, and René Dussault, a justice of the Quebec Court of Appeal, served as co-chairs. They led seven commissioners — four Indigenous and three non-Indigenous — including former Supreme Court justice Bertha Wilson and Métis lawyer Paul Chartrand.

### What did the RCAP report recommend?

Its central call was a complete restructuring of the Crown–Indigenous relationship, including a new Royal Proclamation, recognition of an Aboriginal order of government, an Aboriginal parliament, an independent lands and treaties tribunal, expanded land bases, and major investment in health, education and housing over a 20-year agenda.

### How is RCAP different from the Truth and Reconciliation Commission?

RCAP (1991–1996) examined the whole Crown–Indigenous relationship and made 440 recommendations across governance, land, treaties and social policy. The TRC (2008–2015) came later, focused specifically on the residential schools system, and issued 94 Calls to Action. RCAP's work helped pave the way for the TRC.

### Was the RCAP report implemented?

Only in part. The government responded in 1998 with Gathering Strength and a $350-million healing fund, and split the Department of Indian Affairs in 2017 as RCAP had urged. But its constitutional proposals — a new Royal Proclamation and an Aboriginal parliament — were never adopted, and most recommendations were never fully implemented.
