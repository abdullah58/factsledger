## Overview

The Associated Bill Posters' Association was the national trade association of bill-posting companies in the [United States](/places/united-states/) and Canada, formed in Chicago on 15 July 1891. It organized a fragmented craft of local poster firms into a national body, set the first industry standards, and — through two renamings and a 1925 merger — became the Out of Home Advertising Association of America, which still represents the industry today ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698); [Out of Home Advertising Association of America](https://www-archive.oaaa.org/AboutOOH/OOHBasics/HistoryofOOH.aspx)).

The association was not the first attempt at a national body. Its predecessor, the International Bill Posters' Association of North America, had been formed in St. Louis in 1872 by 11 bill posters, with John D. Walker as its first president, and its charter aimed to regulate a uniform scale of posting prices and act as a watchdog against the "malicious covering of bills" ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698)). That body declined after 1884 and had disbanded by 1888; the 1891 Chicago meeting reorganized it under a new name and a constitution influenced by the old one ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698); [Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt)). Its stated purpose was to promote greater national recognition and understanding of the organized poster medium, to coordinate the services of member companies across the country, and to address the ethical concerns of the industry's early leaders ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698); [Out of Home Advertising Association of America](https://www-archive.oaaa.org/AboutOOH/OOHBasics/HistoryofOOH.aspx)).

## Formation and the first presidents

The reorganization that created the association took place at Chicago on 15 July 1891, when the old International Bill Posters' Association of North America was reorganized and its policy and name changed to the Associated Bill Posters' Association of the United States and Canada ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt)). Edward A. Stahlbrodt of Rochester, New York, was unanimously elected its first president, and Duke University Libraries records that the association's inaugural meeting marked the first national convention of outdoor advertising professionals ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt); [Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698)). Stahlbrodt, who ran the Rochester Bill Posting Company, was re-elected at the Rochester convention in 1892 and again at the New York convention in 1893 ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt)). Stahlbrodt knew the trade from inside: he had built a Rochester bill-posting business that was consolidated as the Rochester Bill Posting Company in 1889 ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt)).

By 1894 the presidency had passed to R. C. Campbell of Chicago, president and general manager of the American Advertising and Bill Posting Company and treasurer of the Illinois State Bill Posters Association ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard01-1894-11/billboard01-1894-11_djvu.txt)). At the 1894 Philadelphia convention the by-laws were changed to give an Executive Committee full charge of the association's business matters, with Stahlbrodt elected its chairman, and a special convention was called at Cincinnati on 9 January 1895 to decide on opening a permanent office in [New York City](/places/new-york-city/) ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt)). The Cincinnati minutes show Campbell in the chair, J. Ballard Carroll of Albany as secretary, state charters issued for Montana and Pennsylvania, and *Billboard Advertising* adopted as the association's official journal ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt)). A committee of Carroll, B. Lynk, George M. Leonard, Stahlbrodt and Campbell was appointed to plan the New York office ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt)).

## Standards, self-regulation and the trade press

The association's lasting work was standardization. In 1896 it first published its official organ, *The Bill Poster — A Monthly Journal Devoted to Outdoor Advertising*, and its members began work toward uniform structure standards ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698)). The following year it changed its name to the Associated Bill Posters of United States and Canada, was incorporated under New York law, and James F. O'Mealia became president; in 1898 *Display Advertising* and *The Bill Poster* merged into a single journal, *The Bill Poster and Display Advertising*, which became the official organ ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698)). The push for standards accelerated after 1910, when the association's Promotion Bureau issued its first Official Membership List, covering over 3,000 towns and listing each member's rates per sheet for a normal four-week showing, together with a plant rating of A for exceeding association standards, B for meeting them, and C for failing them ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)).

The same period brought controls that advertisers could measure. The association licensed official salesmen, set national standards for outdoor advertising, fixed the numbers of panels sold in each market, and began prescribing how many locations a showing needed to give advertisers adequate, representative coverage in a city ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)). In 1912 the 24-sheet poster was standardized at 8 feet 8 inches by 19 feet 6 inches, the standard sheet at 28 by 41 inches, and the agency commission at 16 2/3 percent; national posting revenues rose by more than 500 percent between 1912 and 1915 ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)). Self-regulation ran alongside: around 1891 the national and state associations agreed to refuse offensive "paper", the earliest recorded censorship by an advertising medium over copy in the United States, and in 1915 the membership voted to stop accepting advertising for spirituous liquors, a policy that stood until 1933 ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698); [Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)).

## Two antitrust cases

The association's tightest controls eventually drew the federal government and its courts. The "Landis Decree" of 1916, in *United States v. Associated Bill Posters and Distributors of U.S. and Canada*, ruled that the association could not limit its membership to one member for each town and city, could not prohibit members from competing against one another within a single market, and could not combine to fix prices for poster displays ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)). The decree was dismissed on appeal in 1922, but the same practices were before the U.S. Supreme Court the following year ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)). In 1923 the U.S. Supreme Court decided *Charles A. Ramsay Co. v. Associated Bill Posters of United States and Canada*, a Sherman Act treble-damages case brought by two advertising solicitors whose licences the association had cancelled in July 1911 ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://law.resource.Org/pub/us/case/reporter/US/260/260.US.501.100.101.html)).

Justice McReynolds, writing for the Court, described how the bill posters of 1891 had formed a voluntary association, afterwards incorporated, whose membership had become very large, and set out the system the plaintiffs alleged: membership restricted to one employing billposter in each town or city, members forbidden to compete with each other, funds furnished to members for buying out competitors, a fixed schedule of prices, and work channelled through twelve arbitrarily selected and licensed solicitors who were forbidden to patronize non-members ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://law.resource.Org/pub/us/case/reporter/US/260/260.US.501.100.101.html)). The Court reversed the dismissal, holding that the alleged nationwide combination was designed to destroy competition and secure a monopoly by limiting commerce in posters to channels its members dictated — exactly what the Sherman Act denounced ([U.S. Supreme Court](https://law.resource.Org/pub/us/case/reporter/US/260/260.US.501.100.101.html)). The association survived both cases. The Landis Decree had barred the one-member-per-town rule outright, though it was dismissed on appeal in 1922 ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)).

## From the Poster Advertising Association to the OAAA

In 1912 the association renamed itself the Poster Advertising Association, Inc., because the term "billposter" had taken on a negative connotation and the poster had largely replaced the advertising bill as the standard outdoor medium ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)). In 1913 its Education Committee chose two posters, "The Birth of Christ" and "The Life of General Grant", for the association's first public-service campaign, displayed from that December ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)). Under that name it kept growing: by 1917 its membership represented over 7,500 cities and towns, and by 1920 it served over 9,000 ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/); [Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1920-1929/)). The first Official Membership List of 1910 had covered more than 3,000 towns, and by 1926 the successor organization served over 15,000 cities and towns — a fivefold expansion in sixteen years that shows how the association's standardization work turned a fragmented craft into a national medium ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/); [Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1920-1929/)).

The end of the name came in 1925, when the Poster Advertising Association merged with the Painted Outdoor Advertising Association to form the Outdoor Advertising Association of America, combining the interests of posters and bulletins into one association ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1920-1929/); [Out of Home Advertising Association of America](https://www-archive.oaaa.org/AboutOOH/OOHBasics/HistoryofOOH.aspx)). Harry O'Mealia was elected the first president of the consolidated organization; his father Joseph had previously been president of the Painted Outdoor Advertising Association ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1920-1929/)). The new body held its first convention in Atlanta in 1926, continued the old association's *Association News* as the *Outdoor Advertising Association News*, and in 2019 renamed itself the Out of Home Advertising Association of America ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1920-1929/); [Out of Home Advertising Association of America](https://www-archive.oaaa.org/AboutOOH/OOHBasics/HistoryofOOH.aspx)). The organization founded in Chicago in 1891 therefore survives today under its fourth name, still tracing its founding to the Associated Bill Posters' Association.

## Frequently asked questions

**When was the Associated Bill Posters' Association founded?**

It was formed on 15 July 1891 at a meeting in Chicago, Illinois, when the older International Bill Posters' Association of North America (founded 1872 in St. Louis) was reorganized and renamed. Edward A. Stahlbrodt of Rochester, New York, was unanimously elected its first president at that meeting ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt); [Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698)).

**Who led the association in its early years?**

Edward A. Stahlbrodt was its first president (1891–1893) and then chairman of its Executive Committee from 1894. R. C. Campbell of Chicago was president in 1894 and chaired the January 1895 special convention in Cincinnati. James F. O'Mealia became president in 1897, when the association incorporated under New York law ([Billboard Advertising](https://archive.org/stream/billboard0203-1895a/billboard0203-1895a_djvu.txt); [Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/?p=10698)).

**What did the association actually do?**

It organized local bill-posting firms into state and national bodies, published official journals, set uniform poster sizes and structure standards, rated member plants A, B or C, licensed official salesmen, and fixed agency commissions. It also practised self-regulation, refusing offensive posters from about 1891 and liquor advertising from 1915 ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/)).

**Is it the same organization as the Outdoor Advertising Association of America?**

Yes, by lineage: the Associated Bill Posters' Association became the Associated Bill Posters and Distributors, then the Poster Advertising Association in 1912, which merged with the Painted Outdoor Advertising Association in 1925 to form the Outdoor Advertising Association of America — renamed the Out of Home Advertising Association of America in 2019 ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1920-1929/)).

**Why did the association face antitrust cases?**

Its one-member-per-town rule, fixed price schedule and licensed-solicitor system kept independents out of the poster trade. The 1916 Landis Decree barred those practices, and the Supreme Court's 1923 Ramsay decision held that the alleged nationwide combination violated the Sherman Act ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1910-1919/); [U.S. Supreme Court](https://law.resource.Org/pub/us/case/reporter/US/260/260.US.501.100.101.html)).

It does not state the year the "Associated Bill Posters and Distributors" name was adopted, because no source read in full gives one. It does not give R. C. Campbell's full first name, which no reliable source states. It does not present the OAAA's compressed "ABPA became OAAA" lineage as the full story: the detailed record shows two intermediate names and a 1925 merger ([Duke University Libraries](https://blogs.library.duke.edu/digital-collections/road/timeline/1920-1929/)). It claims no membership figure for the association in the 1890s, because none was verified, and it does not cover the OAAA's later history beyond the 1925 merger and the 2019 renaming.
