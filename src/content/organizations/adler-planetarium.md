## Overview

The Adler Planetarium is America's first planetarium — a public astronomy museum in [Chicago](/places/chicago/), Illinois, that opened on May 12, 1930, as the first planetarium in the Western Hemisphere. Founded by retired Sears, Roebuck & Co. executive Max Adler with a $500,000 gift, it stands on Northerly Island on Lake Michigan and is operated by the [Chicago Park District](/organizations/chicago-park-district/) ([American Physical Society](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2023/04/adler-planetarium); [Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/picturethis/2026/11/the-drama-of-the-heavens-at-adler-planetarium)).

It is more than a show dome: the Adler is a research museum holding one of the world's most important collections of historic astronomical instruments, and a National Historic Landmark since 1987 ([American Physical Society](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2023/04/adler-planetarium)). Its story runs from a Chicago businessman's gift through the 1933 World's Fair, the Space Race, and a NASA partnership — and through Maude Bennot, the first woman in the world to run a planetarium, who directed it from 1937 to 1945 ([Adler Planetarium](https://www.adlerplanetarium.org/blog/meet-maude-bennot-the-woman-behind-the-adler/)).

## A gift from a Sears executive

Max Adler retired from Sears, Roebuck & Co., and a trip to Germany gave him the idea: in Munich he saw the new Zeiss projection planetarium, the first of its kind, which had opened in 1925 ([Astronomy Magazine](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/may-12-1930-adler-planetarium-opens/)). In 1928 he gave $500,000 to build a planetarium in Chicago, and he also funded the projector and bought a collection of antique scientific instruments ([American Physical Society](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2023/04/adler-planetarium)). Architect Ernest Grunsfeld Jr. designed a 12-sided building on Northerly Island, its form symbolising the months of the year and the signs of the zodiac, and the Adler Planetarium and Astronomical Museum opened on Adler's birthday, May 12, 1930 ([Astronomy Magazine](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/may-12-1930-adler-planetarium-opens/); [National Park Service](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/024f5dd7-04d9-4b04-b051-7145c979f7c3)). At the dedication Adler said he wanted people to "observe the action of the heavenly bodies as heretofore only astronomers could do," and called the planetarium a "classroom under the heavens" for astronomy education for all ([National Park Service](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/024f5dd7-04d9-4b04-b051-7145c979f7c3); [Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/picturethis/2026/11/the-drama-of-the-heavens-at-adler-planetarium)).

## More than a show dome

The Adler's dome was 70 feet across, with a Zeiss projector that could reproduce the night sky as seen from any place on Earth, and its 1930s shows — "Architecture of the Heavens", "The Moon and Its Motions", "The Way of the Planets" — were narrated live by presenters ([Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/picturethis/2026/11/the-drama-of-the-heavens-at-adler-planetarium)). The original collection held about 500 astronomical, navigational and mathematical instruments, and by 2005 it had grown to almost 2,000 artifacts, including the world's oldest known window sundial from 1529 and a telescope made by William Herschel ([Congressional Record](https://www.congress.gov/congressional-record/volume-151/issue-60/senate-section/article/S4862-1)). By 2023 the collection held over 6,000 artifacts, books and manuscripts, including a 12th-century astrolabe and a 1500s celestial sphere punched with over 1,000 stars ([American Physical Society](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2023/04/adler-planetarium)). It worked as a science institution: in 1964 it partnered with the National Science Foundation on the Astro-Science Workshop for Chicago high school students, and in 2005 [NASA](/organizations/nasa/) chose it as education partner for the Interstellar Boundary Explorer mission ([Congressional Record](https://www.congress.gov/congressional-record/volume-151/issue-60/senate-section/article/S4862-1)).

## The woman who ran the Adler

The Adler's first director was the astronomer Philip Fox, formerly of the Dearborn Observatory, whom Max Adler recruited in 1929 — and Fox accepted only on condition that Maude Bennot could be his paid research assistant ([Adler Planetarium](https://www.adlerplanetarium.org/blog/meet-maude-bennot-the-woman-behind-the-adler/)). Bennot, a Northwestern University astronomy graduate who had taught at Dearborn, became director when Fox left in 1937, making her the first woman in the world to run a planetarium — or, as far as is known, any science museum ([Adler Planetarium](https://www.adlerplanetarium.org/blog/meet-maude-bennot-the-woman-behind-the-adler/)). The Chicago Park District, which controlled staffing, never formally recognised her as director, paid her at her old assistant's salary — about two-thirds of what Fox had been offered — and barred her from hiring an assistant of her own ([Adler Planetarium](https://www.adlerplanetarium.org/blog/meet-maude-bennot-the-woman-behind-the-adler/)). In 1945 she was fired, allegedly for "insubordination", and replaced by a less-qualified man; she then left astronomy altogether, according to one historian, and died at 90 ([Adler Planetarium](https://www.adlerplanetarium.org/blog/meet-maude-bennot-the-woman-behind-the-adler/)).

## A landmark on the lakefront

The Adler's building is itself an exhibit: three concentric 12-sided rings rise in tiers to a copper-sheathed dome, with bronze zodiac plaques by the sculptor Alfonso Iannelli, their stars placed after Ptolemy's constellation descriptions ([National Park Service](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/024f5dd7-04d9-4b04-b051-7145c979f7c3)). A dedicatory panel inside carries Adler's motto — order, interdependence and unity under the celestial firmament — flanked by symbols of the eight planets; Pluto, discovered in 1930, arrived too late to be included ([National Park Service](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/024f5dd7-04d9-4b04-b051-7145c979f7c3)). Outside stands a statue of [Nicolaus Copernicus](/people/nicolaus-copernicus/), given by the [Copernicus Foundation](/organizations/copernicus-foundation/) and the Illinois Division of the Polish-American Congress in 1973 ([National Park Service](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/024f5dd7-04d9-4b04-b051-7145c979f7c3)). The site grew in stages: a 280-foot underground addition in 1973 more than doubled the floor space, the Doane Observatory opened in 1977, and the 1999 Sky Pavilion added 60,000 square feet of galleries ([National Park Service](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/024f5dd7-04d9-4b04-b051-7145c979f7c3); [Congressional Record](https://www.congress.gov/congressional-record/volume-151/issue-60/senate-section/article/S4862-1)). It was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1987 and an APS Historic Site in 2019 ([American Physical Society](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2023/04/adler-planetarium)).

The Adler drew more than 1.2 million visitors during the two years of the 1933–34 Century of Progress Exposition ([Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/picturethis/2026/11/the-drama-of-the-heavens-at-adler-planetarium)) — and that early crowd proved the planetarium was a public draw, not a curiosity, which is why it still draws about a half-million visitors a year nearly a century later ([Astronomy Magazine](https://www.astronomy.com/today-in-the-history-of-astronomy/may-12-1930-adler-planetarium-opens/)).

## FAQ

### When did the Adler Planetarium open?

It opened to the public on May 12, 1930 — Max Adler's birthday — on Northerly Island in Chicago. It was the first planetarium in the Western Hemisphere and the first modern planetarium in America, built around a Zeiss projection system Adler had seen in Munich.

### Who founded the Adler Planetarium?

Retired Sears, Roebuck & Co. executive Max Adler founded it. In 1928 he gave $500,000 to build the planetarium in Chicago, funded its Zeiss projector, and bought a collection of antique scientific instruments that became the foundation of its history-of-astronomy collection.

### Why is the Adler Planetarium a National Historic Landmark?

It was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1987 as the first planetarium in the Western Hemisphere, and for its distinctive 12-sided building by Ernest Grunsfeld Jr., with zodiac plaques and a copper-sheathed dome. It was also named an APS Historic Site in 2019.

### Who was Maude Bennot?

Maude Bennot directed the Adler from 1937 to 1945, the first woman in the world to run a planetarium. The Chicago Park District never formally recognised her as director and paid her at an assistant's rate; in 1945 she was fired, allegedly for insubordination, and replaced by a less-qualified man.

### What is in the Adler's collection?

The collection grew from about 500 instruments at founding to over 6,000 artifacts, books and manuscripts by 2023. Highlights include a 12th-century astrolabe, a 1500s filigree celestial sphere, the world's oldest known window sundial from 1529, and a telescope made by William Herschel.
