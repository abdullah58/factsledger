## Overview

In February 2024, the Kremlin did something it had not done since before the invasion of Ukraine: it gave a Western journalist an interview with [Vladimir Putin](/people/vladimir-putin/). The journalist was [Tucker Carlson](/people/tucker-carlson/), and the explanation for why he — of all the Western reporters asking — got the sit-down came the next day at a regular Kremlin briefing. Spokesman [Dmitry Peskov](/people/dmitry-peskov/) said Putin had agreed because Carlson's approach differed from the "one-sided" reporting of Western outlets on the Ukraine conflict, adding that the large Western media "take an exceptionally one-sided position" and that there was no desire to communicate with them ([The Straits Times, carrying Reuters](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/putin-gave-tucker-carlson-an-interview-because-he-differs-from-one-sided-media-kremlin)). Asked directly why Carlson had landed the interview, Peskov said the American journalist's approach was "in no way pro-Russian, it is not pro-Ukrainian – it is pro-American" ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/putin-gave-tucker-carlson-an-interview-because-he-differs-from-one-sided-media-kremlin)).

The White House answered within hours: Putin should not be given "an uncritical outlet to justify his war in Ukraine," and National Security Council spokesperson [John Kirby](/people/john-kirby/) added, "I don't think we need another interview with Vladimir Putin to understand his brutality" ([Al Jazeera, via BIG Media](https://big-media.ca/the-controversy-behind-carlson-interview-of-putin/)). The episode is a compact case study in what the Kremlin is as an institution: a presidential office that rations access, speaks through a single spokesman, and frames its media decisions in the language of grievance against the Western press. The interview itself, granted February 6, 2024, was Putin's first to an American journalist since before he ordered the invasion on February 24, 2022 ([The Straits Times](https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/putin-gave-tucker-carlson-an-interview-because-he-differs-from-one-sided-media-kremlin)).

The interview aired on February 8 on Carlson's website and on X, where he ran his show; Carlson said his objective was to counter what he described as pro-Ukraine "fawning pep sessions" by Western media covering the war ([Al Jazeera, via BIG Media](https://big-media.ca/the-controversy-behind-carlson-interview-of-putin/)). The political fallout was immediate: former Belgian prime minister Guy Verhofstadt called for the European Union to explore a travel ban on Carlson, while former U.S. Representative Adam Kinzinger called him a "traitor" for conducting the interview ([Al Jazeera, via BIG Media](https://big-media.ca/the-controversy-behind-carlson-interview-of-putin/)). Critics also noted the backdrop — two American reporters, Evan Gershkovich of *The Wall Street Journal* and Alsu Kurmasheva of Radio Free Europe, were imprisoned in Russia on charges widely viewed as politically motivated ([Al Jazeera, via BIG Media](https://big-media.ca/the-controversy-behind-carlson-interview-of-putin/)).

## The working institution

The Kremlin that matters in day-to-day politics is not only the famous fortress but the administrative machine inside it. The President of Russia's own published records describe the Presidential Executive Office as built around a chief of staff and deputies, with subdivisions spanning the Main Domestic Policy Directorate, the Foreign Policy Directorate, the State Legal Directorate, the Security Council Office, the Press Office, the Protocol Directorate, the Presidential Chancellery, and offices of presidential envoys to the federal districts, among others ([kremlin.ru](http://en.kremlin.ru/events/president/news/38504)). The office formally includes the presidential press secretary alongside the head of protocol, the chief of the chancellery, presidential aides, and advisers ([kremlin.ru](http://en.kremlin.ru/events/president/news/38504)).

That press secretary is Dmitry Peskov, whom the Kremlin's published staff records list as "Deputy Chief of Staff of the Presidential Executive Office and Presidential Press Secretary" — the dual title that makes him both a senior administrator and the public voice of the presidency ([kremlin.ru](http://en.special.kremlin.ru/acts/news/57775)). It is in that capacity that he runs the regular briefings where episodes like the Carlson explanation unfold, and where the Kremlin's position on everything from election schedules to war aims gets its first official wording.

## A fortress with a 900-year paper trail

The institution sits inside one of the most recognizable government complexes in the world. Britannica defines the Kremlin as the "group of buildings in the center of Moscow that serves as the official seat of the government of Russia," noting that *kremlin* simply means "citadel," that about 30 such complexes survive across Russia, and that the term has become synonymous with Moscow's walled compound — "a symbol for Russia itself, having been home to Russian tsars and presidents" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)). The complex covers roughly 70 acres (28 hectares); its walls run 16 to 62 feet tall around an 8,200-foot perimeter; inside stand four cathedrals, five palaces, numerous state offices, and 20 towers ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)). It sits atop Borovitsky Hill, abutting Red Square ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)).

Its history as a government center began in 1156, when Prince Yury Vladimirovich Dolgoruky founded Moscow and had the first wooden walls built ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)). The Mongol army burned those walls during its 1236–40 invasion; Ivan I replaced them with oak and stucco around 1340, and Dmitry Donskoy rebuilt them in white stone in 1367–68 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)). In the late 15th century, Ivan III — aiming to build what he called a "third Rome" — had Italian architects rebuild the fortress in red brick, a material then unseen in Moscow, and erected the Dormition Cathedral and the Cathedral of St. Michael the Archangel on Cathedral Square ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)). The early-16th-century bell tower of Ivan the Great, at 270 feet, was then Russia's tallest building ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)).

When Peter the Great moved the capital to St. Petersburg in 1712, the Kremlin stayed behind as a ceremonial and religious site; some state power returned with Catherine the Great's 1762 coronation, and the Senate building completed in 1787 is now the Russian president's residence ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)). Napoleon occupied the Kremlin in 1812 and his retreating army mined it, damaging parts beyond repair; the Great Kremlin Palace, completed in 1849 to Konstantin Ton's design, became an imperial residence and, in the Soviet era (1917/22–1991), hosted meetings of the Supreme Soviet ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)). After the 1917 Revolution restored Moscow as the capital, churches were repurposed and the Chudov and Ascension monasteries' churches destroyed for state buildings; during World War II the towers were covered and roofs painted to dodge German bombers; Stalin later had the walls painted red; the complex became an open-air museum in 1955; and the Soviet period destroyed 28 of its 54 historical buildings before the 1990 UNESCO World Heritage designation ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Kremlin)).
