## Overview

Native News Online is an independent daily news outlet covering Native American affairs in the United States, founded in Grand Rapids, Michigan, by Levi Rickert, a citizen of the Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2021/11/national-native-american-heritage-month-4-media-organizations-to-watch/); [Hope College](https://hope.edu/news/2023/campus-life/lecture-to-focus-on-history-and-impact-of-indian-boarding-schools.html.amp)). Launched on February 15, 2011, it is part of Indian Country Media, LLC, which also publishes Tribal Business News, and it covers education, health, sovereignty, environment, and arts and entertainment for millions of readers a year, including American Indians and Alaska Natives ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2021/11/national-native-american-heritage-month-4-media-organizations-to-watch/)).

What sets it apart from a general-interest outlet with a Native beat is its position in the chain of record: tribal governments use it as an announcement channel, mainstream institutions cite its publisher as an expert on American Indian affairs, and its own reporting is a cited source for FactsLedger's profile of the [All Pueblo Council of Governors](/organizations/all-pueblo-council-of-governors/) ([Hope College](https://hope.edu/news/2023/campus-life/lecture-to-focus-on-history-and-impact-of-indian-boarding-schools.html.amp); [National Press Club](https://www.press.org/newsroom/news-outlets-covering-underrepresented-communities-help-irrigate-news-desert)).

## A Native-led daily newsroom

Rickert launched the site on February 15, 2011, and its first week drew only 16 viewers ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2021/11/national-native-american-heritage-month-4-media-organizations-to-watch/)). By 2021 readership had grown to more than 3 million unique visitors a year with over 5 million page views, and by 2023 Hope College described it as having more than 5 million annual readers and being one of the most-read daily Native American publications in the country ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2021/11/national-native-american-heritage-month-4-media-organizations-to-watch/); [Hope College](https://hope.edu/news/2023/campus-life/lecture-to-focus-on-history-and-impact-of-indian-boarding-schools.html.amp)). The outlet's stated premise is that readers want accurate and consistent Native American news, and that it can provide that accuracy because it has a Native American-led newsroom publishing every day of the year ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2021/11/national-native-american-heritage-month-4-media-organizations-to-watch/)).

Rickert himself is the outlet's most visible asset: a Grand Rapids resident and Prairie Band Potawatomi citizen who has reported from dozens of reservations as well as from Alcatraz, Standing Rock, White House tribal nations summits, and congressional hearings in Washington, D.C. ([Hope College](https://hope.edu/news/2023/campus-life/lecture-to-focus-on-history-and-impact-of-indian-boarding-schools.html.amp)). Other media outlets call on him as an expert to discuss American Indian affairs, and he writes a weekly op-ed alongside the news operation ([Hope College](https://hope.edu/news/2023/campus-life/lecture-to-focus-on-history-and-impact-of-indian-boarding-schools.html.amp); [Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2020/11/native-news-online-uses-grant-money-to-expand-local-tribal-coverage/)). His individual recognition includes the Native Media Award for Best Column in 2021 from the Native American Journalists Association and the West Michigan chapter of the Public Relations Society of America's Social Justice, Diversity, Equity and Inclusion (JEDI) of the Year award in June 2023 ([Hope College](https://hope.edu/news/2023/campus-life/lecture-to-focus-on-history-and-impact-of-indian-boarding-schools.html.amp)). He also hosts the outlet's weekly Native Bidaské (Spotlight) feature on people making news and leading change in Indian Country, and co-hosts the Hope + Healing podcast with the National Indian Health Board ([Hope College](https://hope.edu/news/2023/campus-life/lecture-to-focus-on-history-and-impact-of-indian-boarding-schools.html.amp)).

## From advertising-only to grants, donations and a network

Before 2020 the outlet relied exclusively on advertising revenue ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2020/11/native-news-online-uses-grant-money-to-expand-local-tribal-coverage/)). The turning point was a Facebook Journalism Project COVID-19 Local News Relief Fund grant in 2020, which paid for a managing editor and several freelance journalists spread across different tribal territories ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2020/11/native-news-online-uses-grant-money-to-expand-local-tribal-coverage/)). Rickert had almost not applied, because the outlet is national rather than local — but, as he put it, the grant would help a national outlet covering Indian Country localize its coverage much better ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2020/11/native-news-online-uses-grant-money-to-expand-local-tribal-coverage/)).

The grant funded "Local Impact," a series of dozens of deep dives into underreported tribes and tribal citizens during the pandemic ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2020/11/native-news-online-uses-grant-money-to-expand-local-tribal-coverage/)). The need was structural: Rickert noted 574 federally recognized tribes and about 400 state-recognized tribes, very few of which had anything more than a newsletter as a credible news source ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2020/11/native-news-online-uses-grant-money-to-expand-local-tribal-coverage/)). The pandemic coverage grew the audience, including to 400,000 Facebook followers by 2020, and reader donations at the foot of each article later kept the managing editor on full-time after the grant expired ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2020/11/native-news-online-uses-grant-money-to-expand-local-tribal-coverage/)). By 2021 the revenue mix had broadened to advertising, donations, sponsorships and grants, and the extra staffing let the outlet concentrate on topics including the missing and murdered Indigenous women crisis and Indian boarding schools ([Local Media Association](http://localmedia.org/2021/11/national-native-american-heritage-month-4-media-organizations-to-watch/)).

The next step was collective rather than solo: the outlet joined URL Media, a network of high-performing BIPOC-owned and operated media organizations, as its 10th member and first Indigenous-led member, according to its sister publication Tribal Business News ([Tribal Business News](https://tribalbusinessnews.com/sections/economic-development/13777-native-news-online-joins-year-old-url-media-network)). URL Media had launched in January 2021 with eight companies and aims to expand revenue opportunities and share content, distribution and resources among its members ([Tribal Business News](https://tribalbusinessnews.com/sections/economic-development/13777-native-news-online-joins-year-old-url-media-network)). Its chief executive, S. Mitra Kalita, said she sought the partnership after the outlet's coverage of the role of Native elders in helping their communities through the COVID-19 pandemic resonated with her ([Tribal Business News](https://tribalbusinessnews.com/sections/economic-development/13777-native-news-online-joins-year-old-url-media-network)).

## The Chaco test: a tribal government's announcement channel

The outlet's distinctive role is clearest in sovereignty coverage, where tribal bodies speak through it rather than merely being covered by it. FactsLedger's profile of the All Pueblo Council of Governors draws on a Native News Online article for the council's applause for the reintroduced legislation to protect [Chaco Canyon](/places/chaco-canyon/) — including the council's membership of 20 Pueblos in New Mexico plus one in Texas, and its chairman attribution — making the outlet part of the documentary chain for a sovereign body's public position, not just a commentator on it.

The legislation at issue, the Chaco Cultural Heritage Area Protection Act, would permanently protect federal lands within 10 miles of Chaco Culture National Historical Park from new oil and gas leasing, covering about 339,000 acres of federal public lands containing thousands of significant cultural properties and sites ([National Parks Conservation Association](https://www.npca.org/articles/3479-protecting-chaco-canyon-advocates-applaud-legislation-to-safeguard-the)). The bill had previously passed the U.S. House of Representatives in 2019, and the council had travelled to Washington to urge the administration to protect Chaco Canyon, with the Bureau of Land Management receiving more than 80,000 public comments in support of the 10-mile withdrawal ([National Parks Conservation Association](https://www.npca.org/articles/3479-protecting-chaco-canyon-advocates-applaud-legislation-to-safeguard-the)). The landscape itself — dozens of ancient villages, roads and shrines built by the ancestors of the Pueblos and other Indigenous Nations who emerged and flourished in Chaco Canyon between 850 and 1250 A.D. — is designated a [UNESCO](/organizations/unesco/) World Heritage Site ([National Parks Conservation Association](https://www.npca.org/articles/3479-protecting-chaco-canyon-advocates-applaud-legislation-to-safeguard-the)). That is why a Native-led outlet's sovereignty beat is not a niche interest here but the primary record: the campaign is led by Pueblo and Diné organizers, and the outlet covers it from inside the community whose heritage is at stake ([National Parks Conservation Association](https://www.npca.org/articles/3479-protecting-chaco-canyon-advocates-applaud-legislation-to-safeguard-the)).

The same inside-out position shapes Rickert's public argument about the wider media. At a National Press Club panel on news deserts, he said tribes typically have their own publications but there is very little collective coverage of Native Americans, and that mainstream coverage tends to paint a negative picture — citing a Native American Journalists Association survey finding that a year's coverage dwelt on poverty, alcoholism and "the dark side of who we are as a Native people" ([National Press Club](https://www.press.org/newsroom/news-outlets-covering-underrepresented-communities-help-irrigate-news-desert)). "It's time for Native Americans to tell our own story," he said, adding that he sees his audience as more than just Native Americans: "We have to constantly educate" ([National Press Club](https://www.press.org/newsroom/news-outlets-covering-underrepresented-communities-help-irrigate-news-desert)). He also said the publication often receives calls from legislators, and that after years running "on a shoestring," recognition — including the National Press Club's own invitation — gave him hope for journalism in his community ([National Press Club](https://www.press.org/newsroom/news-outlets-covering-underrepresented-communities-help-irrigate-news-desert)).
