## Overview

WebBank is a [Utah](/places/utah/)-chartered, FDIC-insured industrial bank headquartered in Salt Lake City, Utah, founded in 1997 and owned by Steel Partners Holdings L.P. ([Steel Partners](https://www.steelpartners.com/companies/webbank/)). It is a bank most of its customers never knowingly use: it originates consumer and small-business credit products that are marketed, underwritten in part, and serviced by brand partners — [Avant](/organizations/avant/), Klarna, PayPal, Shopify, LendingClub and others — under its own "Bank Behind the Brand" slogan ([Steel Partners](https://www.steelpartners.com/companies/webbank/)). That invisible role made it nationally important: its partnership model sits at the centre of the "rent-a-bank" debate over who the true lender is when a fintech sells a loan a bank originated, a question Colorado regulators litigated for three years and federal regulators answered with new rules in 2020.

The bank's scale is large relative to its public profile. Its parent says it has originated and funded more than $290 billion in consumer and commercial credit products since 1997 — a company-reported figure, up from "over $175 billion" in its own 2023 materials ([Steel Partners](https://www.steelpartners.com/companies/webbank/)). It does this from a single-state charter: as a Utah industrial bank it can export Utah's interest-rate rules nationwide under Section 27 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act, which is precisely what makes the charter valuable to its partners — and precisely what its critics challenge ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2020/pr20074.html?source=govdelivery&)).

## The bank behind the brand

WebBank's business model is deliberately low-profile. It issues credit products through its partners' platforms rather than its own branches or brand: consumer loans, revolving lines of credit, credit cards, private-label card issuance, and auto refinancing, plus capital provision to partner platforms, credit funds and other lenders focused on specialty finance assets ([Steel Partners](https://www.steelpartners.com/companies/webbank/)). Most consumers who borrow through it never deal with it directly — they deal with the branded partner — though the bank also offers savings accounts and certificates of deposit in its own name ([FinMasters](https://finmasters.com/webbank-review/)). It is also a provider of commercial insurance premium finance through its wholly owned subsidiary National Partners ([BusinessWire](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/webbank-announces-jason-lloyds-appointment-to-the-utah-bankers-association-board-of)).

Its business partners, listed on the bank's own site, are a roll call of American small-business finance: BILL for invoice financing, CAN Capital for business financing, Capital on Tap for business credit cards, PayPal for Working Capital, Business Loans and Loan Builder loans (and, with Concerto Card Company, the PayPal Business Cashback Mastercard), Shopify for Shopify Capital in the [United States](/places/united-states/), Toast Capital for restaurant installment loans, and PEAC for working capital loans ([WebBank](https://www.webbank.com/business-brand-partners)). On the consumer side, its partners include Avant (personal loans and the AvantCard), Klarna (buy now, pay later and the Klarna Card), Dell Financial Services (the Dell Preferred Account), Fingerhut, Petal, Prosper, DigniFi for auto repairs, Mosaic for solar and home improvements, and Yamaha Motor Finance ([FinMasters](https://finmasters.com/webbank-review/)).

The invisibility has a cost in public confusion. WebBank holds an A rating with the [Better Business Bureau](/organizations/better-business-bureau/), but more than 200 BBB complaints against it are, in FinMasters' assessment, nearly all misdirected — borrowers complaining to the bank about the partner that services their account ([FinMasters](https://finmasters.com/webbank-review/)). It is also routinely confused with WeBank, the Tencent-backed Chinese neobank founded in 2014 — a different institution in a different country ([FinMasters](https://finmasters.com/webbank-review/)).

## Ownership and structure

WebBank is wholly owned by WebFinancial Holding Corporation, a Delaware corporation, which is itself wholly owned by Steel Partners Holdings, L.P., a publicly traded Delaware limited partnership — a chain disclosed in WebBank's own corporate disclosure statement filed in federal court in Connecticut in April 2025 ([U.S. District Court, District of Connecticut](https://archive.org/download/gov.uscourts.ctd.164078/gov.uscourts.ctd.164078.25.0.pdf)). Its president is Jason Lloyd, promoted by the board in 2011 after three years as executive vice president; he had previously worked at CIT Group (CIT Bank) and Mitsubishi Corporation ([FinMasters](https://finmasters.com/webbank-review/)). A 2023 press release described him as President & CEO, though no fully-read recent source confirms his current title.

The Utah charter is the structural fact everything else turns on. Utah is one of a handful of states that charter industrial banks — institutions that can be owned by commercial companies (Steel Partners is an industrial holding company, not a bank holding company) while still taking FDIC-insured deposits and lending nationwide. Section 27 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act, created by the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980, lets a state-chartered bank charge interest at the rate permitted in the state where the bank is located — so a Utah-chartered bank can lend at Utah-permitted rates to borrowers in states with stricter caps ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2020/pr20074.html?source=govdelivery&)). That exportation privilege is the engine of the bank-partnership model, and the target of the litigation that followed.

## The true-lender debate

The dispute that defines WebBank's public record began with Avant. In 2014 Avant, Inc. entered into a lending program agreement with WebBank: WebBank made loans to consumers who applied through Avant's website, and within two business days sold the loans to third-party purchasers, including Avant itself or an Avant affiliate ([U.S. District Court, District of Colorado](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). In January 2017, [Colorado](/places/colorado/)'s consumer-credit regulator, Administrator Julie Ann Meade, sued Avant — not WebBank — alleging the loans charged finance and delinquency charges above Colorado's limits and carried an unlawful Utah choice-of-law clause ([Morrison & Foerster](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/finance-and-banking/dropping-anchor-in-the-safe-harbor-colorado-true-lender-litigation-settles)).

The regulator's argument, as recorded in the federal court order, was that WebBank was not the "true lender" because it did not bear the predominant economic interest in the loans. Avant paid the implementation fee and all of WebBank's legal fees, bore all marketing and underwriting costs, decided which applicants received loans, handled all servicing and consumer communications, bore all default risk, and indemnified WebBank against all claims; Avant and the other non-bank entities collected 99% of the profits, leaving WebBank about 1% ([U.S. District Court, District of Colorado](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). The regulator alleged a primary purpose of the arrangement was to let non-banks circumvent state interest-rate limits.

Avant removed the case to federal court, arguing Section 27 completely preempted the state claims. On 1 March 2018 Judge William J. Martínez adopted a magistrate judge's recommendation in full, overruled Avant's objection, and remanded the case to Denver District Court, holding that Section 27 does not completely preempt state-law claims against a non-bank assignee — even if the valid-when-made doctrine might be a complete defence on the merits in state court ([U.S. District Court, District of Colorado](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). Crucially, the court did not decide whether Avant was the true lender; it left that question open for the state court, while finding the regulator had not manipulated the pleadings in alleging it ([Law360](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=bca5bf72-f640-45cb-9ddf-75723020fa42)). WebBank itself filed an amicus brief supporting Avant's objection. A parallel case against Marlette Funding, WebBank's other Colorado partner, proceeded on the same issues.

The industry read the decision as a warning. Law360's contemporary analysis said the court had "embraced the plaintiff's argument that the economic and operational realities of the underlying loan program, rather than traditional contract principles, should determine who was the 'true lender'" — while noting the court stopped short of expressly finding Avant the true lender ([Law360](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=bca5bf72-f640-45cb-9ddf-75723020fa42)). The bank's defenders argued the arrangement expands credit access and lowers costs; the regulators argued it lets non-banks rent a charter to evade state usury law. Neither side's characterization has been settled by a court on the merits for the Avant program, and this profile does not settle it either.

Two developments in 2020 reshaped the ground. First, the FDIC issued a final rule on 25 June 2020 codifying the "valid when made" doctrine — the nearly 200-year-old principle that a loan's interest rate is judged when the loan is made and is not affected by a later sale, assignment or transfer — mirroring an OCC rule for national banks issued on 29 May 2020 ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2020/pr20074.html?source=govdelivery&)). Second, in August 2020, Colorado settled the litigation: Avant of Colorado and Marlette Funding, with WebBank and Cross River Bank, agreed to a safe harbor under which their marketplace lending programs would be deemed compliant with Colorado's usury caps if they met conditions on oversight, disclosure, funding, licensing, consumer terms and structure — including the bank being named as lender and funding loans from its own account, a 36% APR cap on Colorado loans, supervised-loan licensing for the non-bank partners, and limits on how much economic interest could be transferred ([Morrison & Foerster](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/finance-and-banking/dropping-anchor-in-the-safe-harbor-colorado-true-lender-litigation-settles)). The parties collectively paid a $1,050,000 civil money penalty and contributed $500,000 to a Colorado financial-literacy program, and the banks agreed to five years of safe-harbor compliance ([Morrison & Foerster](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/finance-and-banking/dropping-anchor-in-the-safe-harbor-colorado-true-lender-litigation-settles)).

One more structural detail complicates any simple "rent-a-bank" label. The LendingClub agreement, disclosed in a 2016 SEC filing, shows a different arrangement from the one Colorado alleged for Avant: WebBank "maintains an on-going economic interest in all loans made after they are sold, which is paid only when borrowers make payments on their loans," and "the majority of WebBank's revenue is tied to the terms and performance of the loans" ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1409970/000140997016001801/form8-kfiledon3216webbanka.htm)). The two pictures describe different programs in different years, and neither should be generalized into a claim about the bank's practice as a whole.

## The pandemic test

The bank-partnership model faced a different kind of test in 2020. Fintech lenders were initially excluded from originating Paycheck Protection Program loans and had to partner with a traditional bank, and WebBank became the lender behind PayPal's PPP lending ([Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/community-development-research-briefs/2021/03/ppp-lending-12th-district/)). Across the Twelfth Federal Reserve District, four banks that partnered with fintech firms — Cross River Bank, Celtic Bank, Customers Bank and WebBank — were among the top 10 lenders by number of PPP loans, behind only the largest national banks ([Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/community-development-research-briefs/2021/03/ppp-lending-12th-district/)). The episode showed the model's other face: the same charter that drew regulatory challenge also let small businesses reach emergency credit through platforms they already used.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is WebBank?**
WebBank is a Utah-chartered, FDIC-insured industrial bank headquartered in Salt Lake City, founded in 1997 and owned by Steel Partners Holdings L.P. ([Steel Partners](https://www.steelpartners.com/companies/webbank/)). It originates consumer and small-business credit products that are marketed and serviced by brand partners such as Avant, Klarna, PayPal and Shopify, and describes itself as "the Bank Behind the Brand" ([Steel Partners](https://www.steelpartners.com/companies/webbank/)).

**Is WebBank a real bank?**
Yes. It is a state-chartered industrial bank, FDIC-insured, supervised by the FDIC, and deposits are federally insured up to $250,000 ([FinMasters](https://finmasters.com/webbank-review/)). It has no consumer branches in the usual sense: most of its lending reaches borrowers through partner platforms, and most borrowers deal with the partner, not the bank ([FinMasters](https://finmasters.com/webbank-review/)).

**Why is WebBank chartered in Utah?**
Utah is one of a handful of states that charter industrial banks, which can be owned by commercial companies while taking FDIC-insured deposits and lending nationwide. Under Section 27 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act, a Utah-chartered bank can charge interest at rates permitted in Utah to borrowers in other states ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2020/pr20074.html?source=govdelivery&)).

**What is the "true lender" debate about WebBank?**
Colorado's regulator argued that in the Avant program, WebBank was lender in name only — Avant did the underwriting, servicing and marketing, bore the default risk and kept 99% of the profits, so Avant was the true lender and Colorado's usury caps should apply ([U.S. District Court, District of Colorado](http://business.cch.com/bfld/meade_03022018.pdf)). The question was never decided on the merits: the 2018 federal court remanded without deciding it, and the 2020 settlement created a safe harbor instead.

**Which brands use WebBank?**
Its business partners include PayPal, Shopify, Toast, BILL, CAN Capital and Capital on Tap; its consumer partners include Avant, Klarna, Dell Financial Services, Fingerhut, Petal, Prosper, DigniFi, Mosaic and Yamaha Motor Finance ([WebBank](https://www.webbank.com/business-brand-partners); [FinMasters](https://finmasters.com/webbank-review/)).
