## Overview

The Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage is the [UNESCO](/organizations/unesco/) body that runs the 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage. It decides which living practices join the Representative List and the Urgent Safeguarding List, selects programmes for the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices, and grants international assistance from the Convention's Fund. Established within UNESCO by the Convention itself, it is not an independent organization.

The Committee was created by Article 5 of the Convention, which UNESCO's General Conference adopted in Paris on 17 October 2003 ([UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)). The Convention entered into force on 20 April 2006, and the Committee held its first session in Algiers in November 2006, where it decided that Japan would host its second session ([Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/culture/coop/unesco/c_heritage/i_heritage/convention.html)). The Convention's stated purposes are to safeguard intangible heritage, ensure respect for it, raise awareness of its importance at local, national and international levels, and provide for international cooperation and assistance ([UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)). It answers to the General Assembly of States Parties — the Convention's sovereign body, which meets every two years — and reports to it on all its activities and decisions ([UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)). As of June 2026 the Convention had 185 States Parties ([Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/culture/coop/unesco/c_heritage/i_heritage/convention.html)). The Committee's working language and records are public: its decisions are published session by session with numbers such as 8.COM 8 or 17.COM 7.b.8, and its ordinary sessions are held once a year in rotating host cities.

## Mandate and functions

Under Article 7 of the Convention, the Committee promotes the Convention's objectives and encourages and monitors their implementation, provides guidance on best practices, and makes recommendations on safeguarding measures. It prepares a draft plan for the use of the resources of the Intangible Cultural Heritage Fund and drafts operational directives for the Convention, submitting both to the General Assembly for approval. The Fund itself is fed by the regular contributions of States Parties as well as voluntary contributions, and international assistance can take the form of specialists, training, equipment and know-how. It examines the periodic reports States Parties submit on their implementation of the Convention and summarizes them for the General Assembly. Most visibly, it examines requests from States Parties and decides on inscriptions on the lists and proposals under Articles 16, 17 and 18, and on the granting of international assistance ([UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)).

In practice that means the Committee is the gatekeeper for the Convention's three listing mechanisms. It examines nominations for the Representative List and the Urgent Safeguarding List, and proposals for the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices, after they have been assessed by its Evaluation Body ([Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/culture/coop/unesco/c_heritage/i_heritage/convention.html)). It is also in charge of granting international assistance, and it prepares the Convention's implementation through operational directives and the plan for the Fund's resources, which it submits to the General Assembly ([UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage](https://ich.unesco.org/en/functions-00586)). The Committee may set up temporary ad hoc consultative bodies, and it may invite public or private bodies and private persons with recognized competence to its meetings to consult them on specific matters ([UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)). It also proposes to the General Assembly the accreditation of non-governmental organizations to act in an advisory capacity ([UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)).

## Composition, election and Bureau

The Committee has 24 members: representatives of 24 States Parties elected by the General Assembly of States Parties under the principles of equitable geographical representation and rotation. The Convention originally set the number at 18, rising to 24 once the number of States Parties reached 50. Members serve four-year terms, the General Assembly renews half of them every two years, and a State Member may not be elected for two consecutive terms. At the first election, half of the members served only two-year terms, chosen by lot, so that the staggered renewal cycle could begin. Membership belongs to States, not individuals: each State Member sends representatives, and the Convention requires those representatives to be persons qualified in the various fields of intangible cultural heritage. The General Assembly also elects as many members as are required to fill vacancies ([UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)).

Seats are distributed among the six UNESCO electoral groups in proportion to the number of States Parties from each group, with each group holding at least three seats in a 24-member Committee ([UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage](https://ich.unesco.org/en/functions-00586)). Following the General Assembly's eleventh ordinary session in June 2026, the 24 members were France, Spain, Norway, Ukraine, Albania, Bulgaria, Barbados, Dominican Republic, Haiti, Guatemala, China, Japan, Cambodia, Indonesia, Philippines, Nigeria, Uganda, Zambia, Djibouti, Guinea, Mali, Algeria, United Arab Emirates and Oman ([Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/culture/coop/unesco/c_heritage/i_heritage/convention.html)). The Committee is assisted by the UNESCO Secretariat, whose Section of Intangible Cultural Heritage in the Culture Sector is based in Paris, and which prepares the documentation and draft agendas for the General Assembly and the Committee and ensures their decisions are implemented ([Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/culture/coop/unesco/c_heritage/i_heritage/convention.html); [UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)).

Between sessions, much of the routine work falls to the Bureau, which consists of a Chairperson, one or more Vice-Chairpersons and a Rapporteur, chosen with equitable geographical representation in mind. The Bureau coordinates the Committee's work and fixes the dates, times and order of business of meetings, and exercises any other duty the Committee entrusts to it ([UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage](https://ich.unesco.org/ar/-00803)). Under the Operational Directives, the Bureau is the body authorized to approve international assistance requests of up to US$100,000, and to examine and approve emergency requests above US$100,000; in cases of extreme urgency it may invite a State Party to submit an Urgent Safeguarding nomination on an accelerated schedule ([UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage](https://ich.unesco.org/ar/-00803)).

## Sessions and decisions

The Committee meets in ordinary session once a year, usually in the autumn, and its sessions rotate between host cities rather than staying at UNESCO headquarters in Paris. It has also met in extraordinary session when needed — for example in Chengdu in May 2007 to prepare the Operational Directives, and in Sofia in February 2008 to draft them. Its early ordinary sessions set the pattern: Algiers in 2006, Tokyo in 2007 (which adopted the Operational Directives), Istanbul in 2008, Abu Dhabi in 2009 (which made the first Representative List inscriptions), Nairobi in 2010, Bari in 2011, Paris in 2012, Baku in 2013, Paris again in 2014, Windhoek in 2015, Addis Ababa in 2016, Jeju Island in 2017, Port Louis in 2018 and Bogotá in 2019, with the 2020 and 2021 sessions held online ([Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/culture/coop/unesco/c_heritage/i_heritage/convention.html)).

The eighth session (8.COM), held in Baku in December 2013 under the chairmanship of the Azerbaijani Minister of Culture and Tourism, Abulfas Garayev, examined 31 Representative List nominations, 12 Urgent Safeguarding nominations, two Register proposals and a financial assistance request above US$25,000 ([UNESCO Regional Centre, Sofia](https://www.unesco-centerbg.org/en/2013/11/08/8th-session-of-the-intergovernmental-committee-for-the-safeguarding-of-the-intangible-cultural-heritage/)). In its general decision 8.COM 8, the Committee tightened the rules for Representative List nominations: criterion R.2 would count as satisfied only if a nomination showed how inscription would build visibility for intangible heritage in general and encourage dialogue respecting cultural diversity, and nominations would only be considered complete with documentary evidence of community consent and of the element's inclusion in a national inventory ([UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage](https://ich.unesco.org/en/Decisions/8.COM/8)). At the same session it inscribed [Chovqan](/events/chovgan/), Azerbaijan's traditional Karabakh horse-riding game, on the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding ([UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage](https://ich.unesco.org/en/USL/chovqan-a-traditional-karabakh-horse-riding-game-in-the-republic-of-azerbaijan-00905); [AzerNews](https://www.azernews.az/nation/62189.html)).

## Not the World Heritage Committee

The Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage is not the World Heritage Committee, and the two must not be confused. The World Heritage Committee works under a different treaty — the Convention concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage, adopted by UNESCO in 1972 — and deals with tangible places and natural sites judged to be of outstanding universal value, which it inscribes on the World Heritage List; 196 countries adhere to that Convention ([UNESCO](http://www.unesco.org/en/world-heritage)). Its sessions likewise rotate: the World Heritage Committee met in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, in 2023 ([UNESCO](http://www.unesco.org/en/world-heritage)). This Committee, by contrast, works under the 2003 Convention and deals with living heritage: the practices, representations, expressions, knowledge and skills that communities recognize as part of their cultural heritage, transmitted from generation to generation and constantly recreated in response to environment, interaction with nature and history ([UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)).

The 2003 Convention itself keeps the two regimes apart: nothing in it may be interpreted as altering the status or diminishing the level of protection, under the 1972 Convention, of World Heritage properties with which an item of intangible heritage is directly associated ([UK Government treaty text](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/659d70aa7e3968000da81a7f/MS_1.2024_Convention_Safeguarding_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage.pdf)). In other words, a practice can be inscribed by this Committee while a related place is listed by the World Heritage Committee, and neither decision affects the other. The overlap is real — the twentieth session of this Committee met at the Red Fort in New Delhi, itself a World Heritage Site since 2007 — but the mandates, lists and committees remain legally distinct ([University of Warsaw UNESCO Chair](https://wnpism.uw.edu.pl/en/our-unesco-chair-at-the-20th-session-of-the-unesco-intergovernmental-committee-for-the-safeguarding-of-the-intangible-cultural-heritage-new-delhi-india/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What does the Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage do?**
It runs the 2003 Convention: it decides which practices join the Representative List and the Urgent Safeguarding List, selects programmes for the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices, grants international assistance from the Convention's Fund, drafts operational directives, and examines States Parties' periodic reports on implementation.

**How are the Committee's members chosen?**
The General Assembly of States Parties elects 24 member States for four-year terms, renewing half the membership every two years under equitable geographical representation and rotation. A member State may not serve two consecutive terms, and must appoint representatives qualified in intangible heritage fields.

**How is it different from the World Heritage Committee?**
The World Heritage Committee works under the 1972 Convention and lists tangible places and natural sites of outstanding universal value. This Committee works under the 2003 Convention and decides on living practices, skills and expressions — a separate treaty, separate lists, and separate decisions.

**Where and when does the Committee meet?**
It meets in ordinary session once a year, usually in the autumn, in rotating host cities — from Algiers in 2006 and Tokyo in 2007 to Rabat in 2022, Kasane in 2023, Asunción in 2024 and New Delhi in 2025. Its secretariat sits at UNESCO headquarters in Paris, and the 21st session is scheduled for Xiamen, China.

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not treat the Committee as an independent organization; it is a body established within UNESCO, answerable to the General Assembly of States Parties.
- It does not confuse this Committee with the World Heritage Committee, which operates under the separate 1972 Convention and lists tangible and natural sites.
- It does not state who chairs the Committee after the twentieth session, because no source read here names the Bureau elected for the twenty-first session.
- It does not assert twentieth-session inscription totals from UNESCO directly; those figures (54 Representative List, 11 Urgent Safeguarding) come from a University of Warsaw report, since UNESCO's own 20.COM page could not be re-read during this research.
- It does not resolve the small date discrepancies between sources for the nineteenth session (2–6 vs 2–7 December 2024) and the twentieth session (8–13 vs 7–13 December 2025); both are flagged in `openItems`.
- It does not claim the Committee itself safeguards heritage on the ground; under the Convention, States Parties carry the primary safeguarding role in their own territories, and the Committee decides, guides, funds and monitors.
