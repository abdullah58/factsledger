## Overview

The Government of [Canada](/places/canada/) is the federal executive of Canada — the Prime Minister, the Cabinet and the federal public service that administer the country under the Constitution Act, 1867. Canada is a federal state, a parliamentary democracy and a constitutional monarchy, and its government has operated in that form since Confederation on 1 July 1867 ([Department of Justice Canada](https://canada.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/just/05.html); [Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/corporate/publications-manuals/discover-canada/read-online/how-canadians-govern-themselves.html)).

This profile is about the federal government as an institution, not about any one ministry, party or election. It is not a provincial government — the ten provinces have their own legislatures and premiers — and it is not Parliament itself, although the government sits inside Parliament and answers to it. In the usual Canadian usage, "the government" means the executive branch: the ministers who direct policy and the departments and agencies that carry it out ([Department of Justice Canada](https://canada.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/just/05.html)).

## The Crown, the Cabinet and responsible government

Executive power in Canada is formally vested in the Crown — the Sovereign, who is head of state — but by constitutional convention it is exercised on the advice of ministers who hold the support of the House of Commons. The Sovereign is represented in Canada by the Governor General, appointed on the Prime Minister's recommendation, usually for five years, who gives royal assent to bills, appoints the Prime Minister, dissolves Parliament before elections, reads the Speech from the Throne, and serves as Commander-in-Chief of the Canadian Armed Forces ([Department of Justice Canada](https://canada.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/just/05.html); [House of Commons](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/our-procedure/parliamentaryFramework/c_g_parliamentaryframework-e.html/)). The system comes from the British Westminster tradition, and the Constitution Act, 1867 gave Canada a constitution similar in principle to that of the [United Kingdom's Parliament](/organizations/uk-parliament/) ([House of Commons](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/our-procedure/parliamentaryFramework/c_g_parliamentaryframework-e.html/)).

The Prime Minister is the head of government: the Prime Minister selects the Cabinet ministers and is responsible for the operations and policy of the government. Cabinet is the key decision-making forum — it leads the executive branch, develops policy and introduces bills to turn policy into law. Ministers are first made members of the Privy Council and then sworn in; each holds a portfolio or department, such as the Department of Finance or the Department of Justice, usually assisted by parliamentary secretaries ([House of Commons](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/our-procedure/parliamentaryFramework/c_g_parliamentaryframework-e.html/); [Department of Justice Canada](https://canada.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/just/05.html)). Ministers are individually responsible to Parliament for their departments and collectively accountable for every Cabinet decision, and the whole government must retain the confidence of the House of Commons — defeat on a confidence question, such as the budget, means the Prime Minister is expected to resign or seek dissolution and a general election ([House of Commons](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/our-procedure/parliamentaryFramework/c_g_parliamentaryframework-e.html/)).

## Parliament and how laws are made

Parliament is the legislative branch and has three parts: the Sovereign (usually represented by the Governor General), the Senate and the House of Commons. The Senate has 105 appointed senators, who serve until age 75 and represent the country's regions, provinces and territories; the House of Commons has 343 members elected from electoral districts across the country ([House of Commons](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/our-procedure/parliamentaryFramework/c_g_parliamentaryframework-e.html/)). A government holding more than half the Commons seats is a majority government; one holding the most seats but short of a majority is a minority government, which must rely on other parties' support to govern ([House of Commons](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/our-procedure/parliamentaryFramework/c_g_parliamentaryframework-e.html/)).

Bills are debated and passed by both chambers, and no bill becomes law until it has been adopted by both Houses in identical form and received royal assent from the Governor General — assent that, by convention, is always given to bills passed by both Houses ([Department of Justice Canada](https://canada.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/just/05.html)). Government legislation is usually introduced in the House of Commons by a minister, and bills that spend public money or impose taxes must originate there ([House of Commons](https://www.ourcommons.ca/procedure/our-procedure/parliamentaryFramework/c_g_parliamentaryframework-e.html/)). Parliament also authorizes government spending and holds ministers to account through question period and committees, which is how the confidence convention is enforced day to day.

## The division of powers: federal, provincial and shared

Canada's Constitution divides law-making between two orders of government: Parliament can legislate for all of Canada, but only on matters the Constitution assigns to it, while each provincial legislature can legislate only within its own province's borders ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/intergovernmental-affairs/services/federation/distribution-legislative-powers.html)). The federal list in section 91 covers matters of national interest — trade and commerce, taxation, defence, navigation, fisheries, banking, currency, patents, copyright, citizenship, marriage and divorce, criminal law, penitentiaries, the postal service and the census — while section 92 gives the provinces matters of a local nature, including education, hospitals, municipalities, property and civil rights, natural resources and the administration of justice ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/intergovernmental-affairs/services/federation/distribution-legislative-powers.html)). Some powers are shared, notably agriculture, immigration and old age pensions, and the environment and health sit across both orders because the courts have assigned them under various federal and provincial heads ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/intergovernmental-affairs/services/federation/distribution-legislative-powers.html)).

| Order of government | Constitutional basis | Examples of jurisdiction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal Parliament | Constitution Act, 1867, s. 91 | Defence, criminal law, currency, banking, citizenship, trade and commerce, postal service |
| Provincial legislatures | Constitution Act, 1867, ss. 92, 92(A), 93 | Education, hospitals, municipalities, property and civil rights, natural resources |
| Shared / concurrent | ss. 94A, 95 | Agriculture, immigration, old age pensions |
| Residuary | s. 91, Peace, Order and good Government | Any matter not assigned exclusively to the provinces |

Section 91 also gives Parliament a residuary power to make laws for the "Peace, Order and good Government of Canada" on matters not assigned exclusively to the provinces, so that every area of legislation falls under one or both orders of government ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/intergovernmental-affairs/services/federation/distribution-legislative-powers.html)). When a dispute arises over which order may legislate, only the courts can give an authoritative answer, and judicial review has clarified, limited and expanded both sets of powers over time ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/intergovernmental-affairs/services/federation/distribution-legislative-powers.html)). The division is not frozen: amendments gave Parliament power over employment insurance in 1940 and over old age pensions in 1951, extended in 1964 ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/intergovernmental-affairs/services/federation/distribution-legislative-powers.html)). The federal Parliament is also responsible for the three territories — Yukon, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut — and the Constitution Act, 1867 authorized the courts that now sit at the top of the system, including the [Supreme Court of Canada](/organizations/supreme-court-of-canada/) ([Department of Justice Canada](https://canada.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/just/05.html)). Australia and the United States have federal systems too, but they differ in significant ways from Canada's — a reminder that the Canadian division of powers is a distinct constitutional bargain, not a copy of the [U.S. Executive Branch](/organizations/us-executive-branch/) model ([Department of Justice Canada](https://canada.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/just/05.html)).

## The machinery: departments, agencies and the public service

Ministers direct policy, but departments and agencies deliver it. Each minister is responsible for a department with a broad mandate — Finance, Justice and others — while the Treasury Board is the employer of the core public administration, the departments and agencies named in Schedules I and IV of the Financial Administration Act, and separate agencies named in Schedule V employ their own staff and set their own terms of employment ([Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat](https://www.canada.ca/en/treasury-board-secretariat/services/innovation/human-resources-statistics/population-federal-public-service.html)). The Department of Justice, for example, drafts laws, gives legal advice to the government and its departments, and develops criminal and public law, while its minister also serves as Attorney General, the chief law officer of Canada ([Department of Justice Canada](https://canada.justice.gc.ca/eng/csj-sjc/just/05.html)).

Federal reach extends beyond domestic programs. In 2025 the Government of Canada committed $5 million over five years to [Colleges and Institutes Canada](/organizations/colleges-and-institutes-canada/) to strengthen environment-related academic programs in [Tunisia](/places/tunisia/), preparing graduates for the ecological transition in regions hardest hit by climate change, as part of a larger $50 million federal investment in global education and skills training ([Academica Group](https://academica.ca/top-ten/cican-receives-5m-to-strengthen-environmental-programs-in-tunisia/)).

The federal public service numbered 345,282 people at 31 March 2026 — 270,477 in the core public administration and 74,805 in separate agencies — down from a peak of 367,772 in 2024 ([Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat](https://www.canada.ca/en/treasury-board-secretariat/services/innovation/human-resources-statistics/population-federal-public-service.html)). That is roughly 1 in every 120 people in Canada, which means the size of the federal workforce is itself a measure of how much of the country's governing capacity sits in federal departments and agencies rather than in the provinces or the private sector.

## FAQ

### What is the Government of Canada?

It is the federal executive of Canada — the Prime Minister, the Cabinet and the federal public service — operating under the Constitution Act, 1867 since Confederation on 1 July 1867. Canada is a federal state, a parliamentary democracy and a constitutional monarchy, and "the government" usually means the executive branch rather than Parliament as a whole.

### Who is the head of state and who is the head of government?

The Sovereign is the head of state and is represented in Canada by the Governor General, who performs formal executive acts such as granting royal assent and appointing the Prime Minister. The Prime Minister is the head of government, selects the Cabinet, and is responsible for the operations and policy of the government.

### What can the federal government legislate on that provinces cannot?

Under section 91 of the Constitution Act, 1867, Parliament controls matters of national interest such as defence, criminal law, currency, banking, citizenship, trade and commerce, and the postal service. Provinces control local matters under section 92, including education, hospitals, municipalities and property and civil rights; agriculture, immigration and pensions are shared.

### How big is the federal public service?

It numbered 345,282 people at 31 March 2026 — 270,477 in the core public administration and 74,805 in separate agencies — down from a 2024 peak of 367,772. The count covers active employees, Governor in Council appointees, deputy ministers and federal judges, but excludes RCMP and Canadian Armed Forces members.

### What happens if the government loses the confidence of the House of Commons?

The Prime Minister is expected to resign or seek the dissolution of Parliament so a general election can be held. Confidence questions include explicit confidence motions, the budget, supply motions and the Address in Reply to the Speech from the Throne.

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not name the current prime minister, governor general or ministers; it describes the offices, not the office-holders, because the roster changes with each Cabinet shuffle.
- It does not give a detailed breakdown of Crown corporations, tribunals and agents of Parliament; that taxonomy was not verified from a fully-read source.
- It does not present the 345,282 public-service figure as a count of everyone paid from federal funds: RCMP members, Canadian Armed Forces members, ministers' exempt staff and some separate agencies are excluded from the Treasury Board count.
- It does not claim the division of powers is fixed: amendments in 1940, 1951 and 1964 moved powers between orders, and court decisions continue to reshape the boundary.
- It does not treat the Government of Canada as the same thing as Parliament, the Crown alone, or any provincial government; each is a distinct part of the constitutional system.
