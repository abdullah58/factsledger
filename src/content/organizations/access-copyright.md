## Overview

Access Copyright is a Canadian non-profit copyright collective — legally The Canadian Copyright Licensing Agency — that licenses the copying of published books, journals and articles on behalf of writers, visual artists and publishers, collecting royalties from schools, universities, businesses and governments and distributing them to rights holders ([IP Osgoode](https://www.yorku.ca/osgoode/iposgoode/2021/02/08/access-copyright-and-the-path-to-extended-collective-licensing/)). Founded in 1988 as CANCOPY and based in [Toronto](/places/toronto/), it became the centre of two landmark [Supreme Court of Canada](/organizations/supreme-court-of-canada/) cases that redrew the line between collective licensing and fair dealing in [Canada](/places/canada/).

It is not a government body, and it is not the owner of the works it licenses: authors assign it only their reproduction and "making available" rights, and it licenses its whole repertoire as a single package ([IP Osgoode](https://www.yorku.ca/osgoode/iposgoode/2021/02/08/access-copyright-and-the-path-to-extended-collective-licensing/)). That distinction matters, because in 2021 the Supreme Court held that its tariffs bind only users who choose to be licensed — and that only the actual copyright owners, not the collective, can sue for infringement ([Fasken](https://www.fasken.com/en/knowledge/2021/10/ott-newsletter-york-university-v-access-copyright)).

## What Access Copyright does

Access Copyright exists to solve a clearance problem: before it existed, getting permission to copy a published work meant tracking down each rights holder individually, a task parliamentary testimony in 2018 described as onerous ([OpenParliament](https://openparliament.ca/committees/canadian-heritage/42-1/132/steven-blaney-9/?page=4)). The collective replaces that with blanket licences — pre-cleared permission to copy and share from its repertoire — while its member creators and publishers receive royalties for the use ([Registry of Lobbyists](https://lobbycanada.gc.ca/app/secure/ocl/lrs/do/vwRg?cno=4695&regId=822814&blnk=1)). Its membership is organised in two categories, creator groups and publisher groups, including the Canadian Artists' Representation, the Canadian Association of University Teachers, The Writers' Union of Canada and the Association of Canadian Publishers ([Registry of Lobbyists](https://lobbycanada.gc.ca/app/secure/ocl/lrs/do/vwRg?cno=4695&regId=822814&blnk=1)). Tariffs are negotiated with user groups under sections 68–70.1 of the Copyright Act, typically run for two to three years, and may or may not be certified by the Copyright Board of Canada ([IP Osgoode](https://www.yorku.ca/osgoode/iposgoode/2021/02/08/access-copyright-and-the-path-to-extended-collective-licensing/)). The organisation is led by chief executive Kate Edwards, who took the role in January 2024 after Roanie Levy (2013–2024) and Maureen Cavan ([Registry of Lobbyists](https://lobbycanada.gc.ca/app/secure/ocl/lrs/do/vwRg?cno=4695&regId=822814&blnk=1)).

## From CanCopy to the tariff wars

Access Copyright was formed in 1988 as CANCOPY and kept that name until 2002 ([Partnership](https://www.academia.edu/10072142/Recalibrating_Some_Copyright_Conceptions_Toward_a_Shared_and_Balanced_Approach_to_Educational_Copying)). Its first educational blanket licence came in 1991, with Ontario, for photocopying book and journal excerpts in K-12 schools; most other public systems followed within a few years ([Partnership](https://www.academia.edu/10072142/Recalibrating_Some_Copyright_Conceptions_Toward_a_Shared_and_Balanced_Approach_to_Educational_Copying)). By the 1990s collective licensing was the norm in schools, with terms negotiated by the Association of Universities and Colleges of Canada for its 95 member institutions ([OpenParliament](https://openparliament.ca/committees/canadian-heritage/42-1/132/steven-blaney-9/?page=4); [University of Prince Edward Island](https://www.libraryupei.ca/copyright/watch2011)). The post-secondary licences covered colleges and institutes as well as universities; Access Copyright is named in the profile of [Colleges and Institutes Canada](/organizations/colleges-and-institutes-canada/), the national association for the college sector. The uneasy bargain broke in 2010, when Access Copyright asked the Copyright Board for a post-secondary tariff raising royalties from about $3.50 to $45 per full-time-equivalent student — an effective increase of nearly 250% even after course-pack costs were counted. The Board granted an interim tariff on 23 December 2010, over objections from the AUCC, the Canadian Association of University Teachers and the Canadian Federation of Students ([University of Prince Edward Island](https://www.libraryupei.ca/copyright/watch2011)). York University then stopped paying part-way through the interim tariff's term and relied instead on its own Fair Dealing Guidelines ([IP Osgoode](https://www.yorku.ca/osgoode/iposgoode/2021/02/08/access-copyright-and-the-path-to-extended-collective-licensing/)).

Access Copyright's proposed jump from about $3.50 to $45 per full-time-equivalent student ([University of Prince Edward Island](https://www.libraryupei.ca/copyright/watch2011)) — a nearly thirteen-fold increase in the headline rate — is what turned a licensing negotiation into a decade of litigation, as universities concluded that operating outside the collective was cheaper than staying inside it.

## Two Supreme Court cases

Nine years earlier, in Alberta (Education) v Access Copyright, 2012 SCC 37, the Supreme Court held 5–4 that the Copyright Board had misapplied the fair-dealing test from CCH Canadian Ltd v Law Society of Upper Canada (2004) when it ruled teachers' photocopying of short textbook excerpts for students was not fair dealing, and sent the matter back to the Board ([Smart Biggar](https://www.smartbiggar.ca/insights/publication/-em-alberta-(education)-v-canadian-copyright-licensing-agency-(access-copyright)-em-)). The majority held teachers share a "symbiotic purpose" with students doing research or private study, that the amount copied is judged against the whole work rather than the number of copies, and that buying a textbook for every student was not a realistic alternative ([Smart Biggar](https://www.smartbiggar.ca/insights/publication/-em-alberta-(education)-v-canadian-copyright-licensing-agency-(access-copyright)-em-)). Then, in York University v Access Copyright, 2021 SCC 32, a unanimous court under Justice Rosalie Abella dismissed both sides' appeals: Copyright Board tariffs are not mandatory, users may meet copying needs through other means, and a collective cannot force a licence on an unwilling user ([Fasken](https://www.fasken.com/en/knowledge/2021/10/ott-newsletter-york-university-v-access-copyright)). The Court declined to decide whether York's Guidelines are fair, but said it does not endorse the lower courts' reasoning, which analysed fairness from the institution's view instead of each student's ([Fasken](https://www.fasken.com/en/knowledge/2021/10/ott-newsletter-york-university-v-access-copyright)).

## FAQ

### What is Access Copyright?

Access Copyright is a Canadian non-profit copyright collective, legally The Canadian Copyright Licensing Agency, that licenses the copying of published works on behalf of writers, visual artists and publishers. Founded in 1988 as CANCOPY and based in Toronto, it collects royalties from institutions and distributes them to creators and publishers.

### Is Access Copyright a government agency?

No. It is a non-profit collective society, not a government body. The government institution in the system is the Copyright Board of Canada, which sets or approves the tariffs that Access Copyright proposes and collects under. Access Copyright itself negotiates licences and distributes royalties to its members.

### What did the Supreme Court decide in York University v Access Copyright?

In 2021 SCC 32, a unanimous court held that Copyright Board tariffs are not mandatory: users may meet their copying needs through other means, and a collective can enforce payment only against users who choose to be licensed. The Court declined to decide whether York's Fair Dealing Guidelines were fair.

### How is Access Copyright different from SOCAN and Copibec?

SOCAN is the Canadian music performing-rights collective, while Access Copyright covers published text and visual works. Copibec is the equivalent reprography collective for Quebec; Access Copyright's repertoire covers textual works in the rest of Canada. Neither is a government agency.

### Who runs Access Copyright?

Kate Edwards has been chief executive since January 2024, succeeding Roanie Levy (2013–2024) and Maureen Cavan. It is headquartered in Toronto, Ontario. Its membership is organised into creator groups and publisher groups, including writers' unions, artists' associations and publishers' associations across Canada.
