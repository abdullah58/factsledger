## Overview

Asana, Inc. is a work-management software company based in [San Francisco](/places/san-francisco/), California, in the [United States](/places/united-states/), founded in 2008 by [Facebook](/organizations/facebook/) co-founder Dustin Moskovitz and early Facebook engineer Justin Rosenstein ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/11/29/asana-a-work-management-platform-nabs-50m-growth-round-at-a-1-5b-valuation/)). Its platform lets teams plan, track, and manage work — tasks, projects, portfolios, and goals — on a data model Asana calls the Work Graph, and it went public on the New York Stock Exchange through a direct listing on September 30, 2020 ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/asana-goes-public-here-are-the-big-winners/)). For the fiscal year ended January 31, 2026, Asana reported revenue of $790.8 million, up 9% year over year, with a GAAP net loss of $189.0 million and its first non-GAAP operating profit, $56.7 million ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477720/000147772026000012/asana8-kex991q4fy26.htm)). Since July 2025 the company has been led by its second chief executive, Dan Rogers, as it rebuilds the product around AI agents ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250625491002/en/Asana-Names-Dan-Rogers-as-Chief-Executive-Officer)).

Asana began as an internal fix. At Facebook, Moskovitz — the company's co-founder, first chief technology officer, and VP of Engineering until November 2008 — and Rosenstein built a tool called Tasks to help coworkers manage their workloads; in late 2008 they left to turn it into a company ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2011/11/03/video-interview-the-founders-of-asana-declare-war-against-work-about-work/)). After about two years in production and lengthy beta testing, the product launched to the public for free on November 2, 2011, with the founders declaring "war against work about work" — the meetings, status updates, and email chains that surround real work rather than doing it ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2011/11/03/video-interview-the-founders-of-asana-declare-war-against-work-about-work/)). By the time of the launch they had raised $10.2 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2011/11/03/video-interview-the-founders-of-asana-declare-war-against-work-about-work/)).

## The funding ladder: $9 million to a $1.5 billion unicorn

Asana raised in steps, roughly one round every two years, and each step repriced the company sharply upward. Benchmark led a $9 million Series A in November 2009, and Founders Fund led a $28 million Series B in July 2012 at a $280 million valuation ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/asana-goes-public-here-are-the-big-winners/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/03/30/task-management-app-asana-raises-50m-at-a-600m-valuation-led-by-ycs-sam-altman/)). In March 2016 came a $50 million Series C at a $600 million valuation, led by Y Combinator president Sam Altman and joined by a roster of founder-investors including 8VC, Founders Fund, Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan, Tony Hsieh, and Adam D'Angelo ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/03/30/task-management-app-asana-raises-50m-at-a-600m-valuation-led-by-ycs-sam-altman/)). At that point Asana had 13,000 paying businesses, up from 10,000 six months earlier, and over 140,000 businesses using the product, with annual recurring revenue said to have more than doubled for four consecutive years ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/03/30/task-management-app-asana-raises-50m-at-a-600m-valuation-led-by-ycs-sam-altman/)).

The last two rounds were both led by Generation Investment Management, the London firm backed by former US Vice President Al Gore. The January 2018 Series D brought in $75 million, took total funding to $168 million, and was reported at a $900 million valuation; Generation's Colin le Duc joined the board ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/01/31/asana-raises-75m/)). Eleven months later the $50 million Series E valued Asana at $1.5 billion — unicorn status — and took total funding since founding to $213 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/11/29/asana-a-work-management-platform-nabs-50m-growth-round-at-a-1-5b-valuation/)). By then Asana counted 50,000 paying organizations and "millions" of free users, with customers in 195 countries, and was spending the money on international expansion — a Frankfurt data center and offices in Sydney and Tokyo — plus Portfolios, Timelines, a Business tier, and its first machine-learning features ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/11/29/asana-a-work-management-platform-nabs-50m-growth-round-at-a-1-5b-valuation/)).

Moskovitz framed the market Asana was chasing as three buckets — file collaboration, messaging, and work management — and put Asana in the third, where TechCrunch named [Airtable](/organizations/airtable/), Wrike, Trello, and Basecamp as the alternatives ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/11/29/asana-a-work-management-platform-nabs-50m-growth-round-at-a-1-5b-valuation/)). That positioning also explains the site's own [ClickUp](/organizations/clickup/) profile, which names Asana as the competing productivity platform ClickUp set out to displace with an all-in-one product. Asana's own differentiator, in its telling, is the Work Graph: unlike a folder-style container model in which a piece of work lives in one place, the Work Graph maps the relationships between units of work, the people responsible for them, the processes they run through, and the information attached to them — a model the company now sells as the memory and governance layer that lets AI agents operate inside real workflows ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477720/000147772026000012/asana8-kex991q4fy26.htm)).

## The direct listing

Asana went public on September 30, 2020 through a direct listing rather than a traditional IPO — the same day Palantir did the same, and after Spotify and Slack had shown the route worked ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/asana-goes-public-here-are-the-big-winners/)). No new shares were sold and no underwriters set a price: the New York Stock Exchange published a $21 reference price, the first trade came at $27, and the shares closed the first day at $28.80, a 37% gain ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/podcast/palantir-asana-first-trading-day/)). TechCrunch put the company at about $4.3 billion at the close, and about $4 billion on a fully diluted basis at the reference price ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/02/asanas-strong-direct-listing-lights-alternative-path-to-public-market-for-saas-startups/)). The listing was a test of whether a lesser-known, cash-hungry SaaS company could go public this way — and the verdict, TechCrunch wrote, was that it worked, revaluing Asana to roughly double its last private price ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/02/asanas-strong-direct-listing-lights-alternative-path-to-public-market-for-saas-startups/)).

The debut also made the cap table public. Benchmark held 10.4% of the company (14,012,703 Class B shares), Generation Investment Management 7.3%, and Founders Fund 8,713,329 Class B shares ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/asana-goes-public-here-are-the-big-winners/)). Moskovitz retained a 36% stake and Rosenstein 16.1%, and because there was no lockup period, employees and insiders were free to trade from day one ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/podcast/palantir-asana-first-trading-day/)). The numbers behind the listing were strong but deteriorating: in the six months ending July 31, 2020, revenue grew 63% year over year at an 87% gross margin, but the net loss widened to $76.9 million from $30.5 million a year earlier, and operating cash burn rose to $40.3 million from $13.1 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/02/asanas-strong-direct-listing-lights-alternative-path-to-public-market-for-saas-startups/)). TechCrunch's aside was telling: not every startup has "a billionaire founder willing to lend it a few hundred million ahead of its debut" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/10/02/asanas-strong-direct-listing-lights-alternative-path-to-public-market-for-saas-startups/)).

## Slower growth, a 9% cut, and a founder who kept buying

The growth that carried Asana to its listing did not survive the public markets. Revenue growth fell from 63% in the first half of 2020 to 9% by fiscal 2026, while the company stayed loss-making on a GAAP basis every year — a $189.0 million net loss in fiscal 2026, albeit down from $255.5 million the year before ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477720/000147772026000012/asana8-kex991q4fy26.htm)). On November 15, 2022, Asana authorized a restructuring cutting global headcount by approximately 9% to improve operational efficiency and operating costs, at an estimated $9–11 million in non-recurring charges ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477720/000147772022000054/asan-20221115.htm)). The trade it made in exchange was margin: fiscal 2026 delivered non-GAAP operating income of $56.7 million against a $40.8 million loss the year before, and adjusted free cash flow of $84.5 million against $2.6 million — the first year the business looked profitable on the measures management prefers, even as the GAAP loss remained large because of $214.8 million in stock-based compensation ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477720/000147772026000012/asana8-kex991q4fy26.htm)).

Moskovitz's response to the falling share price was the opposite of most founders: he bought. By February 24, 2022 he had spent $1,188,809,342.03 of his own money buying Asana Class A shares on the open market under 10b5-1 trading plans — Good Ventures Foundation spent a further $69.3 million — leaving him beneficial owner of 93,376,817 Class A shares, about 54.7% of the outstanding Class A ([SEC](http://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477720/000119312522068114/d318146dsc13da.htm)). A founder buying more than a billion dollars of his own public company's stock, rather than selling after the listing, is the single most unusual fact in Asana's story, and it left him a majority holder of the voting stock while the company was still losing money.

## The AI pivot and a new chief executive

Asana's answer to slowing growth is to stop being described as a task tracker. In June 2024 it unveiled "AI teammates" — autonomous agents that work inside the platform — and on October 22, 2024 it launched AI Studio, a no-code builder that lets teams design workflows, embed AI agents in them without code, and deploy them where teams already work ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/3578614/asana-launches-ai-studio-a-no-code-tool-for-building-ai-agents.html)). Agents can run on Anthropic's Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Haiku or OpenAI's GPT-4o and GPT-4o mini, and a team can require an agent to seek human approval before higher-risk actions such as sending an external email ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/3578614/asana-launches-ai-studio-a-no-code-tool-for-building-ai-agents.html)). The pitch rests on the company's own research: its 2024 State of Work Innovation report found employees spend 53% of their time on low-value busywork ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/3578614/asana-launches-ai-studio-a-no-code-tool-for-building-ai-agents.html)).

The pivot accelerated after the founder stepped back. On March 10, 2025, Moskovitz told the board he intended to retire as chief executive and move to Chair once a successor began, keeping his shareholdings and focusing on product vision, AI strategy, and his philanthropy through Good Ventures and Open Philanthropy ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477720/000147772025000032/asanapressrelease31025.htm)). On June 25, 2025, Asana named Dan Rogers — chief executive of LaunchDarkly since 2023, previously president of Rubrik and chief marketing officer at ServiceNow — as chief executive effective July 21, 2025, with Moskovitz remaining Board Chair ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250625491002/en/Asana-Names-Dan-Rogers-as-Chief-Executive-Officer)). Under Rogers the company now calls itself "the operating system for human-agent teams," and on May 28, 2026 it put $75 million behind that claim by acquiring StackAI, a no-code platform for building AI agents that operate inside existing business systems such as Salesforce and Slack; StackAI's founders, Tony Rosinol and Bernard Aceituno, joined Asana ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/28/asana-acquires-no-code-agent-builder-stack-ai/)). TechCrunch noted the context: Asana had lost more than half its market value since the introduction of ChatGPT, a slide that worsened when Moskovitz left as CEO, even as revenue kept growing steadily ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/28/asana-acquires-no-code-agent-builder-stack-ai/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Who founded Asana?** Dustin Moskovitz, a co-founder of Facebook, and Justin Rosenstein, an early Facebook engineer, founded Asana in San Francisco in 2008 after building an internal tool called Tasks at Facebook ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/11/29/asana-a-work-management-platform-nabs-50m-growth-round-at-a-1-5b-valuation/)).

**How did Asana go public?** Through a direct listing on the New York Stock Exchange on September 30, 2020, not a traditional IPO — no new shares were sold and no underwriters set a price. The NYSE set a $21 reference price, shares opened at $27 and closed the first day at $28.80 ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/asana-goes-public-here-are-the-big-winners/)).

**How much money has Asana raised?** $213 million in private funding across Series A to Series E between 2009 and 2018, ending at a $1.5 billion valuation; it has raised no new primary capital since, going public by direct listing instead ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/11/29/asana-a-work-management-platform-nabs-50m-growth-round-at-a-1-5b-valuation/)).

**Is Asana profitable?** Not on a GAAP basis — it lost $189.0 million in fiscal 2026 — but it reported its first non-GAAP operating profit that year ($56.7 million) and $84.5 million of adjusted free cash flow, after a 9% headcount cut in 2022 ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1477720/000147772026000012/asana8-kex991q4fy26.htm)).

**Who runs Asana now?** Dan Rogers, formerly chief executive of LaunchDarkly, became CEO on July 21, 2025, succeeding co-founder Dustin Moskovitz, who moved to Board Chair ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250625491002/en/Asana-Names-Dan-Rogers-as-Chief-Executive-Officer)).
