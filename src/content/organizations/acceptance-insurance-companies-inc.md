# [Acceptance Insurance](/organizations/acceptance-insurance/) Companies Inc.

**Acceptance Insurance Companies Inc. (AICI) was a Delaware-incorporated
insurance holding company headquartered in [Council Bluffs](/places/council-bluffs-iowa/), [Iowa](/places/iowa/), whose
crop-insurance subsidiary, American Growers Insurance Company, became the
largest participant in the federal crop insurance program before collapsing
in 2002 under drought losses and bloated operating costs.** AICI filed for
Chapter 11 bankruptcy in January 2005. Its reinsurance fight with Granite
Reinsurance ended in 2009 with the Eighth Circuit ordering AICI to pay the
full $15 million premium, and its Fifth Amendment claim against the United
States over a blocked sale of its crop book ended when the Supreme Court
denied certiorari in 2010.

## A specialty insurer goes big on crop insurance

AICI was, in the GAO's words, "a publicly owned holding company that sold
specialty property and casualty insurance." In 1993 it acquired American
Growers Insurance Company, then a federal crop insurance writer. As a
wholly owned subsidiary, American Growers sold and serviced federal crop
policies and shared its parent's management, while another AICI subsidiary,
[American Agrisurance Inc.](/organizations/american-agrisurance/), served as the marketing arm (GAO-04-517).

The growth strategy worked until it didn't. In 2001 AICI's subsidiaries
agreed to buy the crop insurance assets of IGF Insurance from Goran
Capital, financing the deal with a stop-loss reinsurance contract from
Granite Reinsurance that ran from July 1, 2000 through June 30, 2005 —
Granite Re would cover losses between 140% and 150% of net retained
premium income, up to $40 million, for a fixed $15 million premium. Then
prolonged Midwestern drought erased the underwriting gains American
Growers had budgeted on. The subsidiary had been spending about $130 for
every $100 the Risk Management Agency reimbursed for selling and servicing
policies, paying agent commissions roughly 12% above the industry average —
and a mispriced new product, Crop Revenue Coverage Plus, compounded the
bleeding (GAO-04-517; 8th Cir. opinion).

## The Rain and Hail sale and the seizure

In November 2002, with American Growers' surplus below state minimums,
AICI signed a non-binding letter of intent to sell its crop insurance book
— roughly 360,000 policies — to Rain and Hail LLC for $21.5 million. The
USDA's Risk Management Agency rejected the sale under its regulatory
authority as reinsurer. On November 22, 2002, the Nebraska Department of
Insurance placed American Growers under supervision as in "hazardous
financial condition"; the company later went into court-ordered
rehabilitation (December 20, 2002) and statutory liquidation (February 28,
2005). The RMA ultimately spent about $40.5 million funding the company's
operations to pay farmers' claims and transfer policies (GAO-04-517).

AICI's own account, in its Supreme Court certiorari petition, says RMA
concluded the sale "would be detrimental to the public interest" because it
would "adversely affect the interests of farmers and taxpayers." AICI then
sued the United States, claiming the blocked sale was a Fifth Amendment
taking of its property. The Court of Federal Claims dismissed the case and
the Federal Circuit affirmed, holding that by voluntarily entering a
federally reinsured line of business subject to RMA sale approval, the
company had no cognizable property right in selling the book to a buyer of
its choosing. The Supreme Court denied certiorari in 2010 (559 U.S. 1106,
No. 09-771; DOJ Brief in Opposition).

## The Granite Re fight

Granite Re claimed AICI owed the $9 million in premium deposits it had
never paid (plus interest, $10.875 million total in its proof of claim).
AICI countered that the contract lacked consideration and demanded back
the $6 million it had paid. After a 2007 trial, the bankruptcy court let
Granite Re keep the $6 million but denied the remaining premiums; the
Bankruptcy Appellate Panel reversed, awarding the full $9 million in
unpaid deposits. In May 2009 the Eighth Circuit affirmed: the reinsurance
contract was enforceable, AICI's subsidiary was bound by it, and the
company owed the full $15 million — a fixed premium that did not depend
on whether it wrote any insurance at all.

## FAQ

**Was Acceptance Insurance Companies Inc. related to AIG?**
No. A text search of AICI's Q2 2004 10-Q for "AIG" returns zero matches;
AICI was an independent, publicly traded holding company.

**Why did American Growers fail?**
The GAO attributed the 2002 failure to a series of company decisions that
drained surplus — losses from the mispriced Crop Revenue Coverage Plus
product, above-average agent commissions, the 2001 IGF asset purchase —
leaving the company vulnerable when drought erased anticipated
underwriting gains.

**What happened to AICI's NYSE listing?**
Insurance Journal reported in December 2002 that the New York Stock
Exchange suspended Acceptance's shares for failing to meet listing
standards; they last closed at 55 cents and continued to trade
over-the-counter.

**Did the government pay anything for blocking the sale?**
No. AICI's takings claim was dismissed, and the Supreme Court denied
review, so no compensation was ever awarded.

**What did the Granite Re dispute cost AICI?**
The courts held AICI owed Granite Re the full $15 million premium under
the reinsurance contract — the $6 million already paid plus $9 million in
unpaid deposits.
