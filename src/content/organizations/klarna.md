## Overview

Klarna Group plc is a Swedish financial-technology company that pioneered buy now, pay later (BNPL) payments: shoppers receive goods before paying, while Klarna takes on the credit and fraud risk for the merchant ([Global Venturing](https://globalventuring.com/blog/2011/12/09/klarna-takes-payment-from-dst/)). Founded in Stockholm in 2005 by three economics students — Sebastian Siemiatkowski, Niklas Adalberth and Victor Jacobsson — as Kreditor, renamed Klarna in 2009, it grew from a Nordic invoicing service into a licensed Swedish bank operating in 26 markets, and on September 10, 2025 it listed on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker KLAR at $40 a share, raising about $1.37 billion in the largest US IPO of 2025 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2011/12/08/klarna-155-millio/); [Global Venturing](https://globalventuring.com/blog/2011/12/09/klarna-takes-payment-from-dst/); [Associated Press](https://virginiabusiness.com/klarna-ipo-nyse-debut-2025/)).

The scale of the business is the core of the Klarna story: by the fourth quarter of 2025 it served 118 million active consumers and 966,000 merchants, processed $127.9 billion of gross merchandise volume in the year, and reported revenue of $3.5 billion — but also a net loss of $273 million, after its first full-year profit of $21 million in 2024 ([Sharecast](https://www.investments.lloydsbank.com/markets-and-commentary/market-news/article/?id=21764108&type=bsm); [PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/whats-behind-klarnas-14b-ipo-valuation)). That swing — rapid growth, thin profitability, and a share price down almost 70% from its IPO within five months — is the tension running through the company's two decades.

## From Kreditor to a European payments network

Klarna's original idea was deliberately old-fashioned: let online shoppers buy the way they do in physical stores, touching and feeling goods before paying for them ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/12/18/klarna-sofort/)). Behind that promise sat credit-risk technology that let Klarna assume the risk a merchant would otherwise carry, paying the merchant on order while the shopper paid later ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2011/12/08/klarna-155-millio/)). By December 2011 the model had attracted a $155 million round led by [General Atlantic](/organizations/general-atlantic/) and [DST Global](/organizations/dst-global/), with Sequoia Capital as an existing backer; the company then had 600 employees and was clearing $2.5 billion of payments from 6 million consumers across 14,000 merchants ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2011/12/08/klarna-155-millio/)).

Expansion followed by acquisition. In December 2013 Klarna bought Germany's SOFORT, the operator of Sofortüberweisung, for a reported ~$150 million; together the two companies covered 14 countries and 43,000 merchants with 25 million users, about 10% of greater Europe's $100 billion e-commerce market, and the deal formed Klarna Group ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/12/18/klarna-sofort/)). Klarna entered the US buy-now-pay-later market in 2015 in partnership with Macy's ([Associated Press](https://virginiabusiness.com/klarna-ipo-nyse-debut-2025/)), and in June 2017 it received a full banking license from Finansinspektionen, the Swedish Financial Supervisory Authority, legally becoming Klarna Bank while continuing to trade as Klarna — by then serving 60 million customers and 70,000 merchants ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2017/06/19/klarna-gets-a-full-banking-license-gears-up-to-go-beyond-financing-payments/)). The license let it fund lending through customer deposits, a cheaper source of capital than the debt and securitizations its US rivals relied on, at the cost of bank-grade regulation ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/whats-behind-klarnas-14b-ipo-valuation)).

## A $39 billion round trip to the public market

Klarna's valuation arc is one of the sharpest in European tech. A SoftBank Vision Fund 2-led round in June 2021 valued the company at $45.6 billion, making it Europe's most valuable venture-backed company ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/fintech-klarna-shares-pop-nyse-market/)). Barely a year later the market had turned: in May 2022 Klarna cut about 10% of its roughly 7,000-strong workforce, blaming inflation and the war in Ukraine, and in July it raised $800 million at a $6.7 billion valuation — an ~85% fall — with a second, smaller round of cuts that September taking headcount to around 6,000 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/09/22/with-new-cuts-klarna-joins-the-ranks-of-companies-having-to-conduct-more-than-one-layoff/)).

The road back ran through the United States. In March 2025 Klarna filed its F-1 prospectus with the [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](/organizations/us-securities-and-exchange-commission/), then paused the offering in early April after tariff announcements rattled markets ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/fintech-klarna-shares-pop-nyse-market/)). The same month it announced a partnership with OnePay to exclusively power installment loans at Walmart in the US, displacing Affirm as the retailer's BNPL provider — a win PitchBook called a major US-growth lever ([Klarna](https://investors.klarna.com/News--Events/news/news-details/2025/Klarna-Announces-Partnership-with-OnePay-to-Exclusively-Power-Installment-Loans-at-Walmart-in-the-U-S--2025-C2mmyrNcu1/default.aspx); [PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/whats-behind-klarnas-14b-ipo-valuation)). Klarna followed [Chime](/organizations/chime/) into the reopening IPO window — Chime had gone public on [Nasdaq](/organizations/nasdaq/) on June 12, 2025, its shares rising 37% on the first day ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/fintech-klarna-shares-pop-nyse-market/)) — and priced its own offering at $40 a share on September 9, above the marketed $35–$37 range. On September 10 the shares opened at $52, peaked near $57, and closed at $45.82, up about 15%, with ~34.3 million shares sold for ~$1.37 billion; the $40 pricing implied a ~$15.1 billion valuation and the market capitalization after the first day stood at ~$17.5 billion ([Associated Press](https://virginiabusiness.com/klarna-ipo-nyse-debut-2025/); [Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/public/fintech-klarna-shares-pop-nyse-market/)). [JPMorgan Chase](/organizations/jpmorgan-chase/) and [Goldman Sachs](/organizations/goldman-sachs/) were the lead underwriters, and at the IPO price co-founder Sebastian Siemiatkowski's ~7% stake was worth around $1 billion, while Sequoia Capital held ~21% ([Associated Press](https://virginiabusiness.com/klarna-ipo-nyse-debut-2025/)).

## AI, thin profits, and the post-IPO slide

Klarna was also one of the loudest corporate champions of AI-driven cost-cutting: it built an assistant powered by OpenAI in 2023 and pursued an aggressive AI-first workforce-reduction strategy, then had to reverse course in 2025 when customer-service quality suffered ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/whats-behind-klarnas-14b-ipo-valuation)). The financials behind the IPO told a similar story of progress with a thin margin for error. In 2024 Klarna reported its first full-year profit since 2019 — $21 million of net income on $2.81 billion of revenue and $105 billion of gross merchandise volume — but the profit rested on a $181 million adjusted operating figure that excluded one-time charges and non-cash items ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/whats-behind-klarnas-14b-ipo-valuation)).

The first results as a public company undid that narrative. For full-year 2025 Klarna reported revenue of $3.5 billion, up 25%, and gross merchandise volume of $127.9 billion, up 22%, but swung to a net loss of $273 million from the $21 million profit a year earlier; the fourth quarter was its first billion-dollar revenue quarter at $1.1 billion, yet still produced a $26 million net loss ([Sharecast](https://www.investments.lloydsbank.com/markets-and-commentary/market-news/article/?id=21764108&type=bsm)). Shares fell 26% on the results, taking the drop since the September IPO to almost 70%, as investors weighed rising credit losses and heavier regulation against a consumer base that kept growing — 118 million active consumers, up 28%, and 966,000 merchants, up 42% ([Sharecast](https://www.investments.lloydsbank.com/markets-and-commentary/market-news/article/?id=21764108&type=bsm)).
