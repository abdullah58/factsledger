## Overview

The Federal Aviation Administration is the U.S. federal regulator of civil
aviation — certifying airplanes and pilots, running air traffic control, and
licensing commercial space launches
([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/about/mission/activities)). Created by the Federal
Aviation Act of 1958 as the Federal Aviation Agency, it took its present name in
1967 when it became part of the U.S. Department of Transportation
([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/about/mission/activities)).

## What it is known for

The FAA is both the promoter and the policeman of American aviation: its own
mission statement pairs "encouraging and developing civil aeronautics" with
"regulating civil aviation to promote safety"
([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/about/mission/activities)). It sets the rules for
flight — issuing and enforcing minimum standards for aircraft manufacturing,
operation, and maintenance, and certifying airmen and airports ([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/about/mission/activities)).

It also operates the nation's airspace — airport towers, air route traffic
control centers, and flight service stations, plus the navigation facilities
they depend on ([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/about/mission/activities)). Abroad,
it exchanges aeronautical information with foreign authorities and negotiates
bilateral airworthiness agreements
([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/about/mission/activities)).

## Regulatory role

Beyond standards and airspace, the FAA's remit also includes the
administrative machinery of flight: registering aircraft, recording title documents, and
developing specifications for aeronautical charts
([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/about/mission/activities)).

That breadth made the FAA a central — and contested — actor in the defining
aviation-safety episode of recent decades.

## The 737 MAX grounding and oversight debate

On October 29, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed; on March 10, 2019,
Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed. Both were Boeing 737 MAX aircraft,
and the two crashes led to a 20-month worldwide grounding of the fleet, with
deliveries halted until November 2020
([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12843)) — and turned attention
from Boeing to its regulator. Certification of the MAX prompted intense
scrutiny of the FAA's delegation of certification responsibilities to
Boeing: the Organization Designation Authorization (ODA) program, which
tasks dedicated units at companies such as Boeing with carrying out testing,
evaluation, and certification functions on its behalf. The scrutiny
culminated in legislation: the 2020 Aircraft Certification, Safety, and
Accountability Act (Division V of P.L. 116-260) required the FAA to reform
certification policy and mandated industry-wide FAA-approved safety
management system (SMS) programs
([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12843)).

A House Transportation Committee investigation — 18 months, ending in a
246-page report released in September 2020 — blamed the crashes (346 dead) on
what its staff called the "horrific culmination" of failed government
oversight, design flaws, and inaction at Boeing despite known problems
([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/economy/2020/9/16/us-congress-report-blasts-boeing-and-faa-over-737-max-failures)).
Investigators found FAA managers overruling technical and safety experts at
Boeing's behest, and found that four Boeing employees acting as FAA
"authorized representatives" knew about a test pilot's slow response to the
MCAS software without evidence they reported it to the agency
([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/economy/2020/9/16/us-congress-report-blasts-boeing-and-faa-over-737-max-failures)).
Both the FAA and Boeing maintained that certification had complied with FAA
regulations, and chairman Peter DeFazio said the planned reforms would not
scrap the delegation program
([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/economy/2020/9/16/us-congress-report-blasts-boeing-and-faa-over-737-max-failures)).

The debate did not end with the grounding. In 2022, Boeing was stripped of
its delegated authority to issue airworthiness certification for completed
787 aircraft pending quality-control improvements
([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12843)). On January 5, 2024, a
door plug separated in flight from an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX (some
injuries, no fatalities), prompting an FAA investigation, a production audit,
and a 38-per-month production cap pending a Boeing action plan
([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12843)). An October 2024
Department of Transportation Office of Inspector General audit found the
FAA's oversight processes for identifying and rectifying Boeing production
problems "not effective" — the agency does not use data-driven methods to
target oversight and lacks a tracking system capable of identifying
systematic compliance deficiencies
([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12843)). The FAA Reauthorization
Act of 2024 (P.L. 118-63) mandates annual ethics training for ODA unit
members and an FAA forum for ODA best practices
([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12843)).

## Leadership and governance

An Administrator manages the FAA, assisted by a Deputy Administrator. Five
Associate Administrators direct the line-of-business organizations; the Chief
Counsel and nine Assistant Administrators also report to the Administrator
([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/about/mission/activities)). The agency has nine
geographical regions and two major centers — the Mike Monroney Aeronautical
Center and the William J. Hughes Technical Center
([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/about/mission/activities)) — though the FAA's
public organization page was last updated in 2016, so this structure may have
changed.

## Timeline

- **1958** — The Federal Aviation Act creates the Federal Aviation Agency.
- **1967** — It joins the U.S. Department of Transportation and becomes the Federal Aviation Administration.
- **2018–2019** — Lion Air Flight 610 (Oct 29, 2018) and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 (Mar 10, 2019) crash; the 737 MAX is grounded worldwide.
- **2020** — House investigation ends with a 246-page report; the grounding ends in November; Congress passes the certification-reform act.
- **2022** — Boeing is stripped of delegated authority to certify completed 787s airworthy.
- **2024** — An Alaska Airlines 737 MAX door-plug mishap (Jan 5) brings a manufacturing investigation, a production audit, and a 38-per-month cap; the FAA Reauthorization Act mandates ODA ethics training.

## Related entities

The FAA's principal relationships are with the U.S. Department of
Transportation (its parent agency), Boeing (the sole U.S. producer of large
transport airplanes and the main focus of FAA aircraft oversight, per the
CRS), and the National Transportation Safety Board (whose investigations of
the 2024 737 rudder incidents the CRS describes
([CRS](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12843))). Its European
counterpart is a distinct body — the European Union Aviation Safety Agency
(EASA), responsible for safety and environmental protection in European air
transport, established in 2002 and headquartered in Cologne, Germany
([EU](https://european-union.europa.eu/institutions-law-budget/institutions-and-bodies/search-all-eu-institutions-and-bodies/european-union-aviation-safety-agency-easa_en)).
