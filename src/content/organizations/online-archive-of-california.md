## The archive that grew out of a data standard

The Online Archive of California is unusual among digital libraries: it exists because of a file format. In 1993, UC Berkeley Libraries built an SGML prototype for describing archival collections — the seed of what became Encoded Archival Description (EAD), the international standard for archival finding aids. When responsibility for the EAD DTD moved to the Society of American Archivists and the Library of Congress in 1995, UC librarians and archivists saw the opening: they formed the UC-EAD consortium to build a prototype union database of EAD-encoded records, backed by the UC Office of the President and the California State Library's LSTA grants program.

The prototype was built between 1995 and 1997, mostly by retrospectively converting existing records. In June 1997, participation opened to other California repositories, and the project was renamed the Online Archive of California to reflect its widened eligibility. In 1998 it was formally folded into the California Digital Library, which immediately seeded it with encoding projects: JARDA (the Japanese American Relocation Digital Archive) and MOAC (Museums and the Online Archive of California), followed by the Local History Digital Resources Project in 2001 and California Cultures in 2006.

## What it became

Today the OAC gives free public access to over 78,000 finding aids maintained by more than 350 statewide partners — libraries, special collections, archives, historical societies and museums, plus all ten University of California campuses. Since 1998 it has evolved repeatedly to track encoding standards, technology and contributor feedback, with major redesigns in 2002 and 2008 (the latter unifying MARC records and EAD in one search).

In July 2025 the CDL launched the OAC's first full rebuild in nearly two decades: completely reconstructed with community-supported open-source technologies, with a mobile-responsive interface meeting WCAG 2.2 accessibility guidelines, new search and browse options, and a self-serve dashboard letting contributing institutions preview and publish their own finding aids.

Independent library-press coverage documents the OAC's role in preserving at-risk media: Library Journal's infoDOCKET reported the addition of 33 digitized sound and video recordings to the OAC and Calisphere — a pilot project in which the California AudioVisual Preservation Project digitized fragile recordings, including a demo of Apple's "Lisa" personal computer, film footage of the 1915 Panama-Pacific International Exposition, and a 1963 interview with Malcolm X. ## What this profile does not claim

- That the 1993 SGML prototype was itself called the OAC; the OAC name dates to June 1997.
- Current budget or staffing figures; the only published numbers are 2018 survey snapshots and are presented as such.
- Details of the post-2025 technology stack beyond "community-supported open-source technologies," which is all CDL has published.
