## ProPublica's enforcement arm

On November 30, 2023, the Senate Judiciary Committee voted 11–0 to
authorize subpoenas of billionaire Harlan Crow and conservative legal
activist Leonard Leo — the first time in decades the committee had aimed
its compulsory power at friends of the Supreme Court. Every Republican
had walked out before the vote, leaving Democrats to dispute whether a
quorum even existed
([Oklahoma Voice](https://oklahomavoice.com/2023/11/30/judiciary-committee-votes-to-subpoena-supreme-court-allies-in-ethics-probe/)).

The investigation was, by the chairman's own account, downstream of
journalism. Chair Dick Durbin said the probe had been spurred by
[ProPublica](https://factsledger.com/organizations/propublica/)'s
reporting: in April 2023 it revealed that Justice Clarence Thomas had
never disclosed private-jet and yacht travel and a real-estate deal
with Crow, and in June 2023 it reported that Justice Samuel Alito had
attended an Alaska fishing trip organized by donors including Leo
([Oklahoma Voice](https://oklahomavoice.com/2023/11/30/judiciary-committee-votes-to-subpoena-supreme-court-allies-in-ethics-probe/)).
Five months earlier, the committee had advanced the Supreme Court
Ethics, Recusal, and Transparency Act on a party-line 11–10 vote; the
bill was placed on the Senate calendar but never reached the floor. On
November 13, 2023 — two weeks before the subpoena fight — the Court
itself adopted a 15-page code of conduct for the first time, which
Durbin said "falls far short"
([Oklahoma Voice](https://oklahomavoice.com/2023/11/30/judiciary-committee-votes-to-subpoena-supreme-court-allies-in-ethics-probe/)).
Durbin called Crow and Leo "central players in this crisis"; the
Republican minority had filed 177 amendments to stall the markup.
Leo's attorney denounced the inquiry as partisan and declined
cooperation; Crow's office called the vote "invalid" while offering to
engage with the committee
([Oklahoma Voice](https://oklahomavoice.com/2023/11/30/judiciary-committee-votes-to-subpoena-supreme-court-allies-in-ethics-probe/)).
The episode was the committee's most aggressive intervention into the
Court's ethics since the 1969 Fortas affair
([Oklahoma Voice](https://oklahomavoice.com/2023/11/30/judiciary-committee-votes-to-subpoena-supreme-court-allies-in-ethics-probe/)).

## The partisan pendulum, 1816 to Bork

Established in 1816 during the second session of the 14th Congress as one
of the Senate's original standing committees, Judiciary began with four
members focused on courts and law enforcement
([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/)).
After the Civil War it took over law related to restoring the Confederate
states, and immigration returned to its jurisdiction in 1946
([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/)).
Lyman Trumbull's 1861–1872 chairmanship authored the Confiscation Acts,
the 13th Amendment, and the Civil Rights Act of 1866
([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/)).

Then came the longest and darkest chairmanship. James Eastland led the
committee from 1957 to 1978 — 22 years, the longest tenure of any
Judiciary chair — and used it as a fortress against integration. Majority
Leader Mike Mansfield routed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 straight to
the floor to spare it "possible oblivion" in the committee, and the
Voting Rights Act required legislation forcing the committee to report
it out by a 67–13 vote
([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/)).
Under Strom Thurmond (1981–1987), the committee staged the first
televised Supreme Court hearing — Sandra Day O'Connor, reported out
17–0–1 and confirmed 99–0 — and confirmed Antonin Scalia unanimously,
98–0
([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/)).
Under Joseph Biden (1987–1995), the committee held the first public
examination of a nominee's ideology rather than credentials — Robert
Bork — and began the 1990 hearings that produced the Violence Against
Women Act, passed in 1994 as part of a crime bill
([U.S. Senate Committee on the Judiciary](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/)).

## The Clinton-linked referral

On January 5, 2018, Chair Chuck Grassley and Sen. Lindsey Graham made
the first known congressional criminal referral of the Russia
investigations, targeting former British intelligence officer
Christopher Steele, author of the Trump dossier. The referral, sent to
Deputy Attorney General Rod Rosenstein and FBI Director Christopher
Wray, cited potential violations of 18 U.S.C. § 1001 — making false
statements to investigators — regarding Steele's claims about his
distribution of the dossier's contents
([Haaretz](https://www.haaretz.com/us-news/2018-01-05/ty-article/.premium/u-s-senators-grassley-graham-refer-dossier-author-steele-for-criminal-investigation/0000017f-df42-dfb9-a77c-ffdeb57f0004)).
The referral letter's own text identified the document as the "Trump
dossier" that Steele had "compiled on behalf of Fusion GPS for the
Clinton Campaign and the Democratic National Committee and also
provided to the FBI"
([People's Pundit Daily](https://www.peoplespunditdaily.com/news/politics/2018/01/05/chairmen-grassley-graham-refer-dossier-author-christopher-steele-criminal-investigation/)) —
the committee putting [Hillary Clinton](https://factsledger.com/people/hillary-clinton/)'s
campaign in its own investigative record.

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim the 2023 subpoenas of Crow and Leo were ever
issued or enforced — the vote authorized them; the follow-through was
not verified. It does not assert the committee's current chair or
ranking member, or anything about the committee's post-1995 history from
the Senate's own history PDF, which was read only through the Biden
era. It does not judge whether the Steele referral's allegations were
sustained by the Justice Department — only that the committee made the
referral and what its letter said.
