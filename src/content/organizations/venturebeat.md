## Overview

VentureBeat is the American technology news site that Matt Marshall founded in 2006 after walking away from a newspaper job to cover Silicon Valley's venture capital scene on his own terms ([Flipboard](https://about.flipboard.com/inside-flipboard/and-the-venturebeat-goes-on-flipboard/)). Marshall, a veteran of the Wall Street Journal and the Washington Post who had been covering venture capital as a beat reporter — with a PhD, no less — quit his day job at the San Jose Mercury News in September 2006 and launched VentureBeat, which quickly differentiated itself from its peers by its quick and in-depth coverage of the VC scene ([Flipboard](https://about.flipboard.com/inside-flipboard/and-the-venturebeat-goes-on-flipboard/)). It grew, unusually for a venture-backed media startup, mostly on its own revenues — and in April 2025 it spun out its games publication GamesBeat to sharpen its focus on enterprise AI ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/gamesbeat-spins-off-from-venturebeat-to-become-niche-independent-publication-)).

## From SiliconBeat to an independent site

VentureBeat did not start from nothing. Marshall and his Mercury News colleague Michael Bazeley had launched SiliconBeat almost two years earlier, as an experiment that began by taking up an hour or so of Marshall's day and grew into several hours daily ([Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.com/venturebeat-replaces-retired-siliconbeat-902061688)). Bazeley, showing what Marshall called more sanity, pulled back from SiliconBeat and took a job managing the Mercury News website, while Marshall approached the paper and said he wanted to go out on his own ([Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.com/venturebeat-replaces-retired-siliconbeat-902061688)). To his delight, the Mercury News became his first customer: it syndicated the content he produced, with the right to run it in the paper and on its website ([Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.com/venturebeat-replaces-retired-siliconbeat-902061688)). Marshall's stated reason for covering technology from a geographical standpoint rather than an industry niche was that he cared about the community in a broader sense, and saw the Mercury News's mission as linked with his own ([Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.com/venturebeat-replaces-retired-siliconbeat-902061688)).

That independence shaped the business that followed. In VentureBeat's own account of the tech-media business, the site grew comfortably, if slowly, on its profitable event and advertising businesses from its founding in 2006 until 2014 — founder Matt Marshall had raised a tiny angel round early on, but subsequent growth was entirely based on revenues ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/media/tech-media-companies-find-that-rapid-growth-doesnt-come-easy)). Only in the summer of 2014 did it take a small seed round, in order to build a research and information business that its leadership believed would be more scalable than advertising or conferences, while continuing to invest in the older businesses because they were self-sustaining and formed a solid foundation ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/media/tech-media-companies-find-that-rapid-growth-doesnt-come-easy)). The argument of that piece — that publishers need something else beyond advertising and conferences, or must give up the dream of startup-like growth — reads, in retrospect, as VentureBeat's own operating philosophy: remain small and independent rather than chase scale at any cost ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/media/tech-media-companies-find-that-rapid-growth-doesnt-come-easy)).

## GamesBeat spins out, and AI enters the newsroom

Two later turns define the current VentureBeat. The first is structural: in April 2025 GamesBeat became an independent media brand after splitting from VentureBeat, overseen by CEO Gina Joseph, formerly chief strategy officer at VentureBeat, and editorial director Dean Takahashi, formerly lead writer at GamesBeat ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/gamesbeat-spins-off-from-venturebeat-to-become-niche-independent-publication-)). GamesBeat is owned by Joseph, Takahashi, and a cohort of undisclosed investors, and was confirmed as backed by strategic advisors including Peter Levin, co-founder and managing director at venture capital fund Griffin Gaming Partners ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/gamesbeat-spins-off-from-venturebeat-to-become-niche-independent-publication-)). Its live events — GamesBeat Summit, GamesBeat Next, GamesBeat Global Tour, and Games & Hollywood — remain part of its plans ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/gamesbeat-spins-off-from-venturebeat-to-become-niche-independent-publication-)). The separation let VentureBeat concentrate on what it identifies as the enterprise shift to AI, data infrastructure, and intelligent security, while GamesBeat pursued gaming as a standalone business.

The second turn is editorial, and it drew attention. Bloomberg's Rachel Metz reported that VentureBeat's editorial director, Michael Nuñez, explained that the tech blog was using Microsoft's OpenAI-infused Bing Chat to help with editing and writing articles, describing the chatbot as like having another person on the team because it could process and summarize information in seconds rather than the hours a human reporter might take ([Talking Biz News](https://talkingbiznews.com/media-news/how-venturebeat-is-using-artificial-intelligence/)). Nuñez said VentureBeat encouraged reporters to use the AI tools available and did not attribute an article with sentences and fragments from a chatbot as long as it was truthful and independently verified ([Talking Biz News](https://talkingbiznews.com/media-news/how-venturebeat-is-using-artificial-intelligence/)). That stance — use the tool, verify the output, don't fuss over fragment attribution — put VentureBeat early and openly on one side of the newsroom-AI debate, consistent with a publication that had always treated itself as a working journalist's tool rather than an institution.

VentureBeat's own history, then, is a study in a particular kind of survival: a beat reporter's blog that became a company, funded itself from events and advertising for eight years, took outside money only to build a research business, and then, nearly twenty years in, split off its games arm to bet the remaining company on enterprise AI coverage. Its coverage also forms part of this site's AdRoll story: VentureBeat covered [AdRoll](/organizations/adroll/)'s launch of SendRoll, its email-retargeting product, with Berke arguing that personalized email could be opened up the way AdRoll had opened up display retargeting ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/business/adroll-launches-sendroll-an-email-retargeting-product-with-huge-potential/)). The venture scene it was founded to cover never changed, even as the beats did.
