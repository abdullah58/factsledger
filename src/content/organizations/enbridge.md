## Overview

Enbridge is a Calgary-based Canadian energy infrastructure company whose pipeline system is the longest in North America — a network built on the Interprovincial Pipe Line that opened in 1950 as the first major trunk line carrying Alberta crude to refineries in Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario and the northern United States ([The Conversation](https://phys.org/news/2018-05-complicated-history-pipelines-canada.html)). It transports crude oil, natural gas and natural gas liquids across Canada and the US, moving about 20% of the natural gas consumed in the United States ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/enbridge-launches-open-season-proposed-texas-natgas-pipe-2026-09-15/)). In 2026 the company is in the middle of an expansion sprint — even as one of its oldest lines, Line 5, generates trespass rulings and one of Wisconsin's largest pipeline spills in half a century.

## The 2026 expansion sprint

In September 2026 Enbridge agreed to acquire Blackstone-owned Tallgrass Energy's crude oil business for $2.55 billion in cash, expanding its US liquids network with a 75% interest in the 1,050-mile Pony Express Pipeline — a 460,000-barrel-per-day system linking Rockies production to the Cushing, Oklahoma storage hub — plus a 51% stake in the Powder River Gateway system, 8.4 million barrels of storage across nine terminals, and the Stanchion Energy crude marketing business ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/enbridge-nears-2-billion-deal-for-blackstones-tallgrass-pipeline-bloomberg-news-reports/)). The deal deepens Enbridge's presence in the Bakken, Powder River and Denver-Julesburg basins and is expected to be accretive to distributable cash flow in its first full year ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/enbridge-nears-2-billion-deal-for-blackstones-tallgrass-pipeline-bloomberg-news-reports/)). The company cites a C$41 billion secured growth backlog to justify the spending ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/enbridge-nears-2-billion-deal-for-blackstones-tallgrass-pipeline-bloomberg-news-reports/)).

On natural gas, Enbridge launched an open season in September 2026 for the proposed West Texas Express pipeline — more than 150 miles of new pipe carrying up to 2 billion cubic feet per day from the Permian's Waha area toward El Paso, targeting in-service in late 2029, with data-centre demand cited as a driver ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/enbridge-launches-open-season-proposed-texas-natgas-pipe-2026-09-15/)). In New England, its Algonquin Gas Transmission unit is pursuing the RARE expansion (about 75 million cubic feet per day, targeting 2028) that Massachusetts' governor asked the Trump administration to expedite in October 2026 — a project pitched as serving 600,000 customers and cutting gas bills by $40 million a year ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/massachusetts-governor-asks-trump-administration-to-speed-natgas-pipeline-review-2026-10-02/)).

## Line 5: trespass rulings and the Saxon spill

The 71-year-old Line 5, running from Superior, Wisconsin to Sarnia, Ontario, is the company's legal headache. Federal courts have found Enbridge trespassing on Bad River Band of Lake Superior Chippewa tribal land, and the company is building a 41-mile reroute around the reservation — a project already contested in court over its wetland and waterway permits ([Heartland Post](https://heartlandpost.com/dnr-told-enbridge-to-halt-they-didnt-wisconsin-now-has-a-fight-over-who-is-in-charge/)). Then, on August 25, 2026, an unoccupied subcontractor flatbed rolled into an open excavation at a Line 5 valve site near Saxon, Wisconsin, struck the pipe and released an estimated 1.3 million gallons of natural gas liquids — mostly propane and butane — which officials called among the largest pipeline spills in Wisconsin in more than 50 years; no injuries were reported and the liquids vaporized ([Heartland Post](https://heartlandpost.com/dnr-told-enbridge-to-halt-they-didnt-wisconsin-now-has-a-fight-over-who-is-in-charge/)).

What followed became a jurisdiction fight: Enbridge built a roughly 1,500-foot temporary bypass to restart the interstate line, but on September 9 the Wisconsin DNR issued a notice of noncompliance, saying the bypass lacked required stormwater, wetland and waterway authorizations — DNR Secretary Karen Hyun wrote that the company had shown "disregard for the environment, human health, and the statutory authority of the department" ([Heartland Post](https://heartlandpost.com/dnr-told-enbridge-to-halt-they-didnt-wisconsin-now-has-a-fight-over-who-is-in-charge/)). Enbridge finished the bypass anyway, pressure-tested it, and returned Line 5 to service over the weekend of September 12–14, saying it had worked with the federal Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration and that monitoring showed no impacts to wildlife, surface water or nearby wells ([Heartland Post](https://heartlandpost.com/dnr-told-enbridge-to-halt-they-didnt-wisconsin-now-has-a-fight-over-who-is-in-charge/)). The permit file remains open — and the dispute now tests whether a state agency can halt interstate energy infrastructure with a letter, or whether federal safety regulators have the final word.
