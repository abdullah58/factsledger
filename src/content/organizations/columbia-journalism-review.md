## The press's press

The most distinctive institution in media criticism is the one that
watches the watchdogs. The Columbia Journalism Review, founded in 1961
under Columbia University's Graduate School of Journalism
([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2015/cjr-cuts-print-schedule-to-two-issues-per-year/)),
describes its mission as being "the intellectual leader in the rapidly
changing world of journalism" and "the most respected voice on press
criticism," shaping the ideas that make media leaders and journalists
smarter about their work
([Columbia Journalism Review](http://www.cjr.org/about_us/mission_statement.php)).

What the reference profiles don't lead with is how CJR adapted when the
industry's own accountability tools died. By 2019, the public editor —
the journalist whose job is to serve as the voice of the news consumer —
was nearly extinct: ESPN had ditched its public editor in 2018, the New
York Times in 2017, the Washington Post back in 2013. CJR's answer was
to hire four outside public editors of its own, assigned to the New York
Times, MSNBC, CNN, and the Washington Post
([NPR Illinois (NPR transcript)](https://www.nprillinois.org/generationlisten/2019-06-14/columbia-journalism-review-hires-its-own-public-editors-for-4-major-outlets)).
Editor-in-chief Kyle Pope argued that social media was "a very, very,
very inadequate" substitute: managements don't respond to Twitter
hubbubs, so "the paper or the network never has to actually defend
itself or say why they did what they did"
([NPR Illinois (NPR transcript)](https://www.nprillinois.org/generationlisten/2019-06-14/columbia-journalism-review-hires-its-own-public-editors-for-4-major-outlets)).
His diagnosis was that public trust in media was collapsing exactly as
outlets were huddling inward — "the wrong thing to do"
([NPR Illinois (NPR transcript)](https://www.nprillinois.org/generationlisten/2019-06-14/columbia-journalism-review-hires-its-own-public-editors-for-4-major-outlets)).

## "Breaking Right": the Journal under Murdoch

CJR's sharpest work in recent years is its special report "Breaking
Right," an investigation of the [Wall Street Journal](https://factsledger.com/organizations/wall-street-journal/)
under Rupert Murdoch built on interviews with roughly fifty current and
former staffers
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/special_report/breaking_right_wall_street_journal_stubborn_murdoch.php)).
The spark was the December 2020 Joseph Epstein op-ed mocking Jill
Biden's doctorate — but the report's substance is the long war between
the newsroom and the opinion section. In July 2020, more than 280
newsroom employees signed a letter to publisher Almar Latour complaining
about a "lack of fact-checking and transparency" on the editorial page
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/special_report/breaking_right_wall_street_journal_stubborn_murdoch.php)).
Reporters told CJR that sources wouldn't talk to them because "they
don't trust that the WSJ is independent of the editorial page"
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/special_report/breaking_right_wall_street_journal_stubborn_murdoch.php)).

The report traces the conflict to Murdoch's 2007 acquisition of Dow
Jones for five billion dollars — and to his promise, in an email to
then-editor Paul Steiger, that "what is on the Opinion pages will never
be allowed to flow into the news pages"
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/special_report/breaking_right_wall_street_journal_stubborn_murdoch.php)).
Under editor Gerard Baker, reporters described editors sanding down
copy, killing or delaying reporting unflattering to Trump, and a "quest
to find negative stories on the Hillary side" in 2016; after Baker's
2018 departure, the same newsroom produced the Pulitzer-winning series
on Trump's secret payoffs to women that triggered criminal inquiries
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/special_report/breaking_right_wall_street_journal_stubborn_murdoch.php)).
CJR's verdict: the Journal had "demonstrated its willingness to reflect
a version of reality that its core right-leaning audience wishes to
see"
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/special_report/breaking_right_wall_street_journal_stubborn_murdoch.php)).

## [Axios](/organizations/axios/)'s "devil's bargain" with OpenAI

CJR turned the same critical lens on [Axios](https://factsledger.com/organizations/axios/),
a much younger outlet, in its investigation of the three-year deal under
which OpenAI would bankroll the startup of thirteen Axios Local
newsletters and give Axios staff free access to its enterprise AI tools —
while Axios, in turn, let OpenAI train its models on Axios's published
coverage
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/feature/axios-wants-to-save-local-news-with-ai-jim-vandehei-tech-newsletters.php)).
Critics saw it as "a devil's bargain, enabling an 852-billion-dollar
Silicon Valley giant to choose local journalism's winners and losers
while burnishing its own ailing reputation"
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/feature/axios-wants-to-save-local-news-with-ai-jim-vandehei-tech-newsletters.php)).
CEO Jim VandeHei called it financial pragmatism — "at the end of the
day, if someone's going to pay you for content, you say: Well, how much
are you going to pay me?" — and declared that "AI will save local
news"
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/feature/axios-wants-to-save-local-news-with-ai-jim-vandehei-tech-newsletters.php)).

What CJR documented, more than the business case, is the opacity: the
exact amount OpenAI pays is undisclosed, and the deal is "silent" on the
editorial firewall — VandeHei said both sides simply assumed Axios would
"write what we write"
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/feature/axios-wants-to-save-local-news-with-ai-jim-vandehei-tech-newsletters.php)).
The investigation also notes the human cost of the model it examines:
one reporter covers two counties — an area larger than Rhode Island —
with every local byline his own
([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/feature/axios-wants-to-save-local-news-with-ai-jim-vandehei-tech-newsletters.php)).

## A review that shrank its print and kept its bite

In 2015, CJR cut its print schedule from six issues a year to two,
arguing the shift would "better align our resources with our mission"
([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2015/cjr-cuts-print-schedule-to-two-issues-per-year/)).
Then-editor Liz Spayd wrote that print readership had been steadily
declining while online visits were up 35 percent, and that "continuing
to spend so many resources on print is, regrettably, limiting our
ability to invest fully in digital"
([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2015/cjr-cuts-print-schedule-to-two-issues-per-year/)).
CJR survived the same contraction that killed its counterpart, the
American Journalism Review, which folded that year after first cutting
its print editions
([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2015/cjr-cuts-print-schedule-to-two-issues-per-year/)).
