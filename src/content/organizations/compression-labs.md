## Overview

Compression Labs, Inc. (CLI) was a San Jose, California videoconferencing pioneer, founded in December 1976, that built its business on squeezing video and audio into far less bandwidth than uncompressed signals required ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)). Its best-known legacy is not a product but a patent: U.S. Patent 4,698,672, filed in October 1986 by Wen-Hsiung Chen and Daniel J. Klenke, which the company's acquirer later claimed covered the JPEG image standard and used to extract more than $105 million in licences before the U.S. Patent Office rejected its broadest claims in 2006 ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4698672A/en); [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/uspto-to-re-examine-jpeg-patent)). CLI itself vanished in 1997, merged into VTEL Corporation, whose successor Forgent Networks waged the JPEG campaign ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1275/0000899078-97-000178.pdf)).

The company described itself as a leader in visual communication systems based on Compressed Digital Video (CDV) technology, using proprietary and industry-standard algorithms to cut the data needed to transmit digital video and audio, and so cut transmission costs over terrestrial, microwave, cable and satellite networks ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)). It traded on the Nasdaq National Market as CLIX from headquarters at 2860 Junction Avenue, San Jose ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)).

## A videoconferencing pioneer

CLI's breakthrough came early. In 1982 it introduced a codec that provided the first economical means to communicate effectively over standard networks at the T1 rate of 1.544 Mbps — roughly a 60:1 reduction from the 90 Mbps needed to transmit uncompressed video, which made videoconferencing affordable over ordinary lines for the first time ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)). By the mid-1990s its product lines were the Rembrandt II/VP and Radiance group systems, operating at 56 kbps to 2.048 Mbps, the eclipse family of midrange systems and the CLI Desktop Video family, all compliant with the ITU H.320 videoconferencing standard ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)).

The company also pushed into broadcast and desktop video. In November 1991 it introduced SpectrumSaver, described as the industry's first compressed digital product for the satellite broadcast video market, which could carry as many as 15 channels on a single satellite transponder ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)). In February 1992 it launched the Cameo Personal Video System Model 2001, a $2,100 box for high-end [Apple](/organizations/apple/) Macintosh computers that used CLI's proprietary PV2 algorithm instead of the international Px64 standard, running at up to 15 frames per second at a resolution of 128 by 112 pixels and making heavy use of Apple's QuickTime technology ([Tech Monitor](https://www.techmonitor.ai/technology/compression_labs_launches_non_standard_videophone_for_use_on_isdn_networks)). AT&T and CLI also worked together on chip sets for videophones and desktop video conferencing over analogue lines, built to a Global VideoPhone Standard the two companies submitted to the CCITT and ANSI ([Tech Monitor](https://www.techmonitor.ai/technology/compression_labs_launches_non_standard_videophone_for_use_on_isdn_networks)).

In March 1994 CLI announced Magnitude, an MPEG-2-based compressed digital video family for entertainment and information services over telephone, cable and satellite networks, cutting the bandwidth video needed by six to sixty times depending on content and transmission method ([Tech Monitor](https://www.techmonitor.ai/technology/magnitude_compressed_digital_video_product_family_from_diversifying_compression_labs)). It supplied Magnitude encoders to a unit of GM Hughes Electronics through an agreement with Thomson Consumer Electronics for the DIRECTV satellite service, and sold the products in Argentina, Australia, China, India, Japan, Namibia, Russia and Taiwan ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)).

By 1995 CLI was a substantial company: 535 employees, products distributed in more than 50 countries, co-marketing agreements with AT&T and MCI, and customers including NASA, the U.S. Department of Defense, Bank of America, Citibank, IBM and Hewlett-Packard ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)). But it was losing money. In November 1995 a strategic review led it to discontinue its Broadcast products division — including Magnitude — and refocus on group and desktop videoconferencing ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)).

## The merger into VTEL

On 23 May 1997, shareholders of VTEL Corporation and Compression Labs approved a merger in which CLI became a direct wholly-owned subsidiary of VTEL, with each CLI share converted into 0.46 VTEL shares — about 8.4 million VTEL shares in total ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1275/0000899078-97-000178.pdf)). Two former CLI directors, T. Gary Trimm and Dr. Arthur G. Anderson, joined the VTEL board, expanding it to seven members ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1275/0000899078-97-000178.pdf)). The combined company kept the VTEL name and its Austin, [Texas](/places/texas/) headquarters, employed more than 800 people, expected annualised revenue of nearly $200 million, and claimed an installed base of about 222,000 units, making it the number-two global supplier of videoconferencing systems ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1275/0000899078-97-000178.pdf)). VTEL later became [Forgent Networks](/organizations/asure-software/) — the company that would turn CLI's old patent into a licensing business ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/ir-archive/newsletter-archive/imaging-resource-newsletter-26-july-2002-are-forgent-jpeg-patent-claims-groundless-fuji-finepix-s2-6-megapixel-equalizer/)).

## The '672 patent and the JPEG fight

The patent at the centre of the storm was U.S. Patent 4,698,672, "Coding system for reducing redundancy", invented by Wen-Hsiung Chen and Daniel J. Klenke, filed on 27 October 1986 and granted on 6 October 1987, originally assigned to Compression Labs ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4698672A/en)). Forgent claimed it applied to the run-length encoding used in JPEG compression — technology used to read and write images by almost all digital cameras, image software and web browsers ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/ir-archive/newsletter-archive/imaging-resource-newsletter-26-july-2002-are-forgent-jpeg-patent-claims-groundless-fuji-finepix-s2-6-megapixel-equalizer/)).

On 11 July 2002 Forgent announced it held "the sole and exclusive right to use and license all the claims under the 672 patent that implement JPEG in all 'fields of use' except in the satellite broadcast business" ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/ir-archive/newsletter-archive/imaging-resource-newsletter-26-july-2002-are-forgent-jpeg-patent-claims-groundless-fuji-finepix-s2-6-megapixel-equalizer/)). It had already signed a multi-million-dollar licence with [Sony](/organizations/sony/) in June 2002, and an earlier licence in April 2002 had generated $15.0 million in a single quarter ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/ir-archive/newsletter-archive/imaging-resource-newsletter-26-july-2002-are-forgent-jpeg-patent-claims-groundless-fuji-finepix-s2-6-megapixel-equalizer/)). The Joint Photographic Experts Group committee responded that it believed prior art existed and threatened to withdraw JPEG as an [ISO](/organizations/iso/) standard, since ISO rules require standards to be implementable free or on reasonable and non-discriminatory terms ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/ir-archive/newsletter-archive/imaging-resource-newsletter-26-july-2002-are-forgent-jpeg-patent-claims-groundless-fuji-finepix-s2-6-megapixel-equalizer/)). The episode drew comparisons with Unisys, which had spent the 1990s enforcing its LZW patent against GIF users ([Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/ir-archive/newsletter-archive/imaging-resource-newsletter-26-july-2002-are-forgent-jpeg-patent-claims-groundless-fuji-finepix-s2-6-megapixel-equalizer/)).

Challenges followed quickly. In August 2002 the Berlin compression firm Algo Vision Luratech alleged the claims were invalid on prior-art grounds, backed by a memorandum from professors Ruedi Seiler of the Technical University of Berlin and Klaus Jung, director of the German delegation to the JPEG commission ([EE Times](https://www.eetimes.com/german-firm-alleges-forgent-jpeg-claims-invalid/)). By February 2006 Forgent said it had licensed the patent to more than 50 companies worldwide, had litigation pending against around 30 more, and had generated more than $105 million in revenues, mostly from the '672 patent ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/uspto-to-re-examine-jpeg-patent)). But in November 2005 the [Public Patent Foundation](/organizations/public-patent-foundation/) had asked the [U.S. Patent and Trademark Office](/organizations/us-patent-and-trademark-office/) to re-examine the patent, and the office agreed, finding the foundation had raised "a substantial new question of patentability" for every claim ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/uspto-to-re-examine-jpeg-patent)). On 25–26 May 2006 the Patent Office rejected the broadest claims, finding the submitted prior art "completely anticipated" them ([Photography Blog](https://www.photographyblog.com/news/forgent_networks_jpeg_patent_claim_rejected)). The campaign's end came through that re-examination.

## Frequently asked questions

**What was Compression Labs?**
Compression Labs, Inc. (CLI) was a San Jose, California company founded in December 1976 that developed, manufactured and marketed videoconferencing and compressed digital video systems, including the Rembrandt, Radiance and eclipse product lines ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)).

**What is the connection between Compression Labs and JPEG?**
CLI owned U.S. Patent 4,698,672, filed in 1986 by Wen-Hsiung Chen and Daniel J. Klenke, which its acquirer's successor Forgent claimed covered the run-length encoding used in JPEG compression and licensed to more than 50 companies ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4698672A/en); [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/uspto-to-re-examine-jpeg-patent)).

**What happened to Compression Labs?**
In May 1997 CLI merged into VTEL Corporation, becoming its wholly-owned subsidiary in a stock-for-stock deal at 0.46 VTEL shares per CLI share; VTEL later became Forgent Networks, which enforced the '672 patent ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1275/0000899078-97-000178.pdf); [Imaging Resource](https://www.imaging-resource.com/ir-archive/newsletter-archive/imaging-resource-newsletter-26-july-2002-are-forgent-jpeg-patent-claims-groundless-fuji-finepix-s2-6-megapixel-equalizer/)).

**Was Forgent's JPEG patent claim upheld?**
No. After the Public Patent Foundation challenged the patent on prior-art grounds, the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office agreed to re-examine it and in May 2006 rejected its broadest claims, finding the submitted prior art completely anticipated them ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/uspto-to-re-examine-jpeg-patent); [Photography Blog](https://www.photographyblog.com/news/forgent_networks_jpeg_patent_claim_rejected)).

**How big was Compression Labs?**
In 1995 it reported $113.0 million in revenue, employed 535 people, and sold products in more than 50 countries, with international sales at 22% of revenue — but it lost $21.0 million from continuing operations that year ([U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/2397/0000891618-96-000217.pdf)).
