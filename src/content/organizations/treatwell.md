## Overview

Treatwell is Europe's largest hair and beauty booking marketplace, a [London](/places/london/)-based company founded in 2008 as Wahanda and renamed Treatwell in January 2016. It lists tens of thousands of salons and spas across 11 European countries and makes its money by charging commission only on the appointments it sends to salons, combined with subscription fees ([UMi Sat Nav](https://www.umisatnav.co.uk/inspiration/treatwell-founder-reveals-the-secret-to-his-success)). Since May 2015 it has been majority-owned by Japan's Recruit Holdings, which bought a further 70% of the company for £112.5 million, about $171 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/05/05/wahanda-exit/)). It is the commission-model rival against which [Booksy](/organizations/booksy/)'s subscription model is usually defined.

## Founding: a massage that could not be booked

Lopo Champalimaud founded the company, then called Wahanda, in 2008 in London after running the lifestyle division at lastminute.com ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/18/wahanda-treatwell/?ncid=rss)). The trigger, as he tells it, was an evening when he could not book a massage by phone or email, and realised the booking problem was the business ([UMi Sat Nav](https://www.umisatnav.co.uk/inspiration/treatwell-founder-reveals-the-secret-to-his-success)). The model he built charges salons commission only on appointments Treatwell sends directly to them, combined with subscription fees, while giving salons reservation-management and loyalty software plus a discovery route to new customers ([UMi Sat Nav](https://www.umisatnav.co.uk/inspiration/treatwell-founder-reveals-the-secret-to-his-success)).

The turning point came in 2012, when Wahanda acquired the Lithuanian company Salonium, which had built an appointment booking system; the traction from that software showed the demand was accelerating ([UMi Sat Nav](https://www.umisatnav.co.uk/inspiration/treatwell-founder-reveals-the-secret-to-his-success)). From there the company grew by buying its way across Europe.

## The 2014–2015 roll-up

Between late 2014 and mid-2015 Wahanda acquired five companies in five countries, in one of the fastest roll-ups in European beauty tech:

| Target | Country | Date | Price / note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Salonmeister | Germany | October 2014 | Undisclosed |
| Beautycheck | Austria | 2014–2015 | Undisclosed |
| LemonLabs | Lithuania | January 2015 | Acqui-hire (mobile agency) |
| ZenSoon | France | June 2015 | Undisclosed |
| Treatwell (Dutch) | Netherlands | June 2015 | €34m (~$38m) |

The Dutch Treatwell deal was the largest. The Dutch company had grown more than 425% year-on-year since its 2013 launch, with over 2,500 salons across the Netherlands, Belgium and Germany, and was backed by Project A Ventures and [Piton Capital](/organizations/piton-capital/) ([SpaBusiness](https://www.spabusiness.com/index.cfm?pagetype=news&codeID=316283)). The combined company counted more than 15,000 venues and 20 million users ([SpaBusiness](https://www.spabusiness.com/index.cfm?pagetype=news&codeID=316283)).

The roll-up was bankrolled by Recruit Holdings, the Japanese group behind the Hot Pepper Beauty marketplace. Recruit first invested in Wahanda in July 2014, then in May 2015 bought a further 70% for £112.5 million, about $171 million, valuing Wahanda at roughly $222 million and buying out Fidelity Growth Partners, Ambient Sound, 14W, Lepe Partners, Brent Hoberman and Stefan Glaenzer; Recruit also invested a further £30 million, about $46 million, for expansion, while the founder and management retained about 20% ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/05/05/wahanda-exit/)). SpaBusiness records a further €42 million Recruit investment in May 2015 alongside the Dutch acquisition ([SpaBusiness](https://www.spabusiness.com/index.cfm?pagetype=news&codeID=316283)). The Dutch company Treatwell had itself been founded only in 2013, and had grown more than 425% year-on-year since launch ([SpaBusiness](https://www.spabusiness.com/index.cfm?pagetype=news&codeID=316283)). By the January 2016 rebrand, the company's valuation was "substantially" higher than at Recruit's majority stake ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/18/wahanda-treatwell/?ncid=rss)).

## The rebrand and the Booksy contrast

In January 2016 Wahanda renamed itself Treatwell, taking the name of its June 2015 Dutch acquisition in order to unify five brands under one name ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/18/wahanda-treatwell/?ncid=rss)). The rebrand was led by DesignStudio, the agency behind Airbnb's redesign, and the company said it was eyeing entry into the United States, possibly by acquisition, naming [StyleSeat](/organizations/styleseat/) and Groupon as the US players to watch ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/18/wahanda-treatwell/?ncid=rss)). At the rebrand the company claimed to be the biggest of its kind in Europe, with 500 employees, 20,000 bookable venues, 10 million users and $108 million in sales projected for the year ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/18/wahanda-treatwell/?ncid=rss)). Later, Champalimaud described a company of more than 500 people across Europe, with over 30,000 salons and spas listed in 11 countries, growing about 100% year-on-year, with more than half of salons using its Connect app and over 60% of bookings made via mobile ([UMi Sat Nav](https://www.umisatnav.co.uk/inspiration/treatwell-founder-reveals-the-secret-to-his-success)).

Treatwell matters to the Booksy story because the two companies embody the two business models in beauty booking. When Booksy raised its $4.2 million Series A in 2017, [OpenOcean](/organizations/openocean/)'s Patrik Backman contrasted Treatwell's per-booking fee with Booksy's transparent monthly subscription ([tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2017/03/03/booksy-series-a/)). Treatwell takes a cut of each appointment it generates; Booksy charges salons a flat monthly fee and keeps the booking revenue in the salon's hands. The contrast is the reason Treatwell is the rival most often named in Booksy's funding coverage.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why did Wahanda change its name to Treatwell?**
To unify five brands under one name after the 2014–2015 roll-up; it took the name of the Dutch company it had acquired in June 2015 for €34 million ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/18/wahanda-treatwell/?ncid=rss)).

**Who owns Treatwell?**
Recruit Holdings of Japan, which bought a further 70% in May 2015 for £112.5 million (~$171m); the founder and management retained about 20% ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2015/05/05/wahanda-exit/)).

**How does Treatwell make money?**
It charges salons commission only on appointments it sends directly to them, combined with subscription fees, and gives salons booking and loyalty software in return ([UMi Sat Nav](https://www.umisatnav.co.uk/inspiration/treatwell-founder-reveals-the-secret-to-his-success)).

**How is Treatwell different from Booksy?**
Treatwell charges a per-booking fee; Booksy charges salons a flat monthly subscription. The contrast was drawn by Booksy's own Series A lead investor, OpenOcean ([tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2017/03/03/booksy-series-a/)).

**Is Treatwell in the United States?**
Not as of the January 2016 rebrand, when the company said it was eyeing US entry, possibly by acquisition ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/01/18/wahanda-treatwell/?ncid=rss)). No later US entry was verified from a fully-read source.
