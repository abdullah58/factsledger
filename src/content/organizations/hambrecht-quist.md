## Overview

Hambrecht & Quist (H&Q) was a San Francisco investment bank founded in 1968 by Bill Hambrecht and George Quist that specialised in taking emerging technology companies public — underwriting or investing in the flotations of [Apple](/organizations/apple/), Genentech, [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/), Netscape and Amazon — before Chase Manhattan Bank bought it for $1.35 billion in 1999 ([UC Berkeley Haas](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/tech-finance-pioneer-bill-hambrecht-speak-jan-31/); [SEC filing](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1215/0000019617-99-000165.pdf)). Its most consequential early bet was also its smallest in absolute terms: the $2.5 million that launched Adobe Systems in 1982 ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

H&Q belonged to a distinct San Francisco species: the boutique investment bank that understood technology companies before Wall Street's giants did. UC Berkeley's Haas School describes the firm as "an investment banking firm focused on emerging high-technology companies" that "played a pivotal role in the technology revolution" ([UC Berkeley Haas](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/tech-finance-pioneer-bill-hambrecht-speak-jan-31/)). Euromoney later grouped it with Robertson Stephens, Montgomery Securities and Alex Brown as the "Four Horsemen, the boutique investment banks specializing in tech and growth companies" ([Euromoney](https://www.euromoney.com/article/27bjsstsqxhkmh1cj8ikf/the-fall-and-rise-of-the-bay-area/)).

## The $2.5 million that founded Adobe

The Adobe story, as co-founder [Charles Geschke](/people/charles-geschke/) told it, began with frustration: he and [John Warnock](/people/john-warnock/) had developed electronic document technology at Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center, but Xerox would not move fast enough to commercialise it ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Warnock's thesis adviser sat on H&Q's board, and Geschke describes Bill Hambrecht as "one of the first guys pushing venture investing in high-tech companies" ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

The pair pitched a plan to build complete turnkey publishing systems — computers, printers and typesetting equipment sold to the Fortune 500. Hambrecht liked the idea, Geschke recalled, partly because he "hated financial printers," and "agreed to invest $2.5 million over two years in two equal payments," on condition the founders quit their Xerox jobs ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Customers then talked the founders out of the turnkey plan and into selling just the software — PostScript — which became Adobe's first product and the engine of desktop publishing. The seed cheque was therefore not just early money; it bought the freedom for two researchers to ignore their own business plan and follow what buyers actually wanted.

## A "new economy" bank at full stretch

By 1999 H&Q had grown far beyond a two-founder boutique. Chase's acquisition announcement describes it as "one of the most experienced providers of investment banking services to the 'new economy,' with a focus on growth industries, including technology, life sciences, and information services," offering public equity, private equity and venture capital, private placements, fixed income and merger advisory ([SEC filing](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1215/0000019617-99-000165.pdf)). It employed roughly 1,000 people across offices in San Francisco, New York, Boston, Atlanta, London, Tel Aviv and Tokyo, and its chairman and chief executive, Daniel H. Case III, said revenue and income had risen fivefold in the previous five years ([SEC filing](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1215/0000019617-99-000165.pdf)).

That scale is also what ended its independence. Consolidation was sweeping up all four Horsemen: Robertson Stephens and Montgomery Securities had already been sold in 1997, and Alex Brown had been absorbed into Deutsche Bank by the end of 1998 ([Euromoney](https://www.euromoney.com/article/27bjsstsqxhkmh1cj8ikf/the-fall-and-rise-of-the-bay-area/)). On 28 September 1999 Chase Manhattan and H&Q announced an agreement for Chase to acquire the firm for $50 per share in cash, $1.35 billion in total, with both boards' approval and completion expected by the end of the year ([SEC filing](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1215/0000019617-99-000165.pdf)). The deal kept H&Q headquartered in San Francisco and set up a $200 million retention pool in Chase stock for key employees over four years — an unusually explicit attempt to buy loyalty along with the franchise ([SEC filing](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1215/0000019617-99-000165.pdf)).

Chase's chief executive, William B. Harrison Jr., framed the purchase as extending Chase's investment banking range into "the highest growth sector of the US economy, where media, telecommunications, information technology and the Internet converge," praising H&Q's "award-winning equity research" and "high-quality equity platform" ([SEC filing](http://pdf.secdatabase.com/1215/0000019617-99-000165.pdf)). Within a year Chase itself merged with J.P. Morgan, and the independent H&Q name faded into the new banking giant ([Company Histories](https://www.company-histories.com/JP-Morgan-Chase-Co-Company-History.html)). Hambrecht, meanwhile, had already left to found WR Hambrecht + Co., whose OpenIPO Dutch-auction process — designed to let anyone, not just insiders, buy into an IPO — achieved prominence when Google used it for its 2004 flotation ([UC Berkeley Haas](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/tech-finance-pioneer-bill-hambrecht-speak-jan-31/)). The firm that taught Wall Street to take tech companies public ended by teaching it a different way to run the offering itself.

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim H&Q underwrote every named company's IPO in the lead role: Haas credits the firm with "underwriting IPOs for or investing in" Apple, Genentech, Adobe, Netscape and Amazon, and this profile preserves that broader wording rather than upgrading it to lead-underwriter status for each. It does not assert a founding month or day in 1968, the later "Chase H&Q" brand sequence, or any post-1999 fate for the H&Q name beyond its absorption into the Chase/J.P. Morgan group — none of those points was verified from a source read in full. The $2.5 million Adobe figure rests on Geschke's own recollection in the Wharton interview, a participant's account rather than contemporaneous investment documentation, and the interview's phrasing attributes the decision to Hambrecht personally (see open items).
