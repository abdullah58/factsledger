## The newsroom that rewrote laws

Most profiles of the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists read like an appendix to the Panama Papers. That gets the emphasis backwards. The consortium's own accounting of its work reads like a foreign-policy record: official inquiries, sweeping legal reform, and public protests in more than 70 countries; the resignations of the leaders of Pakistan, Iceland, and Malta after corruption allegations; the US Corporate Transparency Act, hailed as the biggest anti-corruption measure since the 2001 Patriot Act; and credit for helping bring more than 130 countries together to sign a worldwide minimum corporate tax rate. In 2021 ICIJ was nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize for reporting that made it harder for arms dealers and people smugglers to launder their profits. It has won the Pulitzer Prize.

The model that produces those outcomes is a network rather than a newsroom. Membership is by invitation, and the network has grown to more than 290 investigative reporters from more than 100 countries and territories. The consortium is donor-supported: it relies entirely on donations, and neither pays its media partners nor is paid by them — more than 140 newspapers, television and radio stations, and online outlets have collaborated, from the Washington Post, Le Monde, the BBC, and El País down to small regional nonprofit journalism organisations in Africa, Asia, and Latin America. The headquarters is in Washington, D.C., with core team members spread across the United States, Egypt, Australia, France, Spain, Belgium, Hungary, Serbia, the United Kingdom, and Ireland, plus regional coordinators covering Asia, Africa, Latin America, the Middle East, Europe, and Eastern Europe.

ICIJ describes its method as convincing reporters across the globe to set aside traditional rivalries — and it backs that with technology. It builds and maintains Datashare, an open-source toolkit for picking apart massive datasets with machine learning, which it makes free for other journalists, and it trains hundreds of reporters each year, many from poorer countries. During the Panama Papers it ran a secure "global iHub" forum for the reporting team — adapted, remarkably, from the open-source Oxwall dating platform — alongside a graph database and Apache Solr search.

## From a 1997 project to the biggest investigations ever

ICIJ was founded in 1997 as a project of the Center for Public Integrity, itself founded in 1989 by journalism professor Charles Lewis, with the specific mission of cross-border investigations. The offshore investigations that followed — Offshore Leaks, the Panama Papers, the Paradise Papers, the Pandora Papers, and the FinCEN Files — were, in ICIJ's telling, the biggest cross-border journalism projects ever, with separate teams of hundreds of reporters spread across 80 countries, working in more than 30 languages and combing through more than 25 million documents.

The Panama Papers operation shows the scale: 11.5 million files, 2.6 terabytes of data from the Panamanian law firm Mossack Fonseca, first obtained by Süddeutsche Zeitung reporters Bastian Obermayer and Frederik Obermaier, then shared with more than 370 journalists from over 100 media outlets in 80 countries before breaking in April 2016. Beyond the offshore series, ICIJ brought together more than 75 journalists from 14 countries to document the surveillance and mass internment of Uyghurs and other Muslim minorities in Xinjiang; shared more than 750,000 secret documents with 120 journalists from 20 countries to expose Isabel dos Santos's wealth, leading to a worldwide freeze of her assets; and assembled over 250 journalists from 36 countries to investigate defective medical implants, contributing to the worldwide withdrawal of textured breast implants linked to a rare cancer.

The network's membership includes reporters across the Global South — among them [Golden Matonga](/people/golden-matonga/), the Malawian investigative journalist who joined ICIJ in 2021 and became the only African member of its global Network Committee.
