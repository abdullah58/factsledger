## Overview

The City of Tallinn is the municipal government of [Tallinn](/places/tallinn/), the capital of [Estonia](/places/estonia/): a 79-member City Council that elects a Mayor and a City Government, headed by Mayor Peeter Raudsepp, who took office on 8 December 2025 ([City of Tallinn](https://www.tallinn.ee/en/news/mayor-tallinn-peeter-raudsepp)). The government serves 456,518 residents across eight districts, and it is the administration behind two policies that made the city internationally known — free public transport for residents and the European Green Capital title ([City of Tallinn](https://files.tallinn.ee/live/s3fs-public/inline-files/Tallinn_2025_annual_publication_web_0.pdf); [AFP](https://phys.org/news/2013-01-free-ticket-estonia-capital.html)).

Tallinn's government has two bodies. The Tallinn City Council (Tallinna linnavolikogu) is the representative body of the municipality; the Tallinn City Government is the executive body it elects, and work in the City Government is performed in sittings whose records are published in the Legal Acts Register TEELE ([City of Tallinn](https://www.tallinn.ee/en/tallinn-city-government)). At its session on 8 December 2025 the council elected Peeter Raudsepp as Mayor with 46 votes in favour, in a council of 79 members, with 74 councillors taking part in the vote ([City of Tallinn](https://www.tallinn.ee/en/news/mayor-tallinn-peeter-raudsepp)). Raudsepp, a member of the Isamaa party, had headed the Estonian Institute of Economic Research since 2023 and previously led the Estonian Business and Innovation Agency and companies including Rautakesko, Eesti Post and Werol Tehased; he graduated from the University of Tartu with a degree in economics and credit ([City of Tallinn](https://www.tallinn.ee/en/news/mayor-tallinn-peeter-raudsepp)).

## How Tallinn is governed

The current government rests on a power-sharing deal between the two coalition partners. Raudsepp will serve as mayor for two years, after which the mayorship reverts to the coalition partner, the left-of-centre Estonian Centre Party (Keskerakond), under the coalition agreement running to 2029 ([Via Baltica/BNS](https://viabaltica.fi/estonia-tallinn-city-council-elects-new-mayor/)). The Centre Party holds four deputy-mayor posts and nominates five district elders, while Isamaa holds two deputy-mayor posts and three district elders ([Via Baltica/BNS](https://viabaltica.fi/estonia-tallinn-city-council-elects-new-mayor/)). The council itself is split Centre 37, Isamaa 11, Social Democrats 17, Reform Party 8, and Parempoolsed 6 — a governing majority of 48 against an opposition of 31 ([ERR](https://www.europesays.com/dk/44159/)). Centre Party leader Mihhail Kõlvart was elected council chairman, with Isamaa's Sven Sester as first deputy chair, and the council decided the chair would hold a salaried position, at €9,616 a month ([ERR](https://www.europesays.com/dk/44159/)).

The deputy mayors bring heavyweight CVs. Monika Haukanõmm has worked as a city district elder of Tallinn City Centre and as a deputy chairman of the City Council, and has led the Estonian Paralympic Committee and the Estonian Chamber of Disabled People; Kristjan Järvan is a former Minister of Entrepreneurship and Information Technology; Andrei Kante has worked as a teacher, school principal and deputy mayor, and has led the transition to education in Estonian; Riina Solman is a former Minister of Public Administration and Minister of Population; Tiit Terik is a former Minister of Culture and a former chairman of the City Council ([City of Tallinn](https://www.tallinn.ee/en/tallinn-city-government)). Joel Jesse, the transport deputy, spent more than 20 years in transport and mobility, most recently at the Estonian Transport Administration ([City of Tallinn](https://www.tallinn.ee/en/tallinn-city-government)). The coalition's written programme is the Coalition Agreement 2025–2029 and the City Government Action Programme, both published on the city's site ([City of Tallinn](https://www.tallinn.ee/en/tallinn-city-government)).

## The city it governs

Tallinn is Estonia's capital, and its government administers eight districts — Haabersti, Kesklinn (City Centre), Kristiine, Lasnamäe, Mustamäe, Nõmme, Pirita and Põhja-Tallinn — each with a district elder nominated by the governing parties under the coalition agreement. According to Statistics Estonia, Tallinn was home to 456,518 people at the beginning of 2025, 1,054 fewer than a year earlier ([City of Tallinn](https://files.tallinn.ee/live/s3fs-public/inline-files/Tallinn_2025_annual_publication_web_0.pdf)).

The population is ageing and shrinking at the margins: 3,262 children were born in Tallinn in 2024, 9 percent fewer than in 2023, and the natural growth rate has been negative since 2020, though 82,545 children — 18.5 percent of the population — live in the city ([City of Tallinn](https://files.tallinn.ee/live/s3fs-public/inline-files/Tallinn_2025_annual_publication_web_0.pdf)). ## Two policies that made Tallinn famous

The first is free public transport. Since 1 January 2013, all public transport has been free of charge for people registered as living in Tallinn, making it the first EU capital to abolish fares for its residents ([AFP](https://phys.org/news/2013-01-free-ticket-estonia-capital.html)). The policy followed a 2012 referendum in which 75 percent of voters supported the measure ([EU UPPER Project](https://www.publictransportknowledge.eu/resource/fare-free-public-transport-tallinn-estonia/)). The stat that captures its scale is the take-up: about half of all Tallinners had already taken advantage of the free pass within weeks of launch, and overall public-transport usage rose by around 15 percent in the first year ([AFP](https://phys.org/news/2013-01-free-ticket-estonia-capital.html); [EU UPPER Project](https://www.publictransportknowledge.eu/resource/fare-free-public-transport-tallinn-estonia/)). The implication the city drew was environmental — fewer cars, less pollution — though critics at the time called it a pre-election gimmick by the then-governing Centre Party, and the scheme cost the city around €12.4 million a year, about a quarter of its annual public transport budget ([AFP](https://phys.org/news/2013-01-free-ticket-estonia-capital.html)). Non-residents still pay, and the fare-box revenue of roughly €2 million a year was replaced by municipal tax adjustments, higher parking fees and a modest rise in property tax ([EU UPPER Project](https://www.publictransportknowledge.eu/resource/fare-free-public-transport-tallinn-estonia/)).

The second is the European Green Capital title. The European Commission awarded Tallinn the European Green Capital 2023 title, with a €600,000 prize, after the city impressed the international jury with "their systemic approach to green governance and interlinked strategic goals" ([European Commission](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/congratulations-tallinn-valongo-and-winterswijk-new-winners-european-green-city-awards-old-2021-09-10_en)). Tallinn went on to lead a newly launched network of 19 European cities implementing the UN Sustainable Development Goals at local level ([European Commission](https://environment.ec.europa.eu/news/congratulations-tallinn-valongo-and-winterswijk-new-winners-european-green-city-awards-old-2021-09-10_en)). The city followed the green year by holding the European Capital of Sport title in 2025, and its long-term plan is the "Tallinn 2035" development strategy ([City of Tallinn](https://files.tallinn.ee/live/s3fs-public/inline-files/Tallinn_2025_annual_publication_web_0.pdf)).
