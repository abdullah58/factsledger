## Overview

GoDaddy is the domain registrar and web-hosting company Bob Parsons founded in 1997, which grew into the world's largest web hosting provider — serving more than 9.3 million customers and managing over 48 million domain names by the time it was sold ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/technology/kkr-silver-lake-to-buy-go-daddy-for-225-billion-sources-idUSTRE76066E/)). Based in Scottsdale, Arizona, it spent its early years as the loudest brand in an unglamorous business, famous for deliberately provocative Super Bowl commercials, before private-equity owners scrubbed its image and took it public ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-godaddy-ipo-exclusive-idUSKBN0MR2S220150401/)).

## The IPO that wasn't — then the buyout

GoDaddy first tried to go public in 2006, but the attempt collapsed: bankers told the company it would have to accept a 50% haircut on the offering — a discount subtracted from the par value of assets used as collateral — and Parsons pulled the filing, citing unfavorable market conditions ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/technology/kkr-silver-lake-to-buy-go-daddy-for-225-billion-sources-idUSTRE76066E/)). Instead of the public markets, GoDaddy turned to private equity. After fielding inbound interest from multiple suitors — hiring Frank Quattrone's Qatalyst Partners to run the process — Parsons agreed in July 2011 to sell to a consortium led by KKR and Silver Lake, with Technology Crossover Ventures also participating, for $2.25 billion including debt ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/technology/kkr-silver-lake-to-buy-go-daddy-for-225-billion-sources-idUSTRE76066E/)).

Parsons framed the sale as a path back to the public markets he had been denied: using the buyers' knowledge of public markets "would eventually aid in the company's IPO should it take that path," he told Reuters at the time ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/technology/kkr-silver-lake-to-buy-go-daddy-for-225-billion-sources-idUSTRE76066E/)). The deal reshuffled leadership — Parsons moved from CEO to executive chairman while president and COO Warren Adelman became CEO — and 2011 also became the year Parsons' public persona turned toxic, after he circulated a video of himself shooting an elephant in Zimbabwe ([DataCenterKnowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/business/the-ipo-road-godaddy-s-years-in-the-making-preparation-for-wall-street)).

## Scrubbing the racy image for Wall Street

Under private equity, GoDaddy spent four years making itself IPO-presentable. Blake Irving, a former Yahoo chief product officer, took over as CEO in January 2013; the company acquired web host Media Temple in October 2013 to buy credibility with developers; and in 2013 it formally began "re-orienting our brand position to focus more specifically on how we help individuals start, grow and run their own ventures" — corporate language for retiring the soft-porn Super Bowl ads that had built its fame ([DataCenterKnowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/business/the-ipo-road-godaddy-s-years-in-the-making-preparation-for-wall-street)). As Slate's Seth Stevenson wrote of the strategy, the early crass ads won brand recognition, and the company was now pivoting to "something more broadly palatable" ([DataCenterKnowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/business/the-ipo-road-godaddy-s-years-in-the-making-preparation-for-wall-street)).

The numbers underneath were real: the June 2014 S-1 showed roughly 12 million customers, revenue past $1 billion and growing, though the company had been losing money since 2010 ([DataCenterKnowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/business/the-ipo-road-godaddy-s-years-in-the-making-preparation-for-wall-street)). On April 1, 2015, GoDaddy priced its IPO at $20 per share — above the expected $17–$19 range — valuing the company at about $4.5 billion including debt, and began trading on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker GDDY ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-godaddy-ipo-exclusive-idUSKBN0MR2S220150401/)). Revenue had risen 52% in three years to about $1.4 billion, and the net loss had narrowed to $143.3 million in 2014 from $279 million in 2012 ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-godaddy-ipo-exclusive-idUSKBN0MR2S220150401/)).
