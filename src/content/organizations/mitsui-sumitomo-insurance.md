## Overview

Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance (MSI) is a Japanese non-life insurer that has spent its entire corporate life as a product of mergers — and is about to become one again. It was created in October 2001 when Sumitomo Marine & Fire Insurance and Mitsui Marine & Fire Insurance merged, forming what was then the country's No. 2 casualty-and-property insurer ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2000/03/28/business/sumitomo-mitsui-tieup-advanced-by-six-months/)). In 2010 it became the core of the MS&AD Insurance Group, Japan's largest non-life insurance group ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2009/01/24/business/three-nonlife-insurers-plan-2010-integration/)). In 2015 it paid £3.47 billion for Amlin, the second-biggest insurer in the Lloyd's of London market — Reuters Breakingviews called it expensive but strategically legible ([Reuters Breakingviews](https://www.business-standard.com/article/opinion/land-of-the-surprising-sum-115090801350_1.html)). And in April 2027 it is scheduled to merge with its sister company Aioi Nissay Dowa, as the industry tries to rebuild public trust after price-fixing scandals ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2026/01/12/companies/msad-consolidate-domestic-bases/)).

## The 2001 merger: born from deregulation and bank politics

The 2001 tieup was announced in March 2000, with the timetable moved up six months, and was as much about Japanese banking politics as insurance. Mitsui Marine had just broken away from a three-way consolidation with Nippon Fire & Marine and Koa Fire & Marine after it became clear that those two firms' participation in a Sanwa Bank-led financial alliance made the consolidation unworkable; the tieup was also widely seen as influenced by the merger between Sumitomo Bank and Sakura Bank, which headed the two firms' respective corporate groups ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2000/03/28/business/sumitomo-mitsui-tieup-advanced-by-six-months/)).

The business plan was blunt: raise premium revenues from a combined ¥1.15 trillion in fiscal 1998 to ¥1.24 trillion in fiscal 2004, cut 390 offices (360 domestic, 30 abroad), slash 3,000 workers through attrition and transfers, and quadruple domestic non-life net profit from ¥21.1 billion to ¥84 billion in six years ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2000/03/28/business/sumitomo-mitsui-tieup-advanced-by-six-months/)). Beyond domestic non-life, the new firm planned to enlarge a life-insurance unit formed by merging the two insurers' subsidiaries, offer financial services including asset liquidation and real-estate securitization, and step up overseas operations in Asia and Latin America — especially Brazil, where Japanese corporations were active and deregulation was opening doors ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2000/03/28/business/sumitomo-mitsui-tieup-advanced-by-six-months/)).

## The 2010 integration: Japan's largest non-life group

Nine years later, Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance Group Holdings agreed with Aioi Insurance and Nissay Dowa General Insurance to integrate their managements in April 2010. The combined entity held ¥2.73 trillion in premium revenue — overtaking industry leader Tokio Marine Holdings (¥2.25 trillion) — and became Japan's largest non-life insurance group under the MS&AD Insurance Group Holdings name ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2009/01/24/business/three-nonlife-insurers-plan-2010-integration/)).

The rationale given at the joint Tokyo news conference was demographic and economic: a declining birthrate, a graying population, falling car ownership, and slumping car sales hitting automobile-policy sales — "a key nonlife insurance business" — against the backdrop of the 2008 financial crisis ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2009/01/24/business/three-nonlife-insurers-plan-2010-integration/)). President Toshiaki Egashira said the three firms shared "the sense of crisis and understanding of the environment," and they aimed to streamline businesses, increase profitability, and build "a world-leading insurance and financial group that operates globally" ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2009/01/24/business/three-nonlife-insurers-plan-2010-integration/)).

## The Amlin deal: buying into Lloyd's

The group's international turn came on 8 September 2015, when MSI agreed to buy Amlin plc for 670 pence a share in cash — valuing the Lloyd's insurer and reinsurer at £3.47 billion, a 36% premium over the prior close and 2.4 times Amlin's net tangible book value ([Insurance Times](https://www.insurancetimes.co.uk/mitsui-sumitomo-agrees-347bn-takeover-of-amlin/1415410.article)). Amlin's board recommended the offer unanimously ([Insurance Times](https://www.insurancetimes.co.uk/mitsui-sumitomo-agrees-347bn-takeover-of-amlin/1415410.article)).

Reuters Breakingviews judged that Mitsui was "assuming a fair bit": at 2.4 times tangible book, it was the highest valuation of the various recent acquisitions of Lloyd's insurers, and Amlin's expected 2017 operating profit implied only a ~5.8% return on the enterprise value ([Reuters Breakingviews](https://www.business-standard.com/article/opinion/land-of-the-surprising-sum-115090801350_1.html)). But the strategic logic held: MS&AD wrote only 11% of its premiums overseas while rival Tokio Marine wrote 47%, and Mitsui's cost of capital was around half Amlin's — meaning the deal was, in Breakingviews' words, part of "corporate Japan's" wave of overseas acquisitions funded by cheap domestic capital under Abenomics ([Reuters Breakingviews](https://www.business-standard.com/article/opinion/land-of-the-surprising-sum-115090801350_1.html)).

## The 2027 merger — and a trust crisis

The serial-consolidation story continues. MS&AD's two core non-life subsidiaries — Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance and Aioi Nissay Dowa Insurance — are scheduled to merge on 1 April 2027, with the holding company renamed Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance Group, reflecting the unified brand ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2026/01/12/companies/msad-consolidate-domestic-bases/)). MS&AD president Shinichiro Funabiki said the group would consolidate domestic bases from about 360 to 240, cut costs including through an early-retirement program, and target ¥700 billion in profit by fiscal 2030 to sustain dividend growth ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2026/01/12/companies/msad-consolidate-domestic-bases/)).

The merger is also a response to damage. The Japan Times reports that "a series of scandals in the nonlife insurance industry, including fraudulent insurance claims and price-fixing of insurance policies for corporate clients," has pushed the Financial Services Agency to revise the insurance business law and supervisory guidelines; Funabiki said the company must "rebuild a customer-oriented business model" and keep restoring public trust ([The Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2026/01/12/companies/msad-consolidate-domestic-bases/)).

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not date the company's founding to the predecessor firms' 1918/1893 origins — those claims appeared only on the company's own pages or in tertiary mirrors, which this wave does not cite; the profile uses the 2001 merger that directly created MSI.
- It does not assert the 2027 merger as completed; it is scheduled and announced, subject to shareholder and regulatory approvals.
- It does not verify current premiums, headcount, overseas share, or the ¥700 billion fiscal-2030 profit target — hard numbers here are dated to 2009–2015 reporting.
- It does not investigate or adjudicate the price-fixing and fraudulent-claims scandals; it reports only that The Japan Times describes them as the context for the FSA's regulatory revisions and the merger's trust-rebuilding rationale.
