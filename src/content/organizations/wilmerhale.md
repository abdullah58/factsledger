## Overview

WilmerHale is the law firm the second Trump administration tried to punish for having once employed Robert Mueller. Formed by a 2004 merger of Boston's Hale and Dorr and Washington's Wilmer Cutler Pickering, the firm grew to more than 1,100 lawyers and $1.34 billion in annual revenue — and then, in March 2025, became one of four firms hit with an executive order suspending their lawyers' security clearances and pressuring clients to drop them ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/legalindustry/new-york-ex-prosecutor-elected-next-leader-wilmer-hale-law-firm-2023-06-15/); [Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-judge-blocks-trump-order-against-law-firm-wilmerhale/)). The firm fought back, hired Bush-era solicitor general Paul Clement to make its case, and won a sweeping ruling from a Republican-appointed judge who struck the order down as retaliation for protected speech ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-judge-blocks-trump-order-against-law-firm-wilmerhale/)). It is also the firm where Attorney General [Todd Blanche](/people/todd-blanche/) practiced as a partner after leaving federal prosecution in 2014 ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/dag/bio/deputy-attorney-general-todd-blanche)).

## The executive-order fight

On March 27, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order 14250, titled "Addressing Risks from WilmerHale," accusing the firm of activities that undermine justice and the interests of the United States ([Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/107087/tracker-litigation-legal-challenges-trump-administration/)). The order directed federal agencies to suspend the security clearances of the firm's employees pending a national-interest review, terminate the firm's government contracts to the extent permitted by law, and limit employees' access to federal buildings ([Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/107087/tracker-litigation-legal-challenges-trump-administration/)). The Associated Press reported that the order explicitly cited the firm's prior employment of Robert Mueller, the Republican-appointed special counsel who led the Russia-interference investigation Trump has derided as a "witch hunt" ([Associated Press](https://srnnews.com/trump-campaign-against-law-firms-dealt-another-setback-as-judge-blocks-executive-order/)).

The firm sued the next day, March 28, alleging the order violated the First Amendment (retaliation for protected expression, viewpoint discrimination, the right to petition, freedom of association), the Fifth and Sixth Amendments, and the separation of powers ([Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/107087/tracker-litigation-legal-challenges-trump-administration/)). U.S. District Judge Richard Leon — a George W. Bush appointee — granted a temporary restraining order the same day, though he declined to block the security-clearance section, writing that clearance decisions sit "within the purview of the Executive Branch" ([Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/107087/tracker-litigation-legal-challenges-trump-administration/)). WilmerHale argued the order was "flagrantly" unconstitutional, and its case was argued by Paul Clement, the prominent conservative lawyer who served as solicitor general under Bush ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-judge-blocks-trump-order-against-law-firm-wilmerhale/)).

On May 27, 2025, Leon issued a 73-page opinion granting the firm summary judgment on eight of its eleven counts — dismissing with prejudice the equal-protection, right-to-counsel and spending-power claims — and striking the order down as unconstitutional ([Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/107087/tracker-litigation-legal-challenges-trump-administration/)). "I have concluded that this order must be struck down in its entirety as unconstitutional," he wrote. "Indeed, to rule otherwise would be unfaithful to the judgment and vision of the Founding Fathers!" ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-judge-blocks-trump-order-against-law-firm-wilmerhale/)). Leon found the order was retaliation against the firm in violation of free-speech and due-process protections, calling it "a staggering punishment for the firm's protected speech" that harmed its ability to represent clients ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-judge-blocks-trump-order-against-law-firm-wilmerhale/)). The ruling was the third that month against the law-firm orders: Judge Beryl Howell had struck down the Perkins Coie order on May 2 and Judge John Bates the Jenner & Block order on May 23, with Susman Godfrey's challenge still pending ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-judge-blocks-trump-order-against-law-firm-wilmerhale/)). A White House spokesman responded that reviewing the president's clearance decisions "falls well outside the judiciary's authority" ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-judge-blocks-trump-order-against-law-firm-wilmerhale/)).

The backdrop was a wider capitulation campaign: nine large firms — including Paul Weiss, Latham & Watkins, Skadden and Willkie Farr — reached deals with Trump that averted punitive actions, pledging a combined total of nearly $1 billion in free legal services to advance causes he supports ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/us-judge-blocks-trump-order-against-law-firm-wilmerhale/)). Four firms chose to sue instead. On July 25, 2025, the administration filed a notice of appeal to the D.C. Circuit in the WilmerHale case ([Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/107087/tracker-litigation-legal-challenges-trump-administration/)). On October 8, 2025, the circuit held the appeal in abeyance during the federal government shutdown ([Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/107087/tracker-litigation-legal-challenges-trump-administration/)). On February 6, 2026, the court consolidated the four law-firm appeals — Perkins Coie (No. 25-5241), Jenner & Block (No. 25-5265), WilmerHale (No. 25-5277) and Susman Godfrey (No. 25-5310) — and scheduled oral argument for May 14, 2026, before the same panel hearing a related case; state attorneys general filed an amicus brief for the firms on April 3, 2026 ([Just Security](https://www.justsecurity.org/107087/tracker-litigation-legal-challenges-trump-administration/)).

## The firm and its scale

WilmerHale was built from the 2004 merger of two legacy firms: Boston's Hale and Dorr and Washington's Wilmer Cutler Pickering ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/legalindustry/new-york-ex-prosecutor-elected-next-leader-wilmer-hale-law-firm-2023-06-15/)). Under co-managing partners Susan Murley and Robert Novick, who led the firm together from 2012, it grew to over 1,100 lawyers and opened offices in Denver (2013) and San Francisco (2019), generating more than $1.34 billion in revenue and $2.82 million in profits per equity partner in 2022, per American Lawyer figures ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/legalindustry/new-york-ex-prosecutor-elected-next-leader-wilmer-hale-law-firm-2023-06-15/)). In June 2023 the firm elected New York white-collar litigator Anjan Sahni — a former federal prosecutor — as its next managing partner; he took office January 1, 2024, succeeding Murley and Novick and becoming the firm's first single leader since the merger ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/legalindustry/new-york-ex-prosecutor-elected-next-leader-wilmer-hale-law-firm-2023-06-15/)).

## Blanche's time at the firm

Todd Blanche spent eight years as a federal prosecutor in the Southern District of New York, rising to co-chief of the Violent Crimes Unit and the White Plains Division, before entering private practice in 2014 ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/dag/bio/deputy-attorney-general-todd-blanche)). His Justice Department biography says he then worked "as a partner at firms, including WilmerHale and Cadwalader, Wickersham & Taft" ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/dag/bio/deputy-attorney-general-todd-blanche)). He went on to serve as Deputy Attorney General in 2025, acted as Attorney General in 2026, and was confirmed on August 8, 2026 as the 88th Attorney General of the United States ([U.S. Department of Justice](https://www.justice.gov/dag/bio/deputy-attorney-general-todd-blanche)).
