## The museum that started in a pig barn

The Svalbard Museum's mission is grand — "to collect, preserve, inform about, and do research on natural and cultural history, environmental issues, and cultural heritage protection in Svalbard" — but its premises were humble. The first committee formed in Longyearbyen in 1964; five years later, 86 artifacts were stored in the old post office below the church. The first board of directors was established on January 18, 1979, and in 1981 the museum welcomed the public into its premises in the old pig barn — Grisefjøsen — for the first time, with the venue officially opened on February 20, 1982.

For years the whole operation ran on volunteer commitment: until 1998, the museum had no employees at all. Professionalization began in 1999, again in close cooperation with the local community. The museum passed through several forms — a subsidiary of the local Svalbard Council, a foundation in 2000, a department of the newly established Longyearbyen Community Council from 2002 — before becoming a foundation once more in January 2006 and moving into the newly built Svalbard Science Centre, where its exhibitions, storerooms, laboratories and offices occupy about 1,500 square metres. Its founders — the Longyearbyen Community Council, the Store Norske coal company, the University Centre in Svalbard, the Norwegian Polar Institute and the Governor of Svalbard — still appoint its board.

The museum's mandate has since expanded beyond its walls: in 2023 it established a competence center for cultural heritage, which — under agreements with the Governor of Svalbard and Norway's Ministry of Trade, Industry and Fisheries — advises, monitors and secures state-owned buildings and sites across the archipelago, including trapping cabins, mining facilities and cableway installations. ## The polar bears

The museum holds five stuffed polar bears — three adults and two cubs — and pointedly notes that none were killed for stuffing and display: all are skins from bears shot in other contexts, most often in self-defense, allocated by the Governor upon application. The first was stuffed in 1977 — the first stuffed polar bear in Svalbard — after Governor Leif Eldring wished in 1975 for one at Longyearbyen School; taxidermist Karsten Felsted of Tromsø Museum built it by damoplasty, a clay-model-then-plastic-mold method yielding a hollow 25–30 kg body that could stand, lifelike, on three feet with one paw lifted.

The second bear has spikes mounted on its feet and was exhibited on a styrofoam plate made to look like ice. When the museum moved to the Science Centre, that bear stayed behind in the pigsty, which now serves as storage for larger objects. The third — a 505 kg, 257 cm male shot at Kapp Lee on March 5, 2005, by a UNIS security officer at a distance of 140 cm after warning shots failed — became the centerpiece of the 2006 exhibition and remains so in its February 2024 version. One cub was killed in 1995 by Bamse, a seven-year-old station dog at Isfjord Radio, while the crew was on the phone with the Governor; another, injured in the foot, was euthanized in Van Mijenfjorden in 2004. A 2021 newspaper count found 17 stuffed polar bears in Longyearbyen — likely the most per capita of any city on Earth.
