## Overview

Employment and Immigration Canada (EIC) was the federal department that ran [Canada](/places/canada/)'s employment services, unemployment insurance and immigration programs from 1977 until the 1992–93 government reorganization. Created on 15 August 1977 by the Employment and Immigration Reorganization Act, it merged the Unemployment Insurance Commission with the Department of Manpower and Immigration, and it was run jointly by a department and a tripartite commission — a structure no other federal institution shared ([Library and Archives Canada](https://recherche-collection-search.bac-lac.gc.ca/eng/home/record?app=fonandcol&IdNumber=287)).

Its legal title was the Department of Employment and Immigration; "Employment and Immigration Canada" was the applied title, in French Emploi et Immigration Canada ([Government of Canada (Translation Bureau)](https://www.btb.termiumplus.gc.ca/tpv2alpha/alpha-eng.html?lang=eng&i=&index=ent&srchtxt=EIC)). From 1977 to 1992 it united immigration, employment and unemployment insurance under one roof, admitting visitors, permanent residents and refugees and helping newcomers settle ([York University](http://www.yorku.ca/lfoster/2012-13/MPPAL%206130/lectures/ADMINISTRATIVE%20HISTORY%20OF%20IMMIGRATION.pdf)). Its defining work was the 1979–80 resettlement of 60,049 Indochinese refugees, over half of them privately sponsored. Its functions then split between two successors: immigration to the Department of Citizenship and Immigration, and employment to Human Resources Development Canada ([Library and Archives Canada](http://central.bac-lac.gc.ca:80/.redirect?app=FonAndCol&id=373&lang=eng)).

## A department run by two bodies

EIC inherited two older machineries. The Unemployment Insurance Commission dated to 1941, when the Unemployment Insurance Act created a three-commissioner body — one each from management, labour and government — to run compulsory unemployment insurance and the National Employment Service, and from 1964 it also kept the register of Social Insurance Numbers ([Library and Archives Canada](https://recherche-collection-search.bac-lac.gc.ca/eng/home/record?app=fonandcol&IdNumber=287)). The employment service itself had wandered between departments for decades: part of the Department of Labour during the war as the National Selective Service, back to the insurance commission, then to the new Department of Manpower and Immigration in 1966 ([Library and Archives Canada](https://recherche-collection-search.bac-lac.gc.ca/eng/home/record?idnumber=69&app=FonAndCol&ecopy=)).

What made EIC unusual was that the 1977 Act did not fold the commission into the department — it kept both. The Deputy Minister chaired the Canada Employment and Immigration Commission and the Assistant Deputy Minister was vice-chair, alongside one member representing workers and one representing employers, all appointed by the Governor-in-Council for five-year terms ([Library and Archives Canada](http://central.bac-lac.gc.ca:80/.redirect?app=FonAndCol&id=373&lang=eng)). The commission was a body corporate that could deal directly with other countries and organizations, yet it oversaw most of the department's work instead of standing at arm's length, reporting annually to Parliament through the minister. Its seven divisions covered labour-market development, employment and insurance, and immigration and demographic policy, while the manpower arm split into Employment Services and Labour Market Services, working from [Ottawa](/places/ottawa/) through hundreds of centres across the country. EIC also funded outside research, including [Colleges and Institutes Canada](/organizations/colleges-and-institutes-canada/)'s multi-year "Making Canada Productive" study with $134,000 ([Colleges and Institutes Canada](https://www.collegesinstitutes.ca/cicans-50th-anniversary/evolution-of-cican/)), and its 1985 Canadian Jobs Strategy later drew the association into a long 1987 hearing before the House of Commons committee on employment and immigration.

## The Indochinese resettlement

EIC's largest operation began quietly. In 1978 Minister Bud Cullen approved the Private Refugee Sponsorship Program, which the government launched that July alongside the E&I pamphlet "Sponsoring Refugees: Facts for Canadian Groups and Organizations," and in March 1979 the Mennonite Central Committee signed the first master sponsorship agreement ([Canadian Indochinese Historical Society](https://cihs-shic.ca/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/A-Chronology-of-the-Indochinese-Refugee-Movement-1975-v2-1.pdf)). A matching centre in Ottawa paired arriving families with sponsors, and in June 1979 the government raised its target from 5,000 to 8,000 Indochinese refugees plus 4,000 more from the voluntary sector. Then, on 18 July 1979, Ministers Ron Atkey and Flora MacDonald announced Canada would resettle 50,000 by the end of 1980 — 21,000 privately sponsored, matched by 21,000 government-assisted — days before a [United Nations](/organizations/united-nations/) emergency conference in Geneva endorsed the same three-way bargain of asylum, orderly departure and resettlement.

Delivering on that promise strained the new system to breaking point. The government chartered 76 flights carrying 15,800 refugees by the end of 1979, while Department of National Defence aircraft flew more than 2,000 more from Hong Kong, with staging areas at Longue Pointe in Montreal and Greisbach Barracks in Edmonton. Community groups answered in force: by December 1979, 5,456 private groups had applied to sponsor 29,269 refugees, far beyond the 21,000 sought, and projects such as Operation Lifeline in [Toronto](/places/toronto/) sprang up to organize them. In April 1980 Minister Lloyd Axworthy added 10,000 more government-assisted places, taking the target to 60,000, and the last flight landed on 8 December 1980.

EIC's own 1981 report counted 60,049 Indochinese refugees resettled by 8 December 1980 — 32,281 of them (53.8%) privately sponsored — more than six times the 9,060 resettled in the previous four years combined, and the operation that established private sponsorship as a working part of Canada's refugee system ([Canadian Indochinese Historical Society](https://cihs-shic.ca/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/A-Chronology-of-the-Indochinese-Refugee-Movement-1975-v2-1.pdf)).

## Three end dates, one reorganization

Ask when EIC ended and three authoritative sources give three answers — because they measure different things. Library and Archives Canada says the commission and the department "no longer existed after government re-organization in 1992-93," with functions transferred to the Departments of Citizenship and Immigration Canada and Human Resources Development Canada ([Library and Archives Canada](http://central.bac-lac.gc.ca:80/.redirect?app=FonAndCol&id=373&lang=eng)). In June 1993 Prime Minister Kim Campbell announced the restructuring that created the Department of Human Resources and Labour, soon Human Resources Development Canada, merging EIC's employment and unemployment insurance programs with social-development functions from Health and Welfare Canada, education functions from the Secretary of State, and all of Labour Canada ([Library and Archives Canada](https://recherche-collection-search.bac-lac.gc.ca/eng/home/record?app=fonandcol&IdNumber=287)).

The government's own terminology bank instead records that EIC "was dissolved in 1994, and replaced by Citizenship and Immigration Canada," the year the Department of Citizenship and Immigration Act ratified the successor ([Government of Canada (Translation Bureau)](https://www.btb.termiumplus.gc.ca/tpv2alpha/alpha-eng.html?lang=eng&i=&index=ent&srchtxt=EIC); [York University](http://www.yorku.ca/lfoster/2012-13/MPPAL%206130/lectures/ADMINISTRATIVE%20HISTORY%20OF%20IMMIGRATION.pdf)). And the statute that formally established Human Resources Development and repealed the old framework — the Department of Human Resources Development Act — was only assented to on 29 May 1996 ([Justice Laws](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/H-5.8/20051005/P1TT3xt3.html?wbdisable=true)). So the honest dating is layered: operations ended in 1992–93, the immigration successor was legislated in 1994, and the old legal framework was repealed in 1996. The 1996 Act also carried the commission forward in new form, as the Canada Employment Insurance Commission under Human Resources Development.

| Function | Successor |
| --- | --- |
| Immigration | Department of Citizenship and Immigration (created 1993; legislated 1994) |
| Employment and unemployment insurance | Human Resources Development Canada (created 1993) |
| The commission itself | Canada Employment Insurance Commission, continued under the 1996 Act |

## FAQ

### What was Employment and Immigration Canada?

It was the federal department that ran Canada's employment services, unemployment insurance and immigration programs from 1977 to 1992–93. Created on 15 August 1977 by merging the Unemployment Insurance Commission with the Department of Manpower and Immigration, it was jointly run by a department and a tripartite commission — a structure unique among federal institutions.

### Why was it run by both a department and a commission?

The 1977 Reorganization Act kept the old Unemployment Insurance Commission alive as the Canada Employment and Immigration Commission instead of absorbing it. The Deputy Minister chaired it, worker and employer representatives sat on it, and it oversaw most of the department's work while reporting annually to Parliament through the minister.

### What happened to Employment and Immigration Canada?

It ended in the 1992–93 government reorganization under Prime Minister Kim Campbell. Its immigration functions went to the new Department of Citizenship and Immigration (legislated in 1994), and its employment and unemployment insurance programs went to Human Resources Development Canada. The old legal framework was formally repealed by the 1996 HRDC Act.

### Is it the same as Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada?

No. Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada is the present-day department on the immigration side, descended from EIC through the Department of Citizenship and Immigration created in 1993. EIC itself also covered employment and unemployment insurance, which today sit with Employment and Social Development Canada.

### What was its biggest achievement?

The 1979–80 resettlement of 60,049 Indochinese refugees, announced as a 50,000 target in July 1979 and completed that December. More than half — 32,281 people — were privately sponsored by Canadian community groups, which established private sponsorship as a working part of Canada's refugee system.

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not give a single end date for EIC: Library and Archives Canada says operations ceased in 1992–93, TERMIUM says dissolved in 1994, and the repealing statute was assented to in 1996. The profile presents all three and explains why they differ.
- It does not list every Minister of Employment and Immigration; only Bud Cullen, Ron Atkey and Lloyd Axworthy are named, from the fully-read resettlement chronology.
- It does not assert a budget, staff count or office count for EIC; no such figures appeared in the fully-read sources.
- It does not treat EIC as the same body as Employment and Social Development Canada, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada, or Human Resources Development Canada — all are successors or descendants, not the same department.
