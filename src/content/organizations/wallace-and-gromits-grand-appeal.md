## Overview

Wallace and Gromit's Grand Appeal — known for short as The Grand Appeal — is the official charity of Bristol Children's Hospital in the [United Kingdom](/places/united-kingdom/), founded in 1995 to raise money for a new children's hospital and still fundraising thirty years later, having generated around £90 million ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/the-grand-appeal/)). Its public face is borrowed: the Bristol animation studio Aardman allowed the charity to use its characters Wallace & Gromit to spearhead fundraising, and creator Nick Park and Aardman co-founders Peter Lord and David Sproxton remain active patrons ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/); [Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/third-sector-awards-medium-corporate-partnership-year-%e2%80%93-grand-appeal-aardman-animations/article/1968801?utm_source=website&utm_medium=social)).

The name needs untangling, because three labels circulate for what is essentially one charity family. "The Grand Appeal" is the short working name; the registered charity behind it is Wallace & Gromit's Children's Foundation, number 1043603; and Wallace & Gromit's Children's Charity, established in 2003, is the arm that supports sick children in hospitals and hospices throughout the UK rather than in Bristol alone ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/the-grand-appeal/); [The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/)). This profile is about the Bristol charity, and it also records the one episode its own history page leaves out: a £9,500 theft by a man who went on to steal far more from another charity.

## Building a children's hospital

The Grand Appeal began in 1995 with a single mission: to fundraise for a new building for Bristol Children's Hospital to replace the Victorian building on St Michael's Hill ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/)). Aardman's Nick Park, Peter Lord and David Sproxton allowed the charity to use Wallace & Gromit for the appeal, and instead of inventing a mascot, the charity built its fundraising around the most famous animated duo in Britain ([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/third-sector-awards-medium-corporate-partnership-year-%e2%80%93-grand-appeal-aardman-animations/article/1968801?utm_source=website&utm_medium=social)).

A site was found on Upper Maudlin Street, next to the Bristol Royal Infirmary, and building work began; the new purpose-built hospital opened in April 2001, with around 100 patients transferred from the old building, and fundraising reached £12 million ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/)). The charity's own pages differ slightly on the original target — the current main page says £10 million, while the history timeline and Third Sector's 2026 awards report say £11 million — but the outcome is not in doubt: the appeal succeeded, the Prince of Wales performed the opening ceremony, and the charity stayed on as the hospital's dedicated charity rather than winding up ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/); [Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/third-sector-awards-medium-corporate-partnership-year-%e2%80%93-grand-appeal-aardman-animations/article/1968801?utm_source=website&utm_medium=social)).

What followed was a steady widening of the mission. In 2002 the charity funded dedicated family accommodation at Ronald McDonald House and a paediatric MRI scanner, the first of its kind in the South West, opened by the Princess Royal ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/)). In 2004 it brought music therapy into the hospital, in 2005 it funded a limb reconstruction unit, and in 2010 it launched the Cots for Tots Appeal with the Bristol Evening Post for the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit at St Michael's Hospital, reaching £1 million by 2012 and opening a 12-bedroom home-from-home, Cots for Tots House, for parents of critically ill babies ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/)). By 2019 it was funding a 3D Cardiac Bio-printer that prints exact replicas of patients' hearts to improve care for congenital heart disease ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/)).

## The trails that made it famous

If the hospital appeal made the charity, the sculpture trails made it a national story. Wrong Trousers Day, launched in 1997 and inspired by Aardman's Oscar-winning film, became an annual fixture, going national in 2003 with celebrities including Gary Lineker, Ainsley Harriott and Michael Palin ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/)). Then came Gromit Unleashed in 2013: 80 giant Gromit sculptures, decorated by artists, celebrities and designers, displayed across Bristol and auctioned for £2.3 million for the children's hospital ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/); [University of Bristol](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2013/9812.html)). The University of Bristol's own account of the auction night records unprecedented demand for the five-foot statues, a live stream watched by more than 17,000 people online, and the university itself paying £20,000 to keep the red-and-white "Bark at Ee" in the city ([University of Bristol](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2013/9812.html)).

The formula repeated: Shaun in the City in 2015 put 120 Shaun the Sheep sculptures across London and Bristol, and Gromit Unleashed 2 in 2018 raised £2 million ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/history/)). Third Sector reports that Gromit Unleashed alone has raised more than £20 million since 2013 and has grown into a retail operation selling Wallace & Gromit merchandise, with all proceeds going to the charity ([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/third-sector-awards-medium-corporate-partnership-year-%e2%80%93-grand-appeal-aardman-animations/article/1968801?utm_source=website&utm_medium=social)). In September 2026 the partnership itself won an award: the Grand Appeal–Aardman collaboration was named Medium Corporate Partnership of the Year at the Third Sector Awards, with judges praising a 30-year partnership in which Aardman had embedded its creativity and intellectual property into the charity's mission rather than merely lending a logo ([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/third-sector-awards-medium-corporate-partnership-year-%e2%80%93-grand-appeal-aardman-animations/article/1968801?utm_source=website&utm_medium=social)).

## The theft that connects it to Article 25

The Grand Appeal's history has one dark footnote, and it is the reason this profile sits alongside [Article 25](/organizations/article-25/) in the FactsLedger graph. In December 2015, [Scott Golding](/people/scott-golding/), then 47, was sentenced at Snaresbrook Crown Court to four years and eight months in prison after pleading guilty to stealing £260,479 from the humanitarian charity Article 25, using its money for designer goods and rent and presenting its senior management with fictitious accounts ([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/charity-worker-jailed-260k-fraud/finance/article/1377751)). The fraud almost closed Article 25, leaving it with just £611 in its account before emergency funds from the architecture and construction industry saved it ([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/charity-worker-jailed-260k-fraud/finance/article/1377751)).

What makes the case relevant here is what came before it. Golding had joined Article 25 to look after its accounts in 2013 after being released from a six-month prison sentence for stealing £9,500 from the Bristol children's hospital charity, Wallace and Gromit's Grand Appeal ([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/charity-worker-jailed-260k-fraud/finance/article/1377751)). At Article 25 he worked under his middle name, William, and a Charity Commission spokesman later said the charity had not been aware of the previous conviction and was itself a victim of serious fraud ([Third Sector](https://www.thirdsector.co.uk/charity-worker-jailed-260k-fraud/finance/article/1377751)). The Grand Appeal's own published history does not mention the theft; this profile records it because it is the documented link between the two charities, and because it explains a hiring failure that nearly destroyed one of them.

Today the charity looks forward rather than back. It describes its next project as a new Patient Hotel, the first of its kind in the UK for sick children, to be funded and operated by The Grand Appeal itself ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/the-grand-appeal/)). Its stated mission is to put Bristol Children's Hospital at the centre of change, keeping the hospital at the forefront of children's healthcare into the next decade and beyond ([The Grand Appeal](https://www.grandappeal.org.uk/the-grand-appeal/)).
