## Overview

iQIYI is a Chinese subscription video-streaming service owned by [Baidu](/organizations/baidu/), often described as China's answer to [Netflix](/organizations/netflix/). Launched in April 2010 as Qiyi and listed on Nasdaq in 2018, it grew into one of China's largest video platforms, passed 100 million paying subscribers in 2019, and recorded its first annual profit in 2022 after more than a decade of losses ([SEC IPO prospectus](https://cdn.gmtresearch.com/public-ckfinder/hall-of-shame/iQIYI/Prospectus-Mar2018_iQIYI.pdf)).

The company was founded by Yu Gong, who remains its chief executive, and is headquartered in Beijing ([SEC IPO prospectus](https://cdn.gmtresearch.com/public-ckfinder/hall-of-shame/iQIYI/Prospectus-Mar2018_iQIYI.pdf)). Its business rests on a Netflix-like mix of paid memberships and advertising: at the time of its IPO filing it claimed more than 50 million paying subscribers and 420 million monthly viewers ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/02/28/baidu-iqiyi-us-ipo/)). Its main rivals are Tencent Video and [Alibaba](/organizations/alibaba/)-owned Youku Tudou ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/02/28/baidu-iqiyi-us-ipo/)). What sets iQIYI apart from a straightforward Netflix clone is its ownership: Baidu built it, funded its losses for years, and still controls it through a dual-class share structure, holding all of the high-vote Class B shares ([Variety](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/chinas-iqiyi-sets-nasdaq-share-listing-2018-02-27)).

## From a Baidu project to a Nasdaq listing

iQIYI began as qiyi.com, launched in April 2010 as an internet video streaming service in China, and was rebranded as iQIYI in November 2011 ([SEC IPO prospectus](https://cdn.gmtresearch.com/public-ckfinder/hall-of-shame/iQIYI/Prospectus-Mar2018_iQIYI.pdf)). Baidu was the driving force from the start: between March 2010 and September 2014 it made substantial investments in the company, issuing ordinary and preferred shares to Baidu Holdings ([SEC IPO prospectus](https://cdn.gmtresearch.com/public-ckfinder/hall-of-shame/iQIYI/Prospectus-Mar2018_iQIYI.pdf)). In May 2013 Baidu bought the online video business of PPS for $370 million and merged it into iQIYI, with PPS continuing as a sub-brand, in a deal intended to create China's largest online video platform by mobile users and viewing time ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/05/07/baidu-confirms-370m-purchase-of-pps-underscoring-online-videos-importance-for-internet-companies-around-the-world/)).

The road to the public markets was not smooth. In 2016, founder Gong Yu and Baidu co-founder [Robin Li](/people/robin-li/) together tried to take iQIYI private with a $2.8 billion offer to buy it from Baidu; the offer was challenged by US investors and quickly withdrawn ([Variety](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/chinas-iqiyi-sets-nasdaq-share-listing-2018-02-27)). Two years later iQIYI filed to list on Nasdaq, seeking to raise up to $1.5 billion ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/02/28/baidu-iqiyi-us-ipo/)). At the filing Baidu's stake stood at 70%, diluted from 80.5%, with Xiaomi holding 8% and Hillhouse 6% ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/02/28/baidu-iqiyi-us-ipo/)). Crucially, Baidu kept control: it held all of the Class B shares, each carrying ten votes, giving it 93.3% of total voting power immediately after the offering ([SEC IPO prospectus](https://cdn.gmtresearch.com/public-ckfinder/hall-of-shame/iQIYI/Prospectus-Mar2018_iQIYI.pdf)). The listing completed at the end of March 2018 at $18.00 per ADS, raising $2.25 billion ([AFP](https://phys.org/news/2018-04-baidu-net-profit-video-spin-off.html)).

The prospectus laid bare the cost of growth. iQIYI had been unprofitable since day one, losing RMB 3.7 billion ($574 million) on revenue of RMB 17.4 billion ($2.7 billion) in 2017 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/02/28/baidu-iqiyi-us-ipo/)). Baidu had propped it up financially, including an interest-free $100 million loan in the month before the filing ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/02/28/baidu-iqiyi-us-ipo/)). Yet the scale was real: iQIYI was the largest internet video streaming service in China in 2017 by user time spent and average total monthly active users, with 421.3 million average mobile monthly active users and 126.0 million mobile daily active users in the fourth quarter of 2017 ([SEC IPO prospectus](https://cdn.gmtresearch.com/public-ckfinder/hall-of-shame/iQIYI/Prospectus-Mar2018_iQIYI.pdf)).

## Original content and the subscription engine

iQIYI's strategy was to turn viewers into paying members through original programming, and its early hits proved the model could work. *The Lost Tomb*, released in 2015 as one of China's first high-budget original internet dramas, drew more than 100 million views in its first 24 hours and over 4 billion in total, while *The Mystic Nine* and *Burning Ice* together generated about 13 billion views ([SEC IPO prospectus](https://cdn.gmtresearch.com/public-ckfinder/hall-of-shame/iQIYI/Prospectus-Mar2018_iQIYI.pdf)). Its variety shows worked the same way: *Qipa Talk*, running since 2014, and *The Rap of China* each passed 3 billion views ([SEC IPO prospectus](https://cdn.gmtresearch.com/public-ckfinder/hall-of-shame/iQIYI/Prospectus-Mar2018_iQIYI.pdf)). In 2017 its originals accounted for five of China's top ten original internet variety shows and six of the top ten original internet drama series ([SEC IPO prospectus](https://cdn.gmtresearch.com/public-ckfinder/hall-of-shame/iQIYI/Prospectus-Mar2018_iQIYI.pdf)).

The breakout came in 2018 with *The Story of Yanxi Palace*, a Qing-dynasty palace drama co-produced with Huanyu Film that gathered more than 20 billion views between July and September, was China's most-watched online drama for 39 consecutive days, peaked at 700 million daily views, and was distributed in 70 countries ([China Film Insider](https://chinafilminsider.com/the-story-of-yanxi-palace-drives-iqiyi-revenue-as-profits-remain-elusive/)). Paying subscribers got early access to episodes — eight ahead of free users — and in that quarter iQIYI's subscribers topped 80 million, up 89% year on year, while membership revenue rose 78% to RMB 2.9 billion ([China Film Insider](https://chinafilminsider.com/the-story-of-yanxi-palace-drives-iqiyi-revenue-as-profits-remain-elusive/)). The momentum carried into 2019, when iQIYI became the first Chinese streamer to pass 100 million paying subscribers, reaching 105.8 million by September — ahead of [Tencent](/organizations/tencent/) Video at 100 million, though still behind Netflix's 158 million ([KrASIA](https://amp.kr-asia.com/even-with-over-100-million-paying-subscribers-chinese-netflix-style-providers-are-facing-a-tough-time)). The price was steep: in the third quarter of 2019 alone it spent RMB 6.2 billion on content, equal to 84% of its revenue in the period ([KrASIA](https://amp.kr-asia.com/even-with-over-100-million-paying-subscribers-chinese-netflix-style-providers-are-facing-a-tough-time)).

## Fraud allegations, first profit, and renewed losses

In April 2020, days after the Luckin Coffee accounting scandal, short seller Wolfpack Research — assisted by Muddy Waters — accused iQIYI of inflating its 2019 revenue by roughly RMB 8 billion to 13 billion (27% to 44%) and overstating user numbers by 42% to 60%, based on surveys of 1,563 people in its target demographic ([KrASIA](https://kr-asia.com/chinas-largest-streaming-service-hit-with-fraud-allegations-just-days-after-luckin-bombshell)). iQIYI replied that the report contained "numerous errors, unsubstantiated statements, and misleading conclusions and interpretations" ([KrASIA](https://kr-asia.com/chinas-largest-streaming-service-hit-with-fraud-allegations-just-days-after-luckin-bombshell)). The US Securities and Exchange Commission then sought financial and operating records dating from January 1, 2018, and iQIYI began its own internal review ([Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2020-10-07/chinese-video-streamer-says-internal-review-debunks-fraud-claims-101612574.html)). In October 2020 the company said the review, overseen by its independent audit committee with help from a Big Four accounting firm that was not its auditor, "did not uncover any evidence that would substantiate the allegations," while pledging to keep cooperating with the SEC ([Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2020-10-07/chinese-video-streamer-says-internal-review-debunks-fraud-claims-101612574.html)).

The turnaround came through cost discipline rather than growth. In 2022 iQIYI booked its first annual profit — non-GAAP net profit of RMB 1.3 billion ($186.2 million) on revenue of RMB 29 billion — after cutting revenue costs by 19%, with membership revenue of RMB 17.7 billion making up about 60% of revenue and membership standing near 120 million ([TechNode](https://technode.com/2023/02/23/iqiyi-books-annual-profit-for-the-first-time-expects-high-quality-growth-ahead/)). Its rivals Tencent Video and Youku had yet to turn an annual operating profit ([TechNode](https://technode.com/2023/02/23/iqiyi-books-annual-profit-for-the-first-time-expects-high-quality-growth-ahead/)). Hit dramas kept coming, including *A Lifelong Journey*, *Love Between Fairy and Devil* and, in early 2023, *The Knockout*, which passed 300 million views ([TechNode](https://technode.com/2023/02/23/iqiyi-books-annual-profit-for-the-first-time-expects-high-quality-growth-ahead/)). In February 2023 the company also said it would build Baidu's Ernie Bot chatbot into its content search, promotion and novel-creation services ([TechNode](https://technode.com/2023/02/23/iqiyi-books-annual-profit-for-the-first-time-expects-high-quality-growth-ahead/)).

Profitability proved fragile. For fiscal 2024 iQIYI reported revenue of RMB 29.23 billion, down 8% from 2023, with membership revenue down 13% to RMB 17.76 billion and net income down 60% to RMB 764.1 million, though it held the top drama market share in the fourth quarter and built a mini-drama library of more than 10,000 titles ([iQIYI earnings release](https://www.stockwatch.com/News/Item/U-z9359955-U!IQ-20250218/U/IQ)). The third quarter of 2025 was weaker still: revenue of RMB 6.68 billion, down 8% year on year, an operating loss of RMB 121.8 million and a net loss of RMB 248.9 million, even as overseas operations grew and iQIYI held the top drama viewership share ([iQIYI earnings release](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/iqiyi-announces-third-quarter-2025-financial-results-2025-11-18)). The company's stated response is an IP-centred model — amplifying hit stories from screen to offline experiences and overseas markets, with AI reshaping content creation and consumption ([iQIYI earnings release](https://www.nasdaq.com/press-release/iqiyi-announces-third-quarter-2025-financial-results-2025-11-18)).
