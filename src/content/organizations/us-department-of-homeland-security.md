## Overview

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security is the federal cabinet department responsible for protecting the country against terrorism, securing its borders, enforcing immigration law, and responding to disasters ([U.S. Government Accountability Office](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-04-329r)). It was created by the Homeland Security Act of 2002, signed November 25, 2002, as the government's structural answer to the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks — the largest reorganization of the federal government since the Department of Defense was formed in 1947 ([EBSCO Research Starters](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/political-science/creation-department-homeland-security)). Tom Ridge, who had been running the White House's post-9/11 homeland-security office, became its first secretary in early 2003 ([EBSCO Research Starters](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/political-science/creation-department-homeland-security)).

The scale of the creation is what made it unusual: 22 separate agencies — including the Immigration and Naturalization Service, the Border Patrol, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) — were pulled out of their old departments and welded into one ([EBSCO Research Starters](http://ebsco.com/research-starters/political-science/creation-department-homeland-security)). The Government Accountability Office described it as the most substantial federal reorganization since the 1940s, established in January 2003 from 29 program functions transferred out of 10 legacy agencies ([U.S. Government Accountability Office](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-04-329r)). Today DHS is the third-largest department of the U.S. government, with a workforce of 260,000 employees across 22 components, including TSA, Customs and Border Protection, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, Immigration and Customs Enforcement, U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, FEMA, the Coast Guard and the Secret Service ([U.S. Department of Homeland Security](http://www.dhs.gov/topics/secretary-homeland-security)).

## 2026: a new secretary, then the grant fight

DHS began 2026 under Secretary Kristi Noem. On March 5, 2026, President Trump removed Noem and nominated Senator Markwayne Mullin of Oklahoma to succeed her ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/03/24/markwayne-mullin-confirmed-as-secretary-of-homeland-security/)). The Senate confirmed Mullin on March 23, 2026, by a 54-45 vote — 52 Republicans plus Democrats John Fetterman of Pennsylvania and Martin Heinrich of New Mexico voted yes, while Rand Paul of Kentucky was the only Republican to vote no ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/03/24/markwayne-mullin-confirmed-as-secretary-of-homeland-security/)). Mullin was sworn in the next day, March 24, 2026 ([U.S. Department of Homeland Security](http://www.dhs.gov/topics/secretary-homeland-security)). A former plumber and businessman who served in the House from 2013 to 2023 and in the Senate from 2023 to 2026, he arrived at the department just as it was about to pick its biggest 2026 legal fight ([Ballotpedia News](https://news.ballotpedia.org/2026/03/24/markwayne-mullin-confirmed-as-secretary-of-homeland-security/)).

On June 24, 2026, FEMA published funding opportunities making more than $1.5 billion available across seven preparedness grant programs — including more than $1 billion through the Fiscal Year 2026 Homeland Security Grant Program ([Federal Emergency Management Agency](https://www.fema.gov/press-release/20260624/fema-announces-15-billion-empower-communities-prevent-terrorism-and-protect)). The announcement told recipients of the State Homeland Security Program and the Urban Area Security Initiative to align their projects with "core national security priorities identified by the Secretary of Homeland Security" — and for FY 2026 those priorities included, for the first time, "protecting the integrity of American elections," alongside law-enforcement coordination, critical-infrastructure cybersecurity, border security and crowded-space protection ([Federal Emergency Management Agency](https://www.fema.gov/press-release/20260624/fema-announces-15-billion-empower-communities-prevent-terrorism-and-protect)). Applications opened June 24 and closed July 24, 2026 ([Federal Emergency Management Agency](https://www.fema.gov/press-release/20260624/fema-announces-15-billion-empower-communities-prevent-terrorism-and-protect)).

FEMA's own program page puts the FY 2026 Homeland Security Grant Program at **$1.064 billion** total: $394.3 million for the State Homeland Security Program, $584.3 million for the Urban Area Security Initiative, and $85.5 million for Operation Stonegarden, which funds border-area law-enforcement cooperation ([Federal Emergency Management Agency](https://www.fema.gov/grants/preparedness/homeland-security)).

## The blocked election conditions

Over the summer of 2026, DHS announced that it would withhold 20 percent of funding under grant programs tied to preparing for terrorist attacks and other threats until state and local governments adopted election-administration measures favored by the administration ([Reuters](https://wixx.com/2026/09/28/judge-blocks-trump-from-tying-anti-terrorism-grants-to-election-changes/)). A FEMA grant announcement listed the election-related requirements: verifying the citizenship of all registered voters and election workers; moving away from electronic voting systems that use barcodes or QR codes toward systems accepting hand-marked paper ballots; manually auditing at least 5 percent of ballots cast; and comparing voter-registration records against DHS's citizenship-verification system ([The Associated Press](https://www.dtnext.in/news/world/trump-cant-force-election-changes-by-blocking-counterterrorism-funds-to-states-judge-says); [Reuters](https://wixx.com/2026/09/28/judge-blocks-trump-from-tying-anti-terrorism-grants-to-election-changes/)).

That DHS citizenship-verification system is SAVE — the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements program run by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services. In November 2025, USCIS upgraded SAVE so states could verify voter citizenship using only the last four digits of a Social Security number, saying the change would help ensure "only U.S. citizens vote in federal elections"; by October 2025, state voting agencies had submitted more than 46 million voter-verification queries through the system, part of 205 million total status verifications that year versus 25 million in all of 2024 ([U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services](https://www.uscis.gov/newsroom/news-releases/uscis-enhances-voter-verification-systems)). Secretary Mullin said the new grant requirements would protect election systems from foreign interference and cyberattacks ([Reuters](https://wixx.com/2026/09/28/judge-blocks-trump-from-tying-anti-terrorism-grants-to-election-changes/)).

On September 28, 2026, U.S. District Judge [Amir Ali](/people/amir-ali/) of Washington, D.C. blocked the conditions in a 24-page opinion ([The Associated Press](https://www.dtnext.in/news/world/trump-cant-force-election-changes-by-blocking-counterterrorism-funds-to-states-judge-says)). Ali sided with the suing jurisdictions — Columbus, Ohio; [Nashville and Davidson County, Tennessee](/organizations/nashville-davidson-county-tennessee/); and El Paso and Harris counties in Texas — holding that the grant programs, totaling about $1.1 billion, "have existed for years" and "none of them were designed with election security specifically in mind" ([Reuters](https://wixx.com/2026/09/28/judge-blocks-trump-from-tying-anti-terrorism-grants-to-election-changes/); [The Associated Press](https://www.dtnext.in/news/world/trump-cant-force-election-changes-by-blocking-counterterrorism-funds-to-states-judge-says)). "Congress did not authorize the grant program to be used for the sort of election administration reforms FEMA demands here — changes that, whether sound or not as a matter of policy, are not advanced to prepare for or respond to 'acts of terrorism,'" Ali wrote ([Reuters](https://wixx.com/2026/09/28/judge-blocks-trump-from-tying-anti-terrorism-grants-to-election-changes/)). The ruling found that federal agencies lacked congressional authority to attach such conditions, and that the conditions failed the legal threshold requiring them to relate to the grants' underlying purpose ([The Associated Press](https://www.dtnext.in/news/world/trump-cant-force-election-changes-by-blocking-counterterrorism-funds-to-states-judge-says)).

A separate legal challenge to the same policy, filed by a coalition of mostly Democratic-governed states, was still pending when Ali ruled ([Reuters](https://wixx.com/2026/09/28/judge-blocks-trump-from-tying-anti-terrorism-grants-to-election-changes/)). Neither DHS nor FEMA, and neither DHS nor the Justice Department — which represents the federal government in court — responded to requests for comment on the decision ([The Associated Press](https://www.dtnext.in/news/world/trump-cant-force-election-changes-by-blocking-counterterrorism-funds-to-states-judge-says); [Reuters](https://wixx.com/2026/09/28/judge-blocks-trump-from-tying-anti-terrorism-grants-to-election-changes/)). Reuters noted that the decision marked another defeat for the administration's effort to expand federal authority over elections ahead of the November 3 midterm elections, and that the president's claims of widespread voting fraud have been contradicted by independent studies and audits ([Reuters](https://wixx.com/2026/09/28/judge-blocks-trump-from-tying-anti-terrorism-grants-to-election-changes/)).
