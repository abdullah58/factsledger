## Overview

Mastercard is one of the two global card networks — the other being [Visa](/organizations/visa/) — whose logo on a card represents the network that moves money from the cardholder's issuing bank to the merchant's bank ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2017/04/22/5-things-you-didnt-know-about-mastercard-inc.aspx?yptr=yahoo)). Like Visa, it began life as a bank consortium rather than a technology startup: in 1966 a group of California banks including Wells Fargo, United California Bank, Crocker National Bank and the Bank of California formed the Interbank Card Association (ICA) to compete with Bank of America's BankAmericard ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2017/04/22/5-things-you-didnt-know-about-mastercard-inc.aspx?yptr=yahoo)). The ICA introduced "Master Charge: The Interbank Card" in 1969, renamed it Mastercard in 1979, and stayed a bank cooperative until its 2006 IPO at $39 a share ([Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/investing/2017/04/22/5-things-you-didnt-know-about-mastercard-inc.aspx?yptr=yahoo)).

But the company's recent history reads less like a card network and more like a cybersecurity consolidator. Its largest acquisition ever was not a payments company: in September 2024 it agreed to buy threat-intelligence firm Recorded Future from Insight Partners for $2.65 billion ([Infosecurity Magazine](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/mastercard-acquires-recorded-future/)). That deal caps a five-year buying spree — RiskRecon, Finicity, Recorded Future — that has quietly turned Mastercard into a security-and-data business that happens to run a card network.

## The cybersecurity acquisition arc

The sequence began in December 2019, when Mastercard announced an agreement to acquire RiskRecon, "a leading provider of artificial intelligence and data analytics solutions" for protecting companies' cyber systems and infrastructure; the terms were not disclosed and the deal was expected to close in the first quarter of 2020 ([Mastercard](https://newsroom.mastercard.com/news/press/2019/december/mastercard-acquires-riskrecon-to-enhance-cybersecurity-capabilities/)).

Six months later came the open-banking move: Mastercard agreed to buy Salt Lake City-based Finicity, a real-time financial data aggregation service, for $825 million plus up to $160 million in earnouts — months after Visa had agreed to pay $5.3 billion for [Plaid](/organizations/plaid/), a deal Mastercard itself had previously invested in ([Finextra](https://www.finextra.com/newsarticle/36081/mastercard-to-buy-finicity-for-open-banking-push)). Mastercard said Finicity's APIs would streamline credit decisioning and improve account verification for banks and fintechs ([Finextra](https://www.finextra.com/newsarticle/36081/mastercard-to-buy-finicity-for-open-banking-push)).

Recorded Future is the capstone. One of the world's largest threat-intelligence companies, it counts more than 1,900 clients across 75 countries — including the governments of 45 countries and over half the Fortune 100 ([Infosecurity Magazine](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/mastercard-acquires-recorded-future/)). Insight Partners had bought it in 2019 for $780 million, tripling its money in the sale ([Infosecurity Magazine](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/mastercard-acquires-recorded-future/)). Mastercard said the deal complements its identity, fraud-prevention, real-time decisioning and cybersecurity services; the two already collaborated on an AI-supported service that alerts banks when a card is likely compromised, and since its launch the rate at which compromised cards were identified had doubled ([Infosecurity Magazine](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/mastercard-acquires-recorded-future/)).

## A duopoly, and regulators watching it

Mastercard and Visa jointly dominate the card networks — "Visa — and to a lesser extent Mastercard — continues to dominate the market," NPR reported, even though debit-card-issuing banks have been required since late 2011 to allow merchants to route transactions over multiple networks ([NPR](https://www.kdlg.org/as-heard-on-npr/2024-09-24/justice-department-accuses-visa-of-stifling-competition-in-the-debit-card-business)). In U.S. debit specifically, Mastercard is "a distant second" with less than a quarter of the market to Visa's more-than-60% share, according to the Justice Department's complaint ([Payments Dive](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/visa-loses-bid-to-toss-us-debit-lawsuit-DOJ-antitrust-payments-cards/751607/)). The DOJ's September 2024 suit alleges Visa illegally maintains a debit-network monopoly ([Payments Dive](https://www.paymentsdive.com/news/visa-loses-bid-to-toss-us-debit-lawsuit-DOJ-antitrust-payments-cards/751607/)) — and Mastercard has its own regulatory mark: NPR reported that "Mastercard was also accused of using illegal tactics to limit competition in the debit card market. The company settled a complaint brought by the [Federal Trade Commission](/organizations/federal-trade-commission/) last year" (2023) ([NPR](https://www.kdlg.org/as-heard-on-npr/2024-09-24/justice-department-accuses-visa-of-stifling-competition-in-the-debit-card-business)).

The future may look different from the rivalry. In September 2026, Mastercard joined Visa and [Stripe](/organizations/stripe/) as equal founding partners and investors in Open Standard, the company behind the Open USD stablecoin, with the five founding partners (also Coinbase and Shopify) committing more than $1 billion in liquidity ([CoinDesk](https://Www.coindesk.com/business/2026/09/24/open-usd-takes-on-tether-circle-with-a-different-stablecoin-model-that-s-building-money)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim Mastercard issues cards or extends credit — like Visa, it operates the network between banks; the "does not issue" language is sourced to Visa's filing, not Mastercard's, so the profile states only the network function that its sources support. It does not state that the Recorded Future deal has closed or faced regulatory conditions — the source anticipated a Q1 2025 close subject to review, and closure was not verified from a fully-read source. It does not claim the FTC settlement involved a monetary penalty. The 1966–2006 history rests on investment-press reporting (tier3) and is supporting detail. "Mastercard" here means only Mastercard Incorporated — and a travel Mastercard is not a thing, but for the avoidance of doubt: this is the payments network, nothing to do with immigration documents either.
