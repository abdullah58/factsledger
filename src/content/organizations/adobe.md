## Overview

Adobe was founded in 1982 by two Xerox PARC computer scientists, Charles Geschke and John Warnock, who quit the legendary research lab after Xerox refused to commercialize their work on electronic document production ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Their first product, PostScript — a programming language for describing printed pages — became the invisible engine of the desktop-publishing revolution of the late 1980s. From that beachhead Adobe assembled the creative professional's toolkit piece by piece: Illustrator for drawing, Photoshop — licensed from brothers Thomas and John Knoll in 1988 — for photo editing, and Acrobat, whose PDF format became the de facto standard for electronic documents once Adobe gave the Reader away free ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

## The garage, the creek, and Steve Jobs' 19.9%

Adobe's origin story has the classic Silicon Valley details — it was named for the creek behind Warnock's house — but the business mechanics were unusual. After Xerox told the pair it took "seven years" to bring a product to market, they raised $2.5 million from Hambrecht & Quist on a plan to sell complete turnkey publishing systems, then threw the plan out when customers told them what they actually wanted: just the software ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Steve Jobs came calling early — first trying to buy the whole company, then settling for a licensing deal in which Apple took a 19.9% stake at five times the original venture valuation, plus prepaid royalties, bringing Adobe about $3.5 million in total ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). The Apple LaserWriter, powered by PostScript, made desktop publishing a phenomenon — and Adobe's font-rendering technology, licensed from an underdog Linotype against the wishes of the dominant Compugraphic, gave it the type business too ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

Growth by acquisition followed the same pattern of buying the standard: the 2005 Macromedia purchase, which then-CEO Bruce Chizen summed up in a single word — "Flash" — gave Adobe the interactive web ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

## The $20 billion deal that cost $1 billion for nothing

By 2022, Adobe faced a threat it could not buy its way around with technology alone: Figma, the browser-based collaborative design platform that had won over a generation of product designers. That September Adobe agreed to acquire Figma for about $20 billion in cash and stock — one of the largest private-software takeovers ever attempted. Investors hated the price immediately: more than $30 billion of Adobe's market value evaporated when the deal was announced ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/technology/adobe-figma-terminate-20-bln-deal-2023-12-18/?outputType=chromeless)).

Then the regulators moved. The EU issued a formal antitrust complaint, and Britain's Competition and Markets Authority concluded the merger would harm innovation for software used by the vast majority of UK digital designers — noting that Adobe had refused to offer remedies, on the grounds that no fix preserving the deal's benefits would satisfy its concerns ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/technology/adobe-figma-terminate-20-bln-deal-2023-12-18/?outputType=chromeless)). Adobe's defense — that its only relevant product was Adobe XD, which had lost $25 million as a standalone app over three years — did not persuade them. On December 18, 2023, the two companies mutually terminated the agreement, stating there was "no clear path" to approval; CEO Shantanu Narayen said they "strongly disagree with the recent regulatory findings, but we believe it is in our respective best interests to move forward independently" ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/technology/adobe-figma-terminate-20-bln-deal-2023-12-18/?outputType=chromeless)). The walk-away price: a $1 billion cash termination fee to Figma — paid for a deal that never closed.

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not cover Adobe's 2013 Creative Cloud subscription transition or its later product and marketing-software history — the research pass focused on the founding arc and the Figma saga. It does not claim the Figma termination fee was the largest ever paid; only that it was $1 billion per Reuters. The "$30 billion wiped out" figure describes the market reaction at announcement as reported by Reuters, not a verified accounting of value destruction. The founding narrative rests substantially on co-founder Geschke's own 2008 Wharton interview — a primary account, but a participant's recollection rather than contemporaneous documentation.
