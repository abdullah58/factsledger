## Overview

The Bureau of Consumer Protection is the arm of the [Federal Trade Commission](/organizations/federal-trade-commission/) that does the suing — the office inside the FTC that investigates companies for unfair or deceptive practices, takes them to federal court, writes consumer protection rules, and sends money back to the people they cheated ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/about-ftc/bureaus-offices/bureau-consumer-protection)). Created on 1 July 1970, it is built around eight divisions and eight regional offices, and its director — Christopher Mufarrige since February 2025 — runs the FTC's entire consumer-protection program ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/about-ftc/bureaus-offices/bureau-consumer-protection)).

The Bureau's defining moment came in 2021, when the [U.S. Supreme Court](/organizations/us-supreme-court/) unanimously ruled in *AMG Capital Management v. FTC* that the law the Bureau had used for four decades to win money back for consumers did not actually authorize monetary relief ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/supreme-court-limits-ftc-authority-to-obtain-disgorgement-or-restitution-rejecting-decades-of-precedent)). The Bureau's director from June 2021 called the loss "devastating" — and spent the following years rebuilding the money-recovery toolkit from the FTC Act's remaining provisions, state partnerships, and a new wave of rulemaking ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)).

## Created in 1970: one director, one program

Before 1970, the FTC's consumer-protection work was scattered across four bureaus — Textiles and Furs, Industry Guidance, Deceptive Practices, and Restraint of Trade — and coordinating a single case could require meetings between bureaus with overlapping jurisdiction ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000.pdf)). A reorganization adopted in fiscal year 1970 swept them away: effective 1 July 1970, the Commission was divided into two principal operating bureaus, the Bureau of Competition and the Bureau of Consumer Protection, with the Bureau of Economics kept on as a staff group ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000.pdf)). The annual report's logic was blunt — "a single officer — for example, the Director of the Bureau of Consumer Protection — is now fully responsible for administering the Commission's program in any single area, whether the FTC proceeds by rulemaking or on a case-by-case basis" ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000.pdf)).

The same reorganization pushed enforcement out into the field. On 25 February 1970 the FTC's field offices were empowered to open preliminary investigations on their own, recommend complaints, try local cases and negotiate settlements, and the Commission began hiring Consumer Protection Specialists, non-lawyer investigators meant to detect consumer problems and educate people in disadvantaged areas ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000.pdf)).

Its legal remit is written into the Code of Federal Regulations. Under 16 CFR § 0.17, the Bureau enforces Section 5 of the FTC Act's ban on unfair or deceptive acts or practices "as well as numerous special statutes," by investigating alleged violations, recommending further steps, and prosecuting the actions the Commission authorizes — seeking injunctions in federal court, litigating before administrative law judges, negotiating settlements, starting rules or reports, and bringing civil-penalty proceedings ([Cornell Law Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/0.17)). It also educates consumers and businesses and cooperates with state, local and international agencies ([Cornell Law Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/0.17)).

## The 2021 ruling that took away its strongest tool

For roughly forty years, the Bureau's main way of getting money back to consumers ran through a single provision: Section 13(b) of the FTC Act, which lets the Commission go to federal court for a "permanent injunction" ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/supreme-court-limits-ftc-authority-to-obtain-disgorgement-or-restitution-rejecting-decades-of-precedent)). Courts had long read that phrase as also authorizing restitution and disgorgement — in 2019 alone the FTC invoked Section 13(b) in 49 federal-court complaints and obtained 81 permanent injunctions and orders, yielding $723.2 million in consumer redress or disgorgement ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/supreme-court-limits-ftc-authority-to-obtain-disgorgement-or-restitution-rejecting-decades-of-precedent)).

The test case was a payday lender. In 2012 the FTC sued AMG Capital Management and its owner Scott Tucker, alleging deceptive payday-lending practices, and won a $1.27 billion restitution and disgorgement judgment under Section 13(b) ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/supreme-court-limits-ftc-authority-to-obtain-disgorgement-or-restitution-rejecting-decades-of-precedent)). The Ninth Circuit affirmed on binding precedent, though two of its three judges doubted that precedent — as did similar precedent in at least eight other circuits ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/supreme-court-limits-ftc-authority-to-obtain-disgorgement-or-restitution-rejecting-decades-of-precedent)). On 22 April 2021 the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously reversed: Section 13(b) "refers only to injunctions," and reading it to allow monetary relief "would allow a small statutory tail to wag a very large dog" — Sections 5(l) and 19 provide limited monetary remedies, but only after the slower administrative process and a cease-and-desist order ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/supreme-court-limits-ftc-authority-to-obtain-disgorgement-or-restitution-rejecting-decades-of-precedent)). The Court added that the FTC remained "free to ask Congress to grant it further remedial authority" ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/supreme-court-limits-ftc-authority-to-obtain-disgorgement-or-restitution-rejecting-decades-of-precedent)).

Samuel Levine, who led the Bureau from June 2021, was unsparing about what the ruling meant in practice: "you cannot overstate the significance of AMG for the day-to-day work of the FTC, and in particular for the Bureau of Consumer Protection. This is an authority that we have used for decades, since the early 1980s, to recover billions of dollars for consumers, more money than our entire budget most years… in the years before AMG… 90 percent of the actions we brought where we relied on Section 13(b) to get money back to consumers" ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)). Losing it, he said, was "devastating," and restoring the authority through Congress was "absolutely urgent" — a position every commissioner agreed on across party lines ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)).

The Bureau adapted rather than stopped. It brought administrative litigation to recover under Section 19; partnered with states on privacy, fraud and car-dealer cases, and with cities and counties — suing the anonymous-messaging app NGL alongside the Los Angeles District Attorney — and filed joint cases with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (against TransUnion) and the Department of Justice (against TikTok) ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)). It revived the long-dormant penalty-offense authority and launched a wave of rulemakings — because after AMG, as Levine put it, "the only way we can generally get money back to consumers when we are going into federal court is when a rule has been violated" ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)). The Bureau kept returning "hundreds of millions of dollars to consumers" despite the ruling ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)).

## Landmark cases — and the machine that returns the money

Two 2019 settlements show the Bureau's range. In July 2019 Equifax agreed to pay at least $575 million — potentially up to $700 million — in a global settlement with the FTC, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and 50 states and territories over the 2017 breach that exposed the personal information of about 147 million people ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/07/equifax-pay-575-million-part-settlement-ftc-cfpb-states-related-2017-data-breach)). The FTC alleged Equifax had been warned in March 2017 about a critical vulnerability in its ACIS database, ordered the patch within 48 hours, then never followed up; hackers exploited the unpatched system, found administrative credentials stored in plain text, and operated undetected for months, stealing at least 147 million names and dates of birth, 145.5 million Social Security numbers and 209,000 payment-card numbers ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/07/equifax-pay-575-million-part-settlement-ftc-cfpb-states-related-2017-data-breach)).

The settlement put $300 million into a consumer fund (plus up to $125 million more if needed), $175 million to the states and $100 million to the CFPB, and required a comprehensive security program with biennial third-party assessments ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/07/equifax-pay-575-million-part-settlement-ftc-cfpb-states-related-2017-data-breach)). "Companies that profit from personal information have an extra responsibility to protect and secure that data," said Chairman Joe Simons ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/07/equifax-pay-575-million-part-settlement-ftc-cfpb-states-related-2017-data-breach)).

Three months earlier, the Bureau had settled with [Avant](/organizations/avant/), the online lender, for $3.85 million over deceptive and unfair loan servicing ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/04/online-lending-company-agrees-settle-ftc-charges-it-engaged-deceptive-unfair-loan-servicing)). The FTC alleged Avant withdrew money from consumers' accounts without authorization — one consumer's monthly payment was debited 11 times in a single day — falsely advertised that it accepted credit- and debit-card payments, gave inaccurate payoff quotes, and required borrowers to agree to recurring automatic bank debits as a condition of getting a loan ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/04/online-lending-company-agrees-settle-ftc-charges-it-engaged-deceptive-unfair-loan-servicing)). "We have alleged that Avant gave the run-around to consumers trying to repay their loans, because of systemic issues with the company's loan servicing platform," said Bureau Director Andrew Smith ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/04/online-lending-company-agrees-settle-ftc-charges-it-engaged-deceptive-unfair-loan-servicing)).

Winning judgments is only half the Bureau's job; the other half is getting the money to people. Between 1 July 2016 and 30 June 2017 the Bureau obtained 168 court orders for more than $12.72 billion, and its Office of Claims and Refunds directed mailings that produced nearly $320 million in refunds while supporting defendant-administered programs that delivered more than $6 billion — more than $6.4 billion in refunds in total, including the Volkswagen buyback program ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/bureau-consumer-protection-office-claims-refunds-annual-report-2017-consumer-refunds-effected-july/redressreportformattedforweb122117.pdf)). FTC-administered programs that year mailed $391.38 million to 6.28 million people, with a 72% first-round check-cashing rate; leftover money goes to the U.S. Treasury ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/bureau-consumer-protection-office-claims-refunds-annual-report-2017-consumer-refunds-effected-july/redressreportformattedforweb122117.pdf)). And because the FTC cannot bring criminal cases, the Bureau's Criminal Liaison Unit — created in 2002 — refers the "worst of the worst" offenders to prosecutors; its 2018–2022 referrals led to criminal charges against 107 new defendants, 145 convictions and 181 defendants sentenced, for a total of 746 years, an average of 51 months ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftcs-bureau-consumer-protection-issues-criminal-liaison-unit-report-detailing-efforts-ensure)).

## The Levine years: privacy, dark patterns and a rulemaking revival

Samuel Levine led the Bureau from June 2021, shortly after Lina Khan became FTC Chair, and his tenure was defined by the post-AMG rebuild ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)). The most visible shift was on privacy. Levine called the notice-and-consent model — companies posting privacy policies and consumers supposedly reading them — "a fiction" and "a failure," and the Bureau moved from deception theories to unfairness theories: sharing sensitive health or geolocation data for advertising, excessive data retention and sharing browsing data were alleged to be unfair in themselves ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)). The cases followed: five health-privacy actions banning companies including GoodRx, BetterHelp and Premom from sharing sensitive health data for advertising; two cases banning data brokers from sharing sensitive geolocation data, with a third in litigation; the largest children's-privacy action in FTC history, against Epic Games; and children's-privacy cases against [Amazon](/organizations/amazon/) Alexa and [Microsoft](/organizations/microsoft/) Xbox ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)).

The Bureau also pioneered a new remedy for the AI era. In at least half a dozen cases it won "algorithmic disgorgement" — formally data-product deletion — requiring companies that collected data illegally to delete not just the data but the models, algorithms and work product built from it, as in the Kurbo/Weight Watchers case ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)). And in the Rite Aid case, the Bureau banned the pharmacy chain from using AI facial recognition for five years after the technology improperly flagged women and people of color as shoplifters ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)).

On design, the Bureau's 2022 report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" catalogued manipulative interfaces — hidden fees, hard-to-cancel subscriptions, misleading claims in bold with disclaimers in light grey — and the cases followed: Credit Karma, the first case explicitly describing dark patterns; then Publishers Clearing House, Amazon, Vonage (a $100 million settlement in 2022) and [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/) ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)). Levine was careful about the legal point: "Dark patterns as such are not illegal… They are illegal if they deceive people… if they cause injury… if they make it impossible for people to cancel online subscriptions because that violates ROSCA" ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)). The rulemaking revival ran alongside: the Impersonator Rule finalized in 2024, a fake-reviews rule, a junk-fees rule proposed in fall 2023, a click-to-cancel rule, and a strengthened children's-privacy rule proposed in December 2023 ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)).

The directorship changed hands in February 2025, when Chairman Andrew N. Ferguson appointed Christopher Mufarrige — previously a senior adviser at the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in the first Trump administration and most recently Commissioner Melissa Holyoak's chief of staff — on a 4-0 Commission vote ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-chairman-ferguson-appoints-christopher-mufarrige-director-bureau-consumer-protection)). Analysts expect him to make fraud a key priority and to pivot away from the Biden-era AI enforcement and algorithmic-discrimination theories — an expectation, not yet a record ([Wilson Sonsini](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/ftc-appoints-new-bureau-directors-what-to-expect-from-directors-christopher-mufarrige-and-daniel-guarnera.html)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the FTC Bureau of Consumer Protection?**
It is one of the three bureaus of the Federal Trade Commission, created on 1 July 1970, and the agency's consumer-protection enforcement arm ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/about-ftc/bureaus-offices/bureau-consumer-protection)). It investigates and sues companies for unfair or deceptive practices, writes and enforces consumer protection rules, collects consumer reports, and educates consumers and businesses about their rights and responsibilities ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/about-ftc/bureaus-offices)).

**What happened to the Bureau's power to get money back for consumers?**
In April 2021 the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously held in *AMG Capital Management v. FTC* that Section 13(b) of the FTC Act authorizes only injunctions, not restitution or disgorgement ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/publications/supreme-court-limits-ftc-authority-to-obtain-disgorgement-or-restitution-rejecting-decades-of-precedent)). About 90% of the Bureau's money-back actions had relied on that provision, so the Bureau rebuilt its toolkit around administrative proceedings, state partnerships, penalty-offense authority and new rules ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/magazine/2024-fall/interview-samuel-levine/)).

**How does the Bureau return money to consumers?**
Its Office of Claims and Refunds turns court orders into payments: in fiscal 2016–17 the Bureau obtained 168 court orders for more than $12.72 billion and FTC cases produced more than $6.4 billion in refunds, with 6.28 million people receiving mailed checks ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/bureau-consumer-protection-office-claims-refunds-annual-report-2017-consumer-refunds-effected-july/redressreportformattedforweb122117.pdf)). Money left over, or too small to distribute meaningfully, goes to the U.S. Treasury ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/bureau-consumer-protection-office-claims-refunds-annual-report-2017-consumer-refunds-effected-july/redressreportformattedforweb122117.pdf)).

**Can the Bureau send people to prison?**
No — the FTC has no criminal authority, so the Bureau's Criminal Liaison Unit, created in 2002, refers the worst offenders to prosecutors at U.S. Attorneys' Offices and the Department of Justice ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftcs-bureau-consumer-protection-issues-criminal-liaison-unit-report-detailing-efforts-ensure)). Its 2018–2022 referrals led to 145 convictions and 181 defendants sentenced, for a total of 746 years in prison ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftcs-bureau-consumer-protection-issues-criminal-liaison-unit-report-detailing-efforts-ensure)).

**Is the Bureau the same as the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau?**
No. The Bureau of Consumer Protection is one of the FTC's three bureaus, created in 1970 inside the FTC ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-12955_01_00-003-0011-0000.pdf)), while the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau is a separate agency — the two appear as distinct parties in joint settlements such as Equifax, alongside 50 states and territories ([Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/07/equifax-pay-575-million-part-settlement-ftc-cfpb-states-related-2017-data-breach)).
