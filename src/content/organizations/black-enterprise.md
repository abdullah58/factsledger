## Overview

Black Enterprise is an American business magazine founded in August 1970 by Earl G. Graves Sr. in [New York City](/places/new-york-city/), created to cover Black entrepreneurship, careers and wealth-building for African American readers. It is best known for the BE 100s, its annual ranking of the largest Black-owned businesses, first published in June 1973, and it is now a digital-first multimedia company led by Graves's son, Earl "Butch" Graves Jr. ([BlackPast.org](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-enterprise-1970/); [Black Enterprise](https://www.blackenterprise.com/black-enterprise-lists-nations-most-successful-black-businesses-1973/)).

Graves launched it because the leading Black-targeted magazines of the day covered fashion and beauty, not business: he wanted a publication that would educate readers in business and economic development and inspire them to thrive professionally ([BlackPast.org](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-enterprise-1970/)). The magazine turned a profit by its tenth issue, and its first board of advisers included the civil rights and political figures Shirley Chisholm, John Lewis and Julian Bond ([BlackPast.org](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-enterprise-1970/)). Over five decades it grew from a single magazine into a multimedia company spanning print, digital, broadcast television and live events, and it remains Black-owned and family-led ([JPMorganChase](https://www.jpmorgan.com/insights/corporate-responsibility/diversity-opportunity-and-inclusion/conversation-with-earl-butch-graves-president-and-ceo-of-black-enterprise)).

## From a newsletter idea to a newsstand magazine

The magazine began as a response to an information gap Graves spotted on the Small Business Administration advisory board in 1968: Black businesses had nowhere to find advice on economic development, so he created a newsletter, and in 1970, at the urging of SBA Director Howard J. Samuels, expanded it into a magazine whose first issue appeared that August ([Global Business Hall of Fame](https://www.businesshalloffame.org/earl-g-graves-sr)). Brooklyn-born Graves had been an administrative assistant to Senator Robert F. Kennedy from 1965 until Kennedy's assassination in 1968 left him unemployed ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/economics-magazines/graves-earl-g); [JPMorganChase](https://www.jpmorgan.com/insights/corporate-responsibility/diversity-opportunity-and-inclusion/conversation-with-earl-butch-graves-president-and-ceo-of-black-enterprise)).

He pitched the idea to bank after bank; only Chase said yes, his son recalled ([JPMorganChase](https://www.jpmorgan.com/insights/corporate-responsibility/diversity-opportunity-and-inclusion/conversation-with-earl-butch-graves-president-and-ceo-of-black-enterprise)). To build an audience, Graves sent free copies to Black businesspeople, ministers, politicians and corporate offices ([Global Business Hall of Fame](https://www.businesshalloffame.org/earl-g-graves-sr)). Doubters said too few Black people cared about business, and white advertisers were the hardest fight: cigarette and liquor brands advertised readily, while computer and brokerage firms ignored Black consumers ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/economics-magazines/graves-earl-g)). Until then, John Johnson's *Ebony* and *Jet* were the only successful Black-targeted magazines in the [United States](/places/united-states/), and *Essence* was still only an idea ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/economics-magazines/graves-earl-g)).

## The BE 100s: making Black business visible

Black Enterprise's most consequential editorial decision came three years in. The first BE 100s list, called the "Top 100s," appeared in the June 1973 issue, ranking the largest Black-owned businesses in the country; until then, as Graves Jr. wrote in 2017, "the financial performance of African American firms had not been tracked by the media or government on an annual basis, nor were our entrepreneurial journeys widely chronicled" ([Black Enterprise](https://www.blackenterprise.com/black-enterprise-lists-nations-most-successful-black-businesses-1973/)). Motown topped the first list, led by Barry Gordy with 1972 sales of $40 million and artists including Stevie Wonder, Diana Ross, Marvin Gaye and the Jackson Five, while Johnson Publishing, publisher of *Ebony* and *Jet*, ranked second; President Richard Nixon sent Graves a letter of congratulations on compiling the list ([Black Enterprise](https://www.blackenterprise.com/black-enterprise-lists-nations-most-successful-black-businesses-1973/)).

When the first BE 100s list appeared in June 1973, its companies had combined sales of just $473.4 million; by 2018 the listed companies' combined revenues exceeded $30 billion ([Black Enterprise](https://www.blackenterprise.com/black-enterprise-lists-nations-most-successful-black-businesses-1973/)) — a more than sixty-fold expansion that turned a magazine ranking into the standard measure of Black business scale in America. The list also became a record of who endured: at least three firms — H.J. Russell & Co., Black Enterprise itself and Urban One, formerly Radio One — have appeared on it for decades, and the magazine built companion lists including the Most Powerful Women in Business and the Top 40 Business Leaders Under 40 ([Black Enterprise](https://www.blackenterprise.com/black-enterprise-lists-nations-most-successful-black-businesses-1973/); [BlackPast.org](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-enterprise-1970/)).

## Succession: father to son

The company stayed a family business by design. Graves Jr., who holds a bachelor's degree in economics from Yale University and an MBA from Harvard University, joined the company in 1988, became president and chief operating officer in 1998, and was named president and chief executive officer in January 2006, with his father continuing as chairman and publisher ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/economics-magazines/graves-earl-g); [BlackPast.org](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-enterprise-1970/)). By its 20th anniversary in 1990 the magazine had a circulation of 230,000 and annual revenues of more than $15 million, and by the early 2000s its print circulation had reached half a million, held up alongside *Forbes*, *Money* and *Fortune* ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/economics-magazines/graves-earl-g); [Global Business Hall of Fame](https://www.businesshalloffame.org/earl-g-graves-sr)). Graves Sr. died on 6 April 2020, aged 85, after a long battle with Alzheimer's disease, as the magazine marked its 50th anniversary ([BlackPast.org](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-enterprise-1970/)).

## The turn to digital and events

Under Graves Jr. the business diversified beyond the page. When advertisers doubted the audience in the late 1980s, the company turned to professional events so people could "see, feel and touch an audience they did not have access to otherwise" — events that Graves Jr. says are now "the tail that wagged the dog" — and it ran a private-equity business investing in established minority-owned companies ([JPMorganChase](https://www.jpmorgan.com/insights/corporate-responsibility/diversity-opportunity-and-inclusion/conversation-with-earl-butch-graves-president-and-ceo-of-black-enterprise)). In 2013 the print edition was cut from 12 to 10 issues a year, with the savings shifted to digital, "which has taken over from the print platform as a source of breaking news," while print refocused on evergreen advice, exclusive lists and profiles ([Talking Biz News](https://talkingbiznews.com/they-talk-biz-news/black-enterprise-to-cut-two-issues-per-year/)). By its 50th year in 2020 it was a $15 million multimedia company with six million unique visitors to its digital platforms ([JPMorganChase](https://www.jpmorgan.com/insights/corporate-responsibility/diversity-opportunity-and-inclusion/conversation-with-earl-butch-graves-president-and-ceo-of-black-enterprise)). Graves Jr. has argued that scale, not just access to capital, is the biggest challenge facing Black entrepreneurs: 96 percent of Black-owned businesses are sole proprietorships ([JPMorganChase](https://www.jpmorgan.com/insights/corporate-responsibility/diversity-opportunity-and-inclusion/conversation-with-earl-butch-graves-president-and-ceo-of-black-enterprise)).

## Black Enterprise, Billboard and the business of Black culture

Black Enterprise's remit was never business in the narrow sense, and its coverage of music shows why. In April 2025 it reported Billboard's conclusion that [Nicki Minaj](/people/nicki-minaj/) is the best female rapper of all time, noting that Minaj became the first female rapper with more than 100 Billboard Hot 100 hits in 2018, holds 149 records on the chart, and that her Pink Friday 2 World Tour is the highest-grossing tour by a female rapper ([Black Enterprise](https://www.blackenterprise.com/nicki-minaj-best-female-rapper-of-all-time/)). Billboard's top ten ran from Minaj through Missy Elliott, Lauryn Hill, Lil Kim and Queen Latifah to MC Lyte, Salt-N-Pepa, Roxanne Shante, Eve and Foxy Brown — and Black Enterprise noted that Billboard didn't share its criteria ([Black Enterprise](https://www.blackenterprise.com/nicki-minaj-best-female-rapper-of-all-time/)).

The connection runs both ways. Music was Black business from the magazine's first ranking: Motown topped the inaugural BE 100s in 1973, and the [BET](/organizations/bet/) cable network founded by Robert L. Johnson later appeared on the same lists ([Black Enterprise](https://www.blackenterprise.com/black-enterprise-lists-nations-most-successful-black-businesses-1973/); [BlackPast.org](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-enterprise-1970/)). That is why Billboard's own FactsLedger profile cites Black Enterprise as a source: a magazine that has spent fifty years measuring Black commercial achievement is also a primary witness to Black music's commercial milestones.

## Frequently asked questions

**When was Black Enterprise founded?**
Black Enterprise was founded in 1970 by Earl G. Graves Sr., with the first issue published in August of that year. The idea began in 1969, growing out of a newsletter Graves conceived while serving on the Small Business Administration advisory board. The magazine turned a profit by its tenth issue. ([BlackPast.org](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-enterprise-1970/); [Global Business Hall of Fame](https://www.businesshalloffame.org/earl-g-graves-sr))

**Who founded Black Enterprise?**
Earl G. Graves Sr., a Brooklyn-born Morgan State University graduate who had been an administrative assistant to Senator Robert F. Kennedy until Kennedy's assassination in 1968. He ran the magazine for five decades, published a New York Times bestselling business book in 1997, and died on 6 April 2020 aged 85. ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/economics-magazines/graves-earl-g); [BlackPast.org](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-enterprise-1970/))

**What is the BE 100s?**
The BE 100s is Black Enterprise's annual ranking of the largest Black-owned businesses in the United States, first published as the "Top 100s" in June 1973. The first list's companies had combined sales of $473.4 million; by 2018 the listed companies' combined revenues exceeded $30 billion. Motown topped the first list. ([Black Enterprise](https://www.blackenterprise.com/black-enterprise-lists-nations-most-successful-black-businesses-1973/))

**Is Black Enterprise still in print?**
The magazine shifted decisively toward digital: in 2013 it cut print from 12 to 10 issues a year, saying digital had taken over as its source of breaking news, and the Global Business Hall of Fame notes it now "lives on in digital format online." No 2026 print schedule was verified, so this profile does not state one. ([Talking Biz News](https://talkingbiznews.com/they-talk-biz-news/black-enterprise-to-cut-two-issues-per-year/); [Global Business Hall of Fame](https://www.businesshalloffame.org/earl-g-graves-sr))
