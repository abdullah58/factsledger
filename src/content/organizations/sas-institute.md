## Overview

SAS Institute — pronounced "sass," and unrelated to the Scandinavian airline — is one of the largest independent software companies in the world, and possibly the most successful one to have ignored every rule of Silicon Valley: it never took venture capital, never went public, and never sold ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/sas-institute-inc)). Founded and incorporated in 1976 by North Carolina State University professor James Goodnight and fellow academic John Sall, it grew out of a statistical-analysis software package the two had built for their own research — software so popular with other universities that it outgrew the campus ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/sas-institute-inc)).

## Kicked out of the university

The founding story is usually told as a joke with a point: "Eventually, our fledgling operation grew too big to run out of our offices at State, and they invited us to leave," Goodnight later said — so the team moved across Hillsborough Street, and that is how it all started ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/sas-institute-inc)). The software, dubbed the Statistical Analysis System, had begun in NC State's agricultural department analyzing the effects of soil, weather, and seed varieties on crop yields. Within 18 years it became a modular information-delivery system used by 98% of the Fortune 100, with three million users in 120 countries and 3,000 employees by 1994 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/sas-institute-inc)).

The growth was relentless and entirely self-funded: 1993 revenue of $420.3 million marked the company's 17th consecutive year of double-digit growth, and by 1999 sales exceeded $1 billion — all without public markets ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/sas-institute-inc)).

## The anti-Silicon Valley playbook

Three choices defined SAS. First, research over marketing: in 1993 the company reinvested a record $143 million — 34% of revenue — into R&D, its eighth consecutive year leading the software industry by that percentage, while the top 100 software firms averaged under 20% ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/sas-institute-inc)). Second, licensing over selling: SAS leased rather than sold its software on annual terms, its salespeople earned no commission so they would "concentrate on finding the best way to solve customers' problems," and renewal rates did the talking — 95% of Fortune 500 licensees renewed in 1993 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/sas-institute-inc)).

Third, the campus. From its Cary, North Carolina headquarters near Research Triangle Park, SAS built an on-site health care center (1983), recreation and fitness facilities, two Montessori child care centers, and programs for employees caring for aging relatives — earning repeated spots on best-employer lists, including Fortune's #2 in America in 2000 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/sas-institute-inc)). The payoff was measurable: average annual turnover of 3% against a 22% industry average ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/social-sciences-and-law/economics-business-and-labor/businesses-and-occupations/sas-institute-inc)). Staying private through the tech bubble and bust, the company argued, was what made all of it possible.
