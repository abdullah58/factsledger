## Overview

The UN Security Council is the United Nations body with primary responsibility for maintaining international peace and security — and the only UN organ whose decisions every member state is obligated to obey ([United Nations](https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/en)). It has 15 members, each with one vote, and its presidency rotates monthly among them; France held the chair in September 2026 ([United Nations](https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/en)). When it determines that a threat to the peace exists, it can call for peaceful settlement, impose sanctions, or even authorize the use of force ([United Nations](https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/en)).

Its most consequential feature is also its most criticized: five permanent members — China, France, the Russian Federation (which succeeded the Soviet Union's seat in 1990), the United Kingdom, and the United States — each hold a "right to veto," meaning a single negative vote from any one of them kills a substantive resolution even if the other fourteen members support it ([United Nations](https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/en/content/voting-system)). A permanent member that dislikes a resolution but does not want to block it outright can abstain, letting the measure pass if it still secures nine affirmative votes ([United Nations](https://main.un.org/securitycouncil/en/content/voting-system)).

## Two AI briefings, three years apart

The Council's engagement with artificial intelligence began on July 18, 2023, when it held its first-ever meeting on AI risks ([Reuters](https://gmanetwork.com/news/scitech/content/876237/un-security-council-meets-for-first-time-on-ai-risks/story/)). Britain's Foreign Secretary James Cleverly chaired the session under the UK's July presidency, telling the chamber that AI would "fundamentally alter every aspect of human life" and that the world urgently needed to shape global governance of the technology "because AI knows no borders" ([Reuters](https://gmanetwork.com/news/scitech/content/876237/un-security-council-meets-for-first-time-on-ai-risks/story/)). The 15 members were briefed by Secretary-General Antonio Guterres, [Anthropic](/organizations/anthropic/) co-founder Jack Clark, and Professor Zeng Yi of the China-UK Research Center for AI Ethics and Governance ([Reuters](https://gmanetwork.com/news/scitech/content/876237/un-security-council-meets-for-first-time-on-ai-risks/story/)). China called AI a technology that must not become a "runaway horse," the United States warned against its use to censor or repress people, and Guterres backed calls for a new UN body to govern AI, modeled on the International Atomic Energy Agency ([Reuters](https://gmanetwork.com/news/scitech/content/876237/un-security-council-meets-for-first-time-on-ai-risks/story/)).

Three years later, the industry came back to the same chamber with a sharper warning. On September 23, 2026, [OpenAI](/organizations/openai/) CEO [Sam Altman](/people/sam-altman/) briefed the Council in person while Anthropic CEO [Dario Amodei](/people/dario-amodei/) joined by video, and both told the world's top security body that frontier AI needed international standards and cooperation ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/23/tech/altman-amodei-ai-safety-un-security-council?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). "I believe that this is the most important global security issue facing the world today," Amodei said, warning that "AI could be a risk to humanity as a whole" if mismanaged ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/23/tech/altman-amodei-ai-safety-un-security-council?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)).

Amodei put three concrete ideas before the Council: narrow global agreements such as a ban on using AI to build biological weapons, evaluation and verification systems so countries could check one another's commitments, and common global standards for testing AI models plus a notification system for AI security incidents ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/23/tech/altman-amodei-ai-safety-un-security-council?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). He pledged that Anthropic would "slow down as much as necessary in order to make sure that every successive AI technology that we release is actually safe" ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/23/tech/altman-amodei-ai-safety-un-security-council?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). Altman, for his part, urged international standards for "measuring capabilities, assessing risks, determining whether safeguards are sufficient and preserving meaningful human oversight," plus fast incident reporting and secure government-to-government channels on emerging threats — while cautioning against both the "trap of doomerism" and the "trap of blind optimism" ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/23/tech/altman-amodei-ai-safety-un-security-council?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)).

The session produced no written agreements; an OpenAI official framed it beforehand as a chance to surface a "kernel of an idea in order to spark this conversation" ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/23/tech/altman-amodei-ai-safety-un-security-council?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)). Notably, the two rivals agreed on the need for cooperation even as their prescriptions differed — Altman emphasized standards and reporting, Amodei a slowdown pledge plus a concrete bioweapons ban ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/23/tech/altman-amodei-ai-safety-un-security-council?cid=external-feeds_iluminar_meta)).

## Why the Council matters for AI

The reason AI executives keep walking into this particular chamber is structural. The Council is the one place in the international system where a decision can become binding international law — the reason Guterres reached for the IAEA analogy back in 2023 ([Reuters](https://gmanetwork.com/news/scitech/content/876237/un-security-council-meets-for-first-time-on-ai-risks/story/)). It is also the place where such ambitions most visibly stall: any permanent member can veto, which is why AI governance debates at the UN unfold in the shadow of great-power rivalry rather than above it.
