## Overview

The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) is an international financial institution owned by member central banks, based in Basel, Switzerland, whose mission is to support central banks' pursuit of monetary and financial stability through international cooperation and to act as a bank for central banks ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/index.htm)). Established in 1930, it is the oldest international financial institution in the world.

Its 63 member central banks and monetary authorities represent countries accounting for about 95% of world GDP ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/member_cb.htm)). It is often called the central bankers' central bank — though it sets no monetary policy itself.

The BIS works through meetings, programmes and the Basel Process: hosting the international groups that pursue global financial stability, providing banking services to central banks, and publishing the research that frames their debates ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/member_cb.htm)). Its most consequential offspring is the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, the primary global standard setter for the prudential regulation of banks, whose 45 members come from 28 jurisdictions ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/bcbs/)).

## From reparations to the central bankers' bank

The BIS was created in 1930 at the Hague Conference, by a convention between Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Japan and the United Kingdom on one side and Switzerland on the other, and it opened in Basel on 17 May that year to settle German First World War reparation payments under the Young Plan ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/history.htm)). The reparations job quickly became obsolete, and the institution reinvented itself as a forum for central-bank cooperation. During the Second World War it shipped gold from Europe to the safety of New York, mostly on behalf of European central banks ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/history.htm)). After the war it acted as technical agent for the European Payments Union, which restored currency convertibility in Europe in 1958 ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/history.htm)). In February 1999 the Financial Stability Forum was created under its auspices to coordinate national financial authorities; it became the Financial Stability Board in 2009 ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/history.htm)).

## The Basel standards

The Basel Committee's first landmark was the Capital Accord of July 1988, which set a minimum ratio of capital to risk-weighted assets of 8%, to be implemented by the end of 1992 ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/bcbs/history.htm)). Basel II followed in June 2004, a revised framework built on three pillars — minimum capital requirements, supervisory review, and market discipline through disclosure ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/bcbs/history.htm)). The 2007–09 financial crisis exposed the framework's gaps, and Basel III was announced in September 2010 and endorsed at the G20 summit in Seoul that November, with most reforms phased in between 2013 and 2019 and the final post-crisis reforms completed in 2017 ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/bcbs/history.htm)). The Committee's power is standard-setting, not enforcement: it monitors implementation by member countries, while national authorities write the rules into law ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/bcbs/)). That distinction matters — the BIS hosts the process, but no Basel standard binds a bank until its own regulator adopts it.

| Framework | Agreed | Core content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basel I (Capital Accord) | July 1988 | 8% minimum capital to risk-weighted assets, by end-1992 |
| Basel II | June 2004 | Three pillars: capital requirements, supervisory review, disclosure |
| Basel III | Sept 2010 (G20 endorsement Nov 2010) | Stricter capital quality, leverage ratio, liquidity ratios (LCR, NSFR), buffers |
| Basel III final reforms | 2017 | Revised credit, CVA and operational-risk calculations, output floor |

## A bank, a data hub and a tokenisation lab

The BIS is also a working bank: in the 2024/25 financial year it made a net profit of SDR 843.7 million (about $1.2 billion) and record total comprehensive income of SDR 3.4 billion, an "exceptionally strong year" in Agustín Carstens's final year as General Manager ([Central Banking](https://www.centralbanking.com/central-banks/governance/financial-reporting/7973200/bis-reports-record-income-as-carstens-steps-down)). General Manager Pablo Hernández de Cos warned in the June 2026 Annual Economic Report of rising public debt, fragile core bond markets and AI-driven overinvestment creating a "new fiscal-financial stability nexus", urging policymakers: "Policymakers must act now. Delay will only make the necessary adjustments more costly" ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/global-markets-bis-pix-2026-06-28/)). Its data also shapes private markets: Bloomberg's 2021 report on BlackRock joining [Axoni](/organizations/axoni/)'s Veris cited BIS data putting the global equity-linked forwards and swaps market at about $3.6 trillion ([Bloomberg](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/crypto/blackrock-joins-blockchain-platform-axoni-for-equity-swap-trades)). Its Innovation Hub now tests tokenisation: Project Agorá, eight central banks and over 40 financial institutions, ran July 2026 real-value tests in which 28 institutions settled about CHF 800,000 on a tokenised platform ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/fmis/agora.htm)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the Bank for International Settlements?**
The BIS is an international financial institution owned by 63 member central banks, based in Basel, Switzerland since 1930, whose mission is to foster monetary and financial cooperation and serve as a bank for central banks ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/member_cb.htm)). It hosts the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision and provides banking services to central banks ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/bcbs/)).

**Why is it called the central bankers' central bank?**
Because its members and customers are central banks themselves: its capital is held by central banks only, and it provides them with a forum for dialogue, banking services and policy analysis ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/member_cb.htm)). It does not serve commercial banks, companies or individuals, and it sets no monetary policy of its own.

**What are the Basel Accords?**
They are the global bank-capital standards produced by the Basel Committee, which the BIS hosts: Basel I (1988) set an 8% capital ratio, Basel II (2004) added supervisory review and disclosure, and Basel III (2010, finalised 2017) added stricter capital, leverage and liquidity rules ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/bcbs/history.htm)). National regulators, not the BIS, enforce them.

**What is Project Agorá?**
Project Agorá is a BIS Innovation Hub collaboration testing a tokenised, multi-currency platform for wholesale cross-border payments, bringing together eight central banks and over 40 financial institutions ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/fmis/agora.htm)). In July 2026 it ran real-value tests worth about CHF 800,000, but it is a prototype for evaluation, not a finished product ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/about/bisih/topics/fmis/agora.htm)).
