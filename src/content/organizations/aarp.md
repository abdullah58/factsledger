## Overview

AARP is usually described as a membership club for older Americans — nearly 38 million strong, founded in 1958, a nonprofit lobby for seniors. The description is accurate and incomplete. The more complete picture starts with a 2024 financial restructuring, disclosed in AARP's own statements and surfaced in 2025 reporting: the organization swapped the monthly royalty payments it had collected from UnitedHealth Group for a single lump-sum advance of just over $9 billion, an exclusive license for the nation's largest health insurer to market insurance under the AARP name ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/op-eds/4648693/aarp-membership-dues-unitedhealth-group/)). The figure is hard to overstate — it is roughly 31 times what AARP collects each year in member dues and more than four times its total operating revenues for 2024 ([DC Journal](https://dcjournal.com/aarps-9-billion-reasons-to-side-with-unitedhealth-and-make-obamacare-subsidies-permanent/)). When America's loudest voice for seniors sits on a nine-billion-dollar advance from one insurer, the question of whom it serves is not rhetorical — it is the profile.

## The brand-licensing business

AARP's own telling of its finances, via its 2023 annual report, puts revenue at $1.85 billion across advertising from its publications and royalties for licensing its name and logo, and describes membership fees as the most significant source of revenue ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/aarp.asp)). But an analysis of the organization's Form 990 filing by the nonprofit American Commitment tells a different story: AARP's most recently filed return shows it paid more than $135 million *more* in employee compensation than the organization received in membership dues — a gap no membership club could survive, and one it closes with "royalty fees" for the use of its logo and intellectual property ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/op-eds/4648693/aarp-membership-dues-unitedhealth-group/)). Those fees have grown every single year for a quarter-century, totaling nearly $17.6 billion, with an estimated $10.8 billion since 2007 coming from UnitedHealth alone — figures that do not even include the $9 billion advance, which AARP will recognize as future income ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/op-eds/4648693/aarp-membership-dues-unitedhealth-group/)).

The arrangement, critics argue, can set AARP's financial interests against its members': on Medicare supplemental (Medigap) insurance, AARP receives a percentage of the underlying plan premium, which gives the organization a financial incentive for higher premiums — it makes more money when seniors pay more out of pocket ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/op-eds/4648693/aarp-membership-dues-unitedhealth-group/)). None of this is secret; it is in the filings. What is unusual is how rarely it appears in the organization's public image.

## The 2025 premium fight — and the awkward advocate

The tension became hardest to ignore in 2025. Medicare Part D, the prescription-drug program AARP itself fought hard to create, was in crisis: the national average monthly premium for standalone Part D plans had risen 57% across plan years 2022 through 2025, and the average number of plans offered per state had fallen by more than half, from 29 in 2021 to just 14 in 2025 ([AMAC](https://amac.us/newsline/politics/newsline/politics/biden-harris-admins-medicare-shock-for-seniors/)). To blunt the sticker shock before the 2024 election, the Biden administration spent more than $5 billion on a "demonstration project" that paid insurers extra to cap 2025 premium increases at $35 a month — a $15 reduction in the base beneficiary premium plus narrowed risk corridors, with the Congressional Budget Office estimating the demo would cost $7 billion in the next year alone and run for two more ([AMAC](https://amac.us/newsline/politics/newsline/politics/biden-harris-admins-medicare-shock-for-seniors/)).

AARP's position in that fight was not a passive one. During the fall 2025 government-shutdown and continuing-resolution debate, the organization lobbied actively to make the temporary COVID-era Affordable Care Act premium subsidies permanent — a policy its critics estimated would cost taxpayers nearly $350 billion while funneling billions more to large health insurers, including UnitedHealth ([DC Journal](https://dcjournal.com/aarps-9-billion-reasons-to-side-with-unitedhealth-and-make-obamacare-subsidies-permanent/)). The optics were the story: the senior lobby pushing a half-trillion-dollar subsidy extension while holding a $9 billion advance from the insurer that would benefit most. Critics also noted that UnitedHealth was under a Department of Justice criminal investigation for allegedly overbilling Medicare, while AARP had not publicly commented on the investigation despite lending its brand to the company's Medicare policies ([DC Journal](https://dcjournal.com/aarps-9-billion-reasons-to-side-with-unitedhealth-and-make-obamacare-subsidies-permanent/)). The charge from the organization's critics was direct: with nine billion reasons to side with UnitedHealth, AARP's advocacy was serving corporate interests rather than America's seniors ([DC Journal](https://dcjournal.com/aarps-9-billion-reasons-to-side-with-unitedhealth-and-make-obamacare-subsidies-permanent/)).

## What AARP is, on paper

Beneath the royalty business sits a real organization with a real history. AARP — originally the American Association of Retired Persons — was founded in 1958 by retired educator Dr. Ethel Percy Andrus, and formally shortened its name to AARP in 1999 to reflect members who were still working ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/aarp.asp)). It is a nonprofit, nonpartisan association reporting nearly 38 million members, registered as a 501(c)(4), which permits it to engage in lobbying — and it lobbies hard, with a strong presence in Washington and in state capitals ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/aarp.asp)). Membership is open to anyone 18 or older, though the mission centers on people 50 and above; the organization does not endorse candidates or contribute to campaigns, and describes its mission as helping people choose how they live as they age ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/aarp.asp)).

The structure is a hybrid by design. Alongside the 501(c)(4) sit 501(c)(3) charities such as the AARP Foundation, which assists people over 50 at economic and social risk, and for-profit operations — AARP Services, which develops and manages products, and AARP Financial Services, which holds real estate ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/aarp.asp)). The AARP Insurance Plan administers some of the group's insurance plans, and the organization publishes *Modern Maturity* magazine and the monthly *AARP Bulletin* ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/aarp.asp)). Its advocacy portfolio spans Social Security, Medicare, prescription drug costs, health insurance access, and age discrimination in the workplace — the issues its members say they care about most ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/a/aarp.asp)).

That is the version of AARP every reference entry carries. The missing piece — the one standard profiles never mention — is the financial architecture underneath: an advocacy nonprofit whose own filings show it spends more on payroll than it collects from its members, kept afloat by an intellectual-property business whose single largest customer is the company its advocacy is supposed to keep honest.
