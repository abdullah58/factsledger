## Overview

Linotype is the type company whose name began as a machine — a line-casting typesetter patented in the United States in 1884 by Ottmar Mergenthaler — and which, a century later, survived the death of hot metal by betting its most valuable fonts on an unproven digital language ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Linotype)). In its current form it is Linotype GmbH, based in Bad Homburg, Germany, home of a library of more than 6,000 typefaces including Helvetica, Frutiger and Optima ([CreativePro](https://creativepro.com/monotype-imaging-acquires-linotype/)). Its German present and American origin are both part of the story, and this profile states both openly.

The original machine cast an entire line of type at once — hence "line-o'-type" — rather than setting individual characters. Britannica describes the system: an operator selected a magazine of brass matrices to mould a font of the specified size and face, a keyboard selected the matrices for each line including tapered spacebands that wedged words apart to fill the line perfectly, and the resulting slugs were rectangular solids of type metal, an alloy of lead, antimony and tin, whose raised characters were a mirror image of the printed line ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Linotype)). After casting, a distributing mechanism returned each matrix to its magazine for reuse. For newspapers and magazines setting large amounts of straight text, it was the workhorse of a century — until photocomposition, Britannica notes, largely supplanted it ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Linotype)).

## From Mergenthaler to Bad Homburg

The corporate chain runs from the United States to Germany in stages. Mergenthaler Linotype Company was founded in the United States in 1886 to market the machine Mergenthaler invented, according to Adobe Fonts' lineage summary; a British offshoot formed in 1889 later merged into Linotype & Machinery Ltd in 1903, and "through a series of mergers and reorganizations, the business of Mergenthaler Linotype Company ultimately vested in Linotype-Hell AG, a German company" ([Adobe Fonts](https://fonts.adobe.com/designers/linotype-design-studio)). In 1997 that German company was acquired by Heidelberger Druckmaschinen AG, and Linotype Library GmbH was spun off as a company responsible solely for acquiring, creating and distributing digital fonts; in 2005 it shortened its name to Linotype GmbH, and in 2007 it was acquired by Monotype ([Adobe Fonts](https://fonts.adobe.com/designers/linotype-design-studio)). CreativePro's contemporary report of the Monotype deal confirms the endpoint: Linotype GmbH, a subsidiary of Heidelberg and home of the Linotype Library, would remain based in Bad Homburg, Germany, as a wholly owned Monotype Imaging subsidiary, with a collection including "some of the most popular and time-honored designs such as the Helvetica, Frutiger and Optima typefaces" ([CreativePro](https://creativepro.com/monotype-imaging-acquires-linotype/)).

## The underdog bet on PostScript

Linotype's most consequential decision came in the 1980s, when its hot-metal world was already ending and a new one had not yet been settled. [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/) was building an outline-type business around [PostScript](/creative-works/postscript/), and co-founder [Charles Geschke](/people/charles-geschke/) said Adobe was "the first to be licensed the trademarks 'Times' and 'Helvetica' by the Linotype corporation" ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Convincing a traditional font company was hard, Geschke explained, because Linotype "had a lock on the customers who bought their type": leaving Linotype for Agfa or [Compugraphic](/organizations/compugraphic/) was a major undertaking "because nobody's equipment was compatible" ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

Adobe's first approach, tellingly, was not to Linotype at all. Geschke recalled: "We went to Compugraphic first because they were an American company and we thought that would be easier. They were very arrogant. They had the lion's share of the market at the time. Linotype needed an edge up and so they agreed to do business with us" ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). The result, in Geschke's account, reversed the market in about two years: "Within about two years they had the lion's share of the business in the United States because they had PostScript compatibility. Compugraphic had to sell itself to Agfa and basically went out of business" ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). When Wharton later asked about Adobe's early partners, Geschke answered in two words: "And Linotype" — alongside Apple ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

Britannica corroborates the industrial follow-through from the other side: imagesetter manufacturers "led by Linotype-Hell Company" followed Apple's example in licensing PostScript, and within a few years it became common throughout the publishing industry ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Adobe-Systems-Incorporated)). The LaserWriter had proved the desktop case; Linotype's high-resolution imagesetters proved the professional one, carrying the same fonts from office proof to commercial print. By the time Monotype bought the company, CreativePro could describe Linotype's library in the terms of the digital era it had helped create — more than 6,000 original typefaces, available in PostScript, TrueType and OpenType formats, from designers including Hermann Zapf, Adrian Frutiger and Matthew Carter ([CreativePro](https://creativepro.com/monotype-imaging-acquires-linotype/)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not hide the Germany/US split: the briefed country is Germany because the surviving company is Linotype GmbH of Bad Homburg, but the business was founded in the United States in 1886, and the intermediate mergers that moved it to Germany are reported only as Adobe Fonts summarises them. It does not present Geschke's "lion's share" timing or his "first licensed Times and Helvetica" claim as independently audited market data — they are his participant account, attributed as such. It does not claim a single Monotype acquisition day: CreativePro announced the deal in August 2006, Adobe Fonts dates completion to 2007. And it does not treat the Linotype machine and Linotype GmbH as the same business in the same era: one was a 19th-century hardware company, the other is the digital-font company that inherited its library and name.
