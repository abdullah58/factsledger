## Overview

The Canada Foundation for Innovation (CFI) is an independent, not-for-profit corporation created by the Government of [Canada](/places/canada/) in 1997 to fund research infrastructure — the laboratories, equipment, computer systems and buildings researchers need — at Canadian universities, colleges, research hospitals and non-profit research institutions ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/dictionary/term/canada-foundation-for-innovation-cfi)). It does not fund the research itself: no salaries, no stipends, no operating costs. That single distinction defines it.

The CFI began life in 1997 with $800 million and a five-year mandate, proposed in the federal budget of 18 February and established on 25 April ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/history)). It now operates on permanent funding of up to $450.55 million a year under a 2019 contribution agreement with the [Government of Canada](/organizations/government-of-canada/) ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/sites/default/files/2026-03/CFI-CorporatePlan-26-27-20260130.pdf)). Since its creation it has leveraged about $12 billion of its own funding into more than $27 billion of total investment in research infrastructure and operating and maintenance costs, supporting 13,900 projects at 177 research institutions in 82 municipalities across the country ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/sites/default/files/2026-03/CFI-CorporatePlan-26-27-20260130.pdf)). Its head office is in [Ottawa](/places/ottawa/), [Ontario](/places/ontario/).

## From a five-year experiment to permanent funding

The CFI was an experiment in how a government funds science without running it. Proposed in the 1997 budget, it was set up as an independent corporation at arm's length from government, with its members drawn from the research community and the private sector, and it was given $800 million to spend about $180 million a year over five years ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/history)). Its first President and CEO, metallurgical engineer Keith Brimacombe, was named in June 1997; he died that December, and Denis Gagnon stepped in as acting chief executive ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/history)). The Board of Directors met for the first time on 1 December 1997, and the first Innovation Fund competition followed in 1999, awarding $198 million to 49 institutions for more than 400 projects ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/history)).

The five-year mandate did not last five years in practice. The 2000 federal budget added $900 million and extended it to 2005, and further agreements followed: $4.39 billion in total was allocated under the first funding agreement between 1997 and 2009, generating another $1.73 billion in interest income for project funding ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/sites/default/files/2026-03/CFI-CorporatePlan-26-27-20260130.pdf)). Along the way the CFI built new instruments — a $554-million Research Hospital Fund in 2004, the Research Facilities Navigator directory for industry in 2013 — and became a founding partner of the Governor General's Innovation Awards in 2015 ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/history)). In 2017 the government channelled $328.5 million through the CFI to 17 national research facilities, and the Innovation Fund awarded $554 million to 117 projects, taking the CFI past 10,000 funded projects ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/history)). Budget 2018 then proposed what the temporary model had always pointed toward: permanent funding, delivered through a 2019 contribution agreement worth $5.13 billion over 15 years ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/sites/default/files/2026-03/CFI-CorporatePlan-26-27-20260130.pdf)).

## Infrastructure, not operating grants

The CFI's mandate is "to increase the capability of Canada's universities, colleges, research hospitals and non-profit research organizations to carry out high quality research by investing in research infrastructure" ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/mandate)). That infrastructure means the state-of-the-art facilities, laboratories, equipment, computer hardware, software and databases needed to conduct leading-edge research — from DNA sequencers and mass spectrometers to genome centres and data storage hubs ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/mandate)). The funding model is a lever, not a blank cheque: the CFI generally pays up to 40 percent of a project's infrastructure costs, and the institution must raise the remaining 60 percent from partners in the public, private and non-profit sectors ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/mandate)).

This is what separates the CFI from the three federal granting councils — the Canadian Institutes of Health Research, the [Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council](/organizations/natural-sciences-and-engineering-research-council/) and the [Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council](/organizations/social-sciences-and-humanities-research-council/), collectively the Tri-Agency. Those councils fund the operating costs of doing research: personnel, graduate-student stipends, supplies and travel. The CFI funds only the capital infrastructure, and a researcher cannot use a CFI award to pay a salary or a stipend ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/dictionary/term/canada-foundation-for-innovation-cfi)). The two systems are complementary rather than competing: a research program commonly draws on both in parallel, a CFI award to acquire the equipment and a Tri-Agency grant to run the experiments on it ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/dictionary/term/canada-foundation-for-innovation-cfi)). Awards go to institutions, never to individual researchers, and every proposal must support its institution's strategic research plan and pass merit review by experts from Canada and abroad ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/dictionary/term/canada-foundation-for-innovation-cfi)). Colleges were included as eligible institutions from the start — a point [Colleges and Institutes Canada](/organizations/colleges-and-institutes-canada/) pressed for during the CFI's creation — and the College Fund now serves colleges, polytechnics and CEGEPs specifically ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/dictionary/term/canada-foundation-for-innovation-cfi)).

The CFI managed nine funding programs in 2025–26 ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/sites/default/files/2026-03/CFI-CorporatePlan-26-27-20260130.pdf)). Its newest, the Canada Impact+ Research Infrastructure Fund, comes from the 2025 federal budget, which proposed $400 million over seven years to equip newly recruited international researchers ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/sites/default/files/2026-03/CFI-CorporatePlan-26-27-20260130.pdf)). The John R. Evans Leaders Fund, renamed in 2013 for the CFI's first Board Chair, remains the workhorse for recruiting talent: in October 2026 it provided nearly $8 million for 35 projects at 22 institutions as part of a wider $813-million federal research announcement ([Government of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/research-chairs/news/2026/10/government-of-canada-announces-major-investment-in-canadian-researchers-and-research-institutions.html)).

Since 1997 the CFI has leveraged approximately $12 billion of its own funding into more than $27 billion in total investment in research infrastructure and operating and maintenance costs, supporting 13,900 projects at 177 research institutions in 82 municipalities across Canada ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/sites/default/files/2026-03/CFI-CorporatePlan-26-27-20260130.pdf)) — and that multiplier is the point of the 40-percent model: every federal dollar is designed to pull in a dollar and a half from provinces, institutions and the private sector, rather than paying for the whole laboratory itself.

## National facilities and the current leadership

The CFI's most visible investments are the national facilities it keeps running. Its Major Science Initiatives Fund covers the ongoing operating and maintenance costs of a small number of large, nationally significant facilities, rather than their initial construction ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/dictionary/term/canada-foundation-for-innovation-cfi)). The CFI's own account of its flagship investments includes SNOLAB in Sudbury, where a Canadian researcher won a [Nobel Prize](/events/nobel-prize/) for work in particle physics and cosmology; the Canadian Light Source synchrotron in Saskatoon, where light is used to observe matter down to the atomic level; the CCGS Amundsen icebreaker, dedicated to studying climate change; the Ocean Tracking Network, which connects researchers worldwide to conserve the oceans; and the Museum of Anthropology in Vancouver, whose galleries and laboratory spaces support the study of First Nations artefacts ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/mandate)).

The CFI is led by Sylvain Charbonneau, its sixth President and CEO, who began his tenure on 1 October 2024 after Roseann O'Reilly Runte, in the post since August 2017 ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/news/sylvain-charbonneau-appointed-president-ceo-canada-foundation-innovation)). Charbonneau came from the University of Ottawa, where he was Vice-President, Research and Innovation, after more than 20 years at the National Research Council's Institute for Microstructural Sciences and co-founding the optical-communications spin-off Optenia Inc. ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/news/sylvain-charbonneau-appointed-president-ceo-canada-foundation-innovation)). Under Runte the CFI secured its permanent funding, raised its international profile and launched the bioscience and Northern funds ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/news/sylvain-charbonneau-appointed-president-ceo-canada-foundation-innovation)). The foundation's current direction is set by its 2023–28 strategic plan, "Aiming high", and by the 2025 budget's talent-recruitment push, which pairs the CFI's new infrastructure fund with $1 billion over 13 years to the Tri-Agency for an accelerated research Chairs initiative ([Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/about/mandate); [Canada Foundation for Innovation](https://www.innovation.ca/sites/default/files/2026-03/CFI-CorporatePlan-26-27-20260130.pdf)).

## FAQ

### What does the Canada Foundation for Innovation fund?

It funds research infrastructure only: the facilities, laboratories, equipment, computer hardware, software and databases researchers need to do their work. It does not fund salaries, stipends, travel or day-to-day operating costs — those come from the Tri-Agency granting councils or institutional funds.

### How is the CFI different from NSERC, SSHRC and CIHR?

The CFI is not one of the three federal granting councils and is not administered by them. The councils fund the operating costs of research — people, supplies and travel — while the CFI funds the capital infrastructure. A research program typically uses both in parallel.

### How much of a project does the CFI pay for?

The CFI generally contributes up to 40 percent of a project's eligible infrastructure costs. The institution must secure the remaining 60 percent from partners such as provincial governments, the institution itself, or private and non-profit co-funders.

### Who can apply for CFI funding?

Canadian universities, colleges, research hospitals and non-profit research institutions apply on behalf of their researchers; individual researchers do not apply to the CFI directly. Proposals must support the institution's strategic research plan and pass merit review.

### Who leads the CFI now?

Sylvain Charbonneau has been President and CEO since 1 October 2024, the sixth person to hold the role. He succeeded Roseann O'Reilly Runte, who had served since August 2017, and previously led research and innovation at the University of Ottawa.
