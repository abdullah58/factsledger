## Overview

JVC — the Victor Company of Japan, Limited — was a Japanese electronics company founded in Yokohama, [Japan](/places/japan/), in 1927 as a subsidiary of the American Victor Talking Machine Company ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). It built Japan's first television in 1939, created the VHS home-video format in 1976, and, after decades as a Matsushita affiliate, merged with Kenwood in 2008 to form JVCKENWOOD, into which it was absorbed in 2011 ([JVCKENWOOD Corporation](https://www.jvckenwood.com/content/dam/pdf/211008_other-data_en.pdf)).

The company is best known for two things: introducing Japan's first televisions and developing the Video Home System (VHS) video recorder ([Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom, Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02176FC97FC6AEBF0CBEDF9597722E6E/S0007680500060165a.pdf/strategic_maneuvering_and_massmarket_dynamics_the_triumph_of_vhs_over_beta.pdf)). VHS was introduced in 1976 by the Victor Company of Japan — "Japan Victor or JVC" — and then supported by its parent company Matsushita Electric, as well as by the majority of other firms in Japan, the United States and Europe ([Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom, Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02176FC97FC6AEBF0CBEDF9597722E6E/S0007680500060165a.pdf/strategic_maneuvering_and_massmarket_dynamics_the_triumph_of_vhs_over_beta.pdf)). It was JVC's second act that made it famous: a small firm with limited manufacturing and distribution beat [Sony](/organizations/sony/)'s better-financed Betamax by licensing openly and building the bigger coalition ([Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom, Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02176FC97FC6AEBF0CBEDF9597722E6E/S0007680500060165a.pdf/strategic_maneuvering_and_massmarket_dynamics_the_triumph_of_vhs_over_beta.pdf)). JVC also spent its final independent years as a defendant in one of the tech industry's most notorious patent campaigns, sued in 2004 over the JPEG image format ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/171812/forgent.html)).

## From gramophones to television: the Victor years, 1927-1976

JVC was founded in 1927 as the wholly owned subsidiary of the Victor Talking Machine Company of the United States, to manufacture and market phonographs in Japan ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). Victor was bought in 1929 by the Radio Corporation of America and renamed RCA Victor, and minority shares of the Japanese company were sold to the Mitsubishi and Sumitomo financial groups, leaving JVC as a US-Japanese joint venture ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). In 1930 it built a large phonograph-and-record plant in Yokohama, at the time the largest in Asia ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)).

The company's first great engineer was Kenjiro Takayanagi, who began work on a Japanese-made television at Hamamatsu Technical College in 1924 and succeeded in projecting images two years later ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). In 1939 JVC produced the first television set manufactured entirely from Japanese-made components, a set that never entered mass production but established the company's reputation as a leading electronics firm ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). Takayanagi joined JVC as head of the television research department in July 1946 and was promoted to managing director in 1953 ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)).

War remade the company. RCA Victor sold a majority of its JVC shares to Nihon Sangyo (later Nissan) and its remaining minority interest to Tokyo Shibaura Electric and Nihon Denko in 1938, making JVC an entirely Japanese enterprise ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). In 1943, as part of a government-imposed industrial reorganisation, its name was changed to Nippon Onkyo (Japan Acoustics), and in April 1945 its Yokohama plant was destroyed by aerial bombing ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). The plant was rebuilt after the war and the company returned to its former name ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)).

The rescue came in 1953, when Matsushita Electric Industrial Company was permitted to buy a 50% interest in the nearly bankrupt JVC ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). Matsushita's founder Konosuke Matsushita kept JVC operationally autonomous, offering only managerial direction and capital infusions, and the two companies settled into what the company history calls "competitive cooperation" ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). JVC kept inventing: it developed the 45/45 stereo record system in 1956, introduced stereo phonographs in 1957, and began commercial production of colour television sets in 1960 ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). In 1968 it established a wholly owned US subsidiary, JVC America ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)).

## VHS and the format war: why the second mover won

JVC's video engineers had been working on home recording since the 1960s, and in 1971 the video products division, led by Yuma Shiraishi and Shizuo Takano, started developing VHS from a requirement matrix of twelve goals ([IEEE Engineering and Technology History Wiki](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Development_of_VHS%2C_a_World_Standard_for_Home_Video_Recording%2C_1976)). One goal mattered more than the rest: a more-than-two-hour recording capacity, because Shiraishi had noticed that movies and sporting events typically lasted two hours ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/revisiting-the-vcrs-origins)). Sony's Betamax, shown to Matsushita and JVC in 1974, held only one hour, and JVC declined to adopt it ([Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom, Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02176FC97FC6AEBF0CBEDF9597722E6E/S0007680500060165a.pdf/strategic_maneuvering_and_massmarket_dynamics_the_triumph_of_vhs_over_beta.pdf)).

Sony reached the market first, releasing the Betamax SL-6300 in 1975 at 229,800 yen (about $820) ([IEEE Engineering and Technology History Wiki](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Development_of_VHS%2C_a_World_Standard_for_Home_Video_Recording%2C_1976); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/revisiting-the-vcrs-origins)). JVC answered on 9 September 1976 with the HR-3300, the first VHS recorder, announced at the Okura Hotel in Tokyo and on sale in Akihabara on 31 October 1976 at 256,000 yen (about $915) ([IEEE Engineering and Technology History Wiki](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Development_of_VHS%2C_a_World_Standard_for_Home_Video_Recording%2C_1976); [Museum of Magnetic Sound Recording](https://museumofmagneticsoundrecording.org/ManufacturersJVC.html)). The two-hour tape was the difference: since few consumers could tell the formats' picture quality apart, the convenience of longer playing time won out ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/revisiting-the-vcrs-origins)).

But the decisive move was commercial, not technical. In contrast to Sony, JVC followed a strategy aimed at forming as large a group as possible, aggressively pursuing both licensing and OEM agreements ([Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom, Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02176FC97FC6AEBF0CBEDF9597722E6E/S0007680500060165a.pdf/strategic_maneuvering_and_massmarket_dynamics_the_triumph_of_vhs_over_beta.pdf)). By the end of 1976 it had lined up Hitachi, Mitsubishi and Sharp in addition to Matsushita, and it began shipping machines to Hitachi in December 1976 and to Sharp and Mitsubishi in early 1977 — while Sony refused to build VCRs for licensees and insisted they meet its own standard ([Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom, Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02176FC97FC6AEBF0CBEDF9597722E6E/S0007680500060165a.pdf/strategic_maneuvering_and_massmarket_dynamics_the_triumph_of_vhs_over_beta.pdf)). RCA launched VHS in the United States in 1977, and the American market drove its enormous growth ([IEEE Engineering and Technology History Wiki](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Development_of_VHS%2C_a_World_Standard_for_Home_Video_Recording%2C_1976)). VHS passed Betamax in market share during 1978, and by the end of the 1980s Sony and its partners had ceased producing Beta models ([Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom, Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02176FC97FC6AEBF0CBEDF9597722E6E/S0007680500060165a.pdf/strategic_maneuvering_and_massmarket_dynamics_the_triumph_of_vhs_over_beta.pdf)). The payoff was dramatic: JVC's video division grew from 6% of total sales in 1976 to 69% in 1982, and profits rose tenfold ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)).

## Camcorders, a patent lawsuit and the Kenwood merger

VHS made JVC rich, but the company kept innovating in video. In 1984 it introduced the GR-C1, a single-unit video camera, and in 1986 the GR-C7, the world's smallest and lightest VHS-C video camera ([Museum of Magnetic Sound Recording](https://museumofmagneticsoundrecording.org/ManufacturersJVC.html); [JVCKENWOOD Corporation](https://www.jvckenwood.com/content/dam/pdf/211008_other-data_en.pdf)). In 1995 it launched the GR-DV1, the world's first pocket-sized digital camcorder ([JVCKENWOOD Corporation](https://www.jvckenwood.com/content/dam/pdf/211008_other-data_en.pdf)). On 16 October 2001 JVC shared a Technology and Engineering Emmy for the development of consumer camcorders with Eastman Kodak, Hitachi, Matsushita Electric and Sony ([TV Technology](https://www.tvtechnology.com/news/tech-emmys-2001)). The 1990s were harder: JVC posted pre-tax losses in 1992, 1993 and 1994, cut 700 video-plant workers in August 1992, and cut a further 3,000 staff in 1993 ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)).

Then came the lawsuit. Forgent Networks had acquired [Compression Labs](/organizations/compression-labs/) in 1997, gaining US Patent 4,698,672, filed in October 1986 and covering a coding system for reducing redundancy that Forgent said was integral to JPEG image compression ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2004/04/23/jpeg-patent-holder-renews-royalty-offensive/799817); [Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/171812/forgent.html)). Forgent began enforcing the patent in June 2002, persuaded Sony to pay a one-time fee of about $16 million, and claimed some $90 million in royalties by April 2004 ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/171812/forgent.html); [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1702489/forgent-sues-pc-makers-over-jpeg-patent-claims.html)). In April 2004 its Compression Labs subsidiary sued 31 companies that had rejected its royalty terms — including JVC Americas Corp., [Apple](/organizations/apple/) and [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/) — seeking damages and an injunction ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/171812/forgent.html); [Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1702489/forgent-sues-pc-makers-over-jpeg-patent-claims.html)). The campaign collapsed in 2006: in May the US Patent Office rejected 19 of the patent's 47 claims — the broadest ones — on prior-art grounds at the request of the [Public Patent Foundation](/organizations/public-patent-foundation/), and in November Forgent settled its remaining cases for about $8 million from about 30 companies, having collected more than $110 million from over 60 companies over the patent's life ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/180138/jpeg.html); [BetaNews](https://betanews.com/article/forgent-settles-jpeg-patent-litigation/)). Forgent Networks is now known as [Asure Software](/organizations/asure-software/), after renaming itself and reinventing as a payroll company.

Matsushita, meanwhile, was trying to shed its loss-making affiliate. In July 2007 Kenwood and the investment firm SPARKX agreed to spend 30 billion yen subscribing to new JVC shares, taking a combined 25% stake and cutting Matsushita's holding from 52% to 39% ([domain-b](https://www.Domain-b.com/companies/companies_m/matsushita/20070720_acquire.html)). On 12 May 2008 JVC and Kenwood announced they would set up the joint holding company JVC Kenwood Holdings, effective 1 October 2008, with Victor shareholders receiving two shares for each Victor share and Kenwood shareholders one for one; the combined market capitalisation was just over 150 billion yen ([Associated Press](http://www.manufacturing.net/industry40/news/13066866/jvc-kenwood-set-up-joint-holding-company)). The holding company was established on 1 October 2008 and listed on the First Section of the Tokyo Stock Exchange, with JVC and Kenwood as unlisted operating companies and part of the car-electronics business passed to J&K Technologies, a joint venture set up in 2007 ([What Hi-Fi?](https://www.whathifi.com/news/update-more-details-jvc-and-kenwood-merger)). The stated reasons were growing product complexity, the need for more software investment, and competition from China, Korea and Taiwan ([What Hi-Fi?](https://www.whathifi.com/news/update-more-details-jvc-and-kenwood-merger)). In 2011 the holding company completed an absorption-type merger of its three subsidiaries — Victor Company of Japan, Kenwood Corporation and J&K Car Electronics Corporation — and became JVCKENWOOD Corporation ([JVCKENWOOD Corporation](https://www.jvckenwood.com/content/dam/pdf/211008_other-data_en.pdf)). JVC survives today as a brand of the merged group ([JVCKENWOOD Corporation](https://www.jvckenwood.com/content/dam/pdf/211008_other-data_en.pdf)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What was JVC?**
JVC was the Victor Company of Japan, Limited, a Japanese electronics company founded in Yokohama in 1927 as a subsidiary of the American Victor Talking Machine Company ([Company-Histories.com](https://Company-Histories.com/Victor-Company-of-Japan-Limited-Company-History.html)). It built Japan's first television in 1939, created the VHS format in 1976, and was absorbed into JVCKENWOOD Corporation in 2011 ([JVCKENWOOD Corporation](https://www.jvckenwood.com/content/dam/pdf/211008_other-data_en.pdf)).

**Who invented VHS?**
A JVC engineering team headed by Shizuo Takano and Yuma Shiraishi developed VHS at the company's Yokohama plant, starting in 1971 and working from a twelve-goal requirement matrix ([IEEE Engineering and Technology History Wiki](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Development_of_VHS%2C_a_World_Standard_for_Home_Video_Recording%2C_1976)). The first model, the HR-3300, was announced on 9 September 1976 ([IEEE Engineering and Technology History Wiki](https://ethw.org/Milestones:Development_of_VHS%2C_a_World_Standard_for_Home_Video_Recording%2C_1976)).

**Why did VHS beat Betamax?**
Two reasons: VHS recorded for two hours against Betamax's one, and JVC licensed the format openly, building a coalition of 40 companies by 1983-84 against Betamax's 12 ([Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom, Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02176FC97FC6AEBF0CBEDF9597722E6E/S0007680500060165a.pdf/strategic_maneuvering_and_massmarket_dynamics_the_triumph_of_vhs_over_beta.pdf)). VHS passed Betamax in market share in 1978 and held an 80% annual production share by 1984 ([Cusumano, Mylonadis & Rosenbloom, Business History Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/02176FC97FC6AEBF0CBEDF9597722E6E/S0007680500060165a.pdf/strategic_maneuvering_and_massmarket_dynamics_the_triumph_of_vhs_over_beta.pdf)).

**Was JVC sued over JPEG?**
Yes. In April 2004 Forgent Networks sued 31 companies, including JVC Americas Corp., alleging their use of JPEG image compression infringed US Patent 4,698,672 ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/171812/forgent.html)). In May 2006 the US Patent Office rejected the patent's broadest claims on prior-art grounds, and Forgent settled its remaining cases that November ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/180138/jpeg.html); [BetaNews](https://betanews.com/article/forgent-settles-jpeg-patent-litigation/)).

**What happened to JVC?**
JVC merged with Kenwood Corporation: the joint holding company JVC Kenwood Holdings was established on 1 October 2008, and in 2011 the operating companies were absorbed into it and the group became JVCKENWOOD Corporation ([What Hi-Fi?](https://www.whathifi.com/news/update-more-details-jvc-and-kenwood-merger); [JVCKENWOOD Corporation](https://www.jvckenwood.com/content/dam/pdf/211008_other-data_en.pdf)). JVC continues as a brand of the merged group ([JVCKENWOOD Corporation](https://www.jvckenwood.com/content/dam/pdf/211008_other-data_en.pdf)).
