## The chairmanship factory

Standard histories of the Republican Study Committee list its chairmen as a chronology. Read as a ladder, that list is something else: a factory for Republican House leadership. Speaker [Mike Johnson](/people/mike-johnson/) chaired the RSC for the 116th Congress. House Majority Leader Steve Scalise chaired it for 2013–2014. Jim Jordan chaired it for the 112th. Mike Pence chaired it for the 109th Congress. Jim Banks chaired it for the 117th before moving to the Senate. Earlier chairs include Sue Myrick, Jeb Hensarling, Tom Price, Bill Flores, Mark Walker, and Kevin Hern ([Republican Study Committee](http://rsc-pfluger.house.gov/about/history)).

That lineage is not just a historian's observation; it is how the current chairman describes his own job. When Rep. August Pfluger (TX-11) was elected RSC chairman for the 119th Congress, he said the organization "has been led by incredible mentors of mine, including Speaker Mike Johnson, Majority Leader Steve Scalise, Jim Jordan, Senator-Elect Jim Banks, and current Chairman Kevin Hern" ([Rep. August Pfluger](https://pfluger.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=2190)). The queue profile this one resolves — "conservative caucus he formerly chaired" — points to Mike Johnson's turn at the helm in the 116th Congress, one rung on that same ladder ([Republican Study Committee](http://rsc-pfluger.house.gov/about/history)).

Pfluger came to the chairmanship after leading the RSC's House Energy Action Team, and he credited Hern with having used "our slim majority in the House of Representatives to push back against the Biden-Harris Agenda" ([Rep. August Pfluger](https://pfluger.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=2190)). The caucus describes itself as "the largest caucus of House Conservatives on Capitol Hill" ([Rep. August Pfluger](https://pfluger.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=2190)).

## Founded 1973, abolished 1995, revived 2001

The RSC was founded in 1973 by Rep. Phil Crane (R-IL) "and others" ([Republican Study Committee](http://rsc-pfluger.house.gov/about/history)). For years it operated as a Legislative Service Organization — a House-sanctioned support entity — until such organizations were abolished under House rules in the first days of the 104th Congress in 1995. Rep. Dan Burton (R-IN) was the last chairman before abolition ([Republican Study Committee](http://rsc-pfluger.house.gov/about/history)).

The caucus did not stay dead. Reps. John Doolittle (R-CA), Ernest Istook (R-OK), Sam Johnson (R-TX), and Burton restarted it as the Conservative Action Team — the "CATs." Rep. John Shadegg (R-AZ) became CAT chairman in 2000 and restored the Republican Study Committee name in 2001 ([Republican Study Committee](http://rsc-pfluger.house.gov/about/history)). That revival arc explains the chair sequence that follows: Myrick for the 108th, Pence for the 109th, Hensarling for the 110th, Price for the 111th, Jordan for the 112th, Scalise (and then Rob Woodall for part of the year) for the 113th, Flores for the 114th, Walker for the 115th, Mike Johnson for the 116th, Banks for the 117th, Hern for the 118th, and Pfluger for the 119th ([Republican Study Committee](http://rsc-pfluger.house.gov/about/history)).

## The night the staff director was fired

The most revealing episode in the RSC's internal history is one most summaries skip. In December 2013, Chairman Steve Scalise abruptly fired Paul Teller, the caucus's longtime executive director — a man Politico described as "nearly as powerful as a member of Congress," who had "been involved in conservative strategy for more than a decade, helping drag legislative debates to the right," but who "often chafed on Republican leadership, who saw him as causing intra-party drama" ([Reason](https://reason.com/2013/12/11/republican-study-committee-director-abru/)).

The stated reason, relayed to Politico by a lawmaker present at the meeting where the firing was announced, was that Teller had been "divulging private, member level conversations and actively working against strategies developed by RSC members." The National Journal's analysis put it more bluntly: the chairman had fired his longtime director "out of concern he was leaking confidential conversations to conservative groups hostile to Republican interests," with the two sides "at odds with each other over political strategy" ([Reason](https://reason.com/2013/12/11/republican-study-committee-director-abru/)).

The episode captures the fault line that has run through the conservative movement for a decade: the caucus as an instrument of House Republican strategy versus the caucus as a lever for outside pressure groups. It is the single clearest window into how the RSC actually functions when its members disagree — and it belongs in any serious account of the organization.

## The Weyrich troika

The RSC's founding has a lineage that mainstream histories underplay. The RSC, the American Legislative Exchange Council (ALEC), and the Heritage Foundation were all founded in 1973 by conservative activist Paul Weyrich — who also founded the Free Congress Foundation the following year and, in 1977, co-founded the Moral Majority with Jerry Falwell ([PR Watch](https://WWW.PRWATCH.ORG/news/2012/09/11752/paul-weyrichs-troika-reunited-alec-partners-republican-study-committee-heritage-f)).

In September 2012, that shared origin became an active partnership: the RSC opened a channel with ALEC, scheduling a first meeting at Heritage Foundation headquarters that Roll Call reported would include at least six federal and 18 state lawmakers ([PR Watch](https://WWW.PRWATCH.ORG/news/2012/09/11752/paul-weyrichs-troika-reunited-alec-partners-republican-study-committee-heritage-f)). At the time, the RSC was described as a caucus of 169 House Republicans, with Jim Jordan as its chair ([PR Watch](https://WWW.PRWATCH.ORG/news/2012/09/11752/paul-weyrichs-troika-reunited-alec-partners-republican-study-committee-heritage-f)).

The link mattered enough that, when Weyrich died in 2008, then-RSC Chair Jeb Hensarling issued a statement saying: "The Heritage Foundation, which he co-founded, is an intellectual home for all conservatives. And without Paul, the Republican Study Committee, which he worked to create, would not exist today" ([PR Watch](https://WWW.PRWATCH.ORG/news/2012/09/11752/paul-weyrichs-troika-reunited-alec-partners-republican-study-committee-heritage-f)). Critics were less warm: Common Cause's president at the time called it "mind-boggling" for the RSC and its members to establish ties to ALEC as the group faced IRS scrutiny and corporate defections ([PR Watch](https://WWW.PRWATCH.ORG/news/2012/09/11752/paul-weyrichs-troika-reunited-alec-partners-republican-study-committee-heritage-f)). The partnership illustrates how the caucus has always sat at the intersection of the House, the think-tank world, and the state-level legislative machinery — the conservative movement's infrastructure in one room.

## The budget as the signature product

Policy-wise, the RSC's calling card is its annual budget. In May 2019, Rep. Jim Banks — then chair of the RSC's Budget & Spending Task Force — took to the House floor with a caucus he described as "141-member strong" to present the "Preserving American Freedom" budget resolution ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/congressional-record/volume-165/issue-81/house-section/article/H3830-1)).

The proposal's shape is the RSC's ideology in miniature: $12.6 trillion in spending cuts over ten years, permanent solvency for Medicare and Social Security, repeal of ObamaCare with control shifted to the states, welfare reform aimed at moving people into employment, full funding of the border wall, and sustained military investment — all framed as a rebuke to Democratic budget priorities of the moment ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/congressional-record/volume-165/issue-81/house-section/article/H3830-1)). Heritage Action praised the plan for striking "good balance on cutting spending and investing in national defense"; Democrats attacked it on Social Security, Medicare, and its abortion provisions. That pattern — the RSC produces the most detailed conservative governing blueprint in the House, the opposition attacks its sharpest edges, and the document sets the terms of the intra-party debate — has repeated with each year's release.

The caucus is formally part of the [Republican Party](/organizations/republican-party/)'s House machinery, but its budgets have always been more aggressive than leadership's — which is precisely the point. The RSC exists to keep the conference's right flank organized, informed, and armed with alternatives. The chairmanship ladder that starts with Phil Crane and runs through Pence, Jordan, Scalise, Johnson, Banks, and Hern to Pfluger is the proof that the strategy works: the caucus that writes the most conservative budget in the House keeps producing the people who run the House.
