## Overview

Generic explainers describe the House Oversight Committee in the abstract: the House's main investigative body, with jurisdiction over nearly the entire federal government. That is true and not very informative. The committee as it exists in late 2026 is better understood through three facts no explainer covers: it is being sued in federal court by a billionaire over its Epstein investigation, it published a self-declared "wins" report on September 29, 2026, and it effectively operates as two openly opposed partisan investigations sharing a single name.[^1][^2][^3]

Formally the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform in the 119th Congress, chaired by James Comer (R-Ky.), it traces its lineage to 1927 and holds the broadest investigative mandate of any House panel.[^1][^4]

## The Epstein investigation

The committee's defining 2026 story is its probe into the federal government's handling of the Jeffrey Epstein and [Ghislaine Maxwell](/people/ghislaine-maxwell/) criminal cases. On May 12, 2026, it held a hearing titled "Survivors Fight for Justice: Exposing Epstein's Crimes in Palm Beach and Across the World."[^3] Then the investigation collided with Wall Street.

In June 2026, [[Leon Black](/people/leon-black/)](https://factsledger.com/people/leon-black/) — co-founder of Apollo Global Management — sat for a voluntary transcribed interview and refused to answer questions about Epstein's victims and about nondisclosure agreements. Comer issued two subpoenas in the middle of the session: one demanding any NDAs Black had entered into, including any linked to Epstein or Maxwell, and one compelling a sworn deposition. Black walked out.[^2] He had told the committee he had no knowledge of Epstein's crimes and described roughly $158 million in payments to the late financier as compensation for legitimate tax and estate planning advice; he had relinquished Apollo's chief executive role in 2021 after an external review of those payments.[^2]

On September 3, 2026, Black sued the committee and Comer in Washington federal court, alleging they had "weaponized" the voluntary interview into an unlawful "fishing expedition" with no legitimate legislative purpose, threatening the privacy of women unconnected to the scandal. His attorney, Susan Estrich, said: "This is no longer about finding the truth about Epstein."[^2] Comer defended the probe's scope, saying it aims to uncover how federal authorities mishandled the Epstein and Maxwell cases for decades and how Epstein used powerful connections to evade justice — and that Black's testimony is crucial because he was one of Epstein's fee-paying clients with several nondisclosure agreements.[^2]

The background, as recounted in the [Reuters](https://factsledger.com/organizations/reuters/) reporting: Epstein pleaded guilty to 2008 state prostitution charges and served 13 months in jail, was arrested again in 2019 on federal sex-trafficking charges, and was found dead in a New York City jail cell with his death ruled a suicide. Maxwell is serving a 20-year prison sentence after her 2021 conviction for conspiring with Epstein to sexually abuse minors.[^2]

## The September 2026 wins report

On September 29, 2026 — the day before this profile was written — Comer released "Restoring America Through Oversight: 119th Congress Wins from the House Oversight Committee," a report summarizing what the majority counts as its accomplishments: protecting taxpayer dollars from waste, fraud, and abuse; making life more affordable; and making government more efficient.[^1] Its ten listed wins ranged from exposing fraud in state-administered federal programs to holding sanctuary-city mayors and governors accountable to "Exposing the Biden Autopen Presidency" — which Comer called "the biggest political scandal in U.S. history" — and investigating the federal handling of the Epstein and Maxwell cases.[^1]

"Our goal at the Oversight Committee from day one has been to protect American taxpayers," Comer said. "We've worked tirelessly to root out waste, fraud, and abuse of taxpayer dollars by exposing massive fraud in federally funded programs from California to Minnesota."[^1] The report is, by its nature, the majority's account of itself — a primary document, and a partisan one.

## Two committees under one roof

The minority tells a different story on its own website. The Democrats' 119th Congress hearing agenda reads as a counter-investigation of the Trump administration: "Our Values at Stake (Part V): Deaths, Deception, and a Failure of Accountability at DHS" (September 22, 2026), "A Bad Bet for America: Trump's Attack on Affordability" (August 28), "Monumental Waste: How Donald Trump Is Spending Millions Destroying American Landmarks" (July 21), "Abandoning Americans to Disease" (June 23), "How DOGE and Trump Damaged Federal Workers, Public Services, and How We Fix It" (February 12), and "Examining DHS' Use of Violence in Immigration Enforcement" (February 3) — alongside the May Epstein survivors' hearing both sides could agree on.[^3]

This is the structural reality of the modern committee: the majority and minority run parallel, mutually opposed inquiry agendas, each using the committee's machinery to investigate the other's party. Any profile that describes "the committee's" 2026 record without specifying whose is describing only half of it.

## What it can actually do

The committee's power comes from House Rule X. Clause 2(b) lets it review the application and effectiveness of laws, programs, and federal agencies, and study where new legislation is needed. Clause 3(i) mandates reviewing government activities for "economy and efficiency." Clause 4(c)(1) gives it access to the Comptroller General's reports and the authority to send recommendations to the full House. And clause 4(c)(2) — the sweeping one — provides that the committee "may at any time conduct investigations of any matter" within any standing committee's jurisdiction, regardless of its own.[^4]

Its enumerated jurisdiction covers the federal civil service, municipal affairs of the District of Columbia, paperwork reduction, government management and accounting, holidays and celebrations, the overall economy, the national archives, the census, the postal service, public records, federal-state-municipal relations, and executive branch reorganizations.[^4] In practice, the "any matter" clause is what makes it the venue for whatever the majority wants to investigate.

## A century of name changes

The panel began in 1927 as the Committee on Expenditures in the Executive Departments, consolidating eleven separate spending-oversight committees. In 1952 it became the Committee on Government Operations, a name meant to reflect its broad mission of overseeing government activities "with a view to determining their economy and efficiency." In 1995 the Republican majority cut its fourteen subcommittees to seven; in 2007 the Democratic majority cut seven to five.[^4]

In 1999 it absorbed oversight of the Post Office and the civil service and was renamed the Committee on Government Reform. House Democrats renamed it the Committee on Oversight and Government Reform in 2007 and the Committee on Oversight and Reform in 2019; for the 119th Congress it is once again the Committee on Oversight and Government Reform.[^4][^1] The name, in short, changes with the political winds.

Its cultural footprint comes from hearings that transcended jurisdiction: the 2005 steroids-in-Major-League-Baseball hearings that pushed the players' association to a zero-tolerance policy; the 2005 Terri Schiavo case, where the committee issued an unenforceable subpoena as a delay tactic; a 2012 hearing on the Affordable Care Act's contraception-coverage requirement under Chairman Darrell Issa; and the 2019 "Kids in Cages" hearing on the treatment of children and families in border detention.[^4]

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not claim that either party's account of the committee's 2026 record is the neutral one — the "wins" report is the majority's document, and the minority's hearing list is the minority's framing. It does not predict the outcome of Leon Black's lawsuit, which was pending at the time of writing. It does not assert the current ranking member's identity. And it does not treat the committee's "any matter" investigative power as unlimited in practice — subpoena fights like Black's are precisely where those limits get tested.
