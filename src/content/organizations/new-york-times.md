## Overview

In January 2009, the richest man in the world lent a quarter of a billion dollars to a newspaper — at 14 percent interest. The New York Times Company, publisher of the daily long described as America's newspaper of record, was sitting on $1.1 billion in debt with $350 million in loans coming due within two years, and it had just suspended its dividend entirely ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/); [Economic Times](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/international/new-york-times-sells-leases-back-part-of-building/articleshow/4246713.cms)). The lender was Mexican telecom billionaire Carlos Slim — then reckoned by Forbes the richest man in the world — and the terms were punishing: a six-year loan plus warrants to buy stock at a rock-bottom $6.35 a share ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)). Two months later the company sold 21 floors of its own 52-story Midtown Manhattan headquarters, a building that had opened only in 2007, for $225 million — and leased the space straight back for 15 years ([Economic Times](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/international/new-york-times-sells-leases-back-part-of-building/articleshow/4246713.cms)).

That is the part of the Times story the standard profiles skip. The paper survived because the Ochs-Sulzberger family, which has controlled it since 1896, refused to sell when the owners of the Wall Street Journal and the [Washington Post](https://factsledger.com/organizations/washington-post/) chose to — and because a family trust's grip on a special class of shares made a sale nearly impossible anyway ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/); [TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/investing/stocks/times-we-will-deliver-10352495)). Today the company is the juggernaut of the newspaper industry: 12.78 million subscribers at the end of 2025, 139 [Pulitzer](https://factsledger.com/events/pulitzer-prize/) Prizes (more than any other news organization), a newsroom of more than 2,000 journalists, and 2024 pre-tax profits of $351 million ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/media-platforms/journalism/the-new-york-times-q4-2025-earnings/); [Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/world/pulitzer-prize-2025-nyt-reuters-fentanyl-full-list-of-winners-9984918/lite/); [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)). This profile is about the business machinery behind the journalism: the debt crisis, the family firewall, the paywall that failed before the one that worked, and the games-and-recipes bundle that made the Times a subscription company.

## The winter of 2009

The crisis had been building for years. Through the late 1990s the Times Company had diversified — buying The Boston Globe, investing in TV and radio, even taking a stake in the Boston Red Sox — and by 2009 the bill came due in the middle of a global financial meltdown ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)). Few banks would lend to a newspaper company at any price, so the Times paid Slim's double-digit rate; company treasurer Tony Benten later called it "a very stressful time frame," adding that cheaper offers had come with terms "much more restrictive," potentially tying management's hands just as a broad digital transformation was beginning ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)).

The Slim money was only the start of the triage. The $225 million headquarters sale-leaseback to investment firm W.P. Carey & Co. followed in March ([Economic Times](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/international/new-york-times-sells-leases-back-part-of-building/articleshow/4246713.cms)). The dividend — cut the previous fall — was suspended entirely in February 2009, saving $133 million a year ([Economic Times](https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/international/new-york-times-sells-leases-back-part-of-building/articleshow/4246713.cms)). The company shopped its 17.8 percent stake in the group that owns the Red Sox, sold About.com for $300 million, and in 2013 unloaded The Boston Globe for $70 million — a paper it had bought for $1.1 billion in 1993, its "worst investment ever" ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)). Inside the building, March 2009 brought a 5 percent temporary pay cut and October brought the elimination of 100 newsroom jobs ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)).

Slim did very well out of the rescue. The Times paid the loan off early — after two years and seven months — and refinanced cheaper; Slim's profit on the loan itself was roughly $90 million ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)). He waited until January 2015 to fully exercise his warrants, by which time the shares had doubled to $12.60; his 16 million shares gave him 17 percent of the company's public stock, worth roughly $100 million on paper ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)). He kept holding as the stock climbed — $23.25 in January 2018, $32 at the start of 2020, the year he sold the last of his stake — turning his $100 million warrant investment into more than five times that ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)). Three months after the loan closed, Arthur Sulzberger Jr. profiled Slim for Time's 100 most influential people issue, calling him "a model of responsible capitalism" ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)).

## The family firewall

What made the 2009 story possible — and what made a sale unthinkable — is the Times' capital structure. The company has two classes of stock: Class A, which the public buys, and Class B, held privately — with the Ochs-Sulzberger family trust owning 89 percent of the Class B shares and 19 percent of the Class A ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/investing/stocks/times-we-will-deliver-10352495)). Class A shareholders elect only 4 of the company's 13 directors; Class B elects 9 ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/investing/stocks/times-we-will-deliver-10352495)). The family set it up this way when the company went public in 1969 — Sulzberger said it was designed "specifically to get us through times like this" — and defends it as protection for the paper's journalism against Wall Street's quarterly pressures ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/investing/stocks/times-we-will-deliver-10352495)).

Wall Street has never liked it. In 2008, Morgan Stanley portfolio manager Hassan Elmasry, whose fund owned 7 percent of the company, led a campaign against the structure — and at that year's annual meeting roughly 42 percent of Class A shareholders withheld their votes for the four public directors, up from 30 percent the year before ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/investing/stocks/times-we-will-deliver-10352495)). It changed nothing: the Class B holders were unanimous, and the revolt was purely symbolic ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/investing/stocks/times-we-will-deliver-10352495)). Washington Post CEO Donald Graham, whose own company used a similar structure, warned that dropping it would mean "a line of buyers eager to purchase the company would form within minutes" — private equity firms, billionaires, international media companies ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/investing/stocks/times-we-will-deliver-10352495)). The structure also explains the fine print of the Slim deal: even as the company's largest shareholder at around 17 percent, Slim could only vote for Class A directors — a third of the board — and a board seat was never part of his agreement ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)).

## The paywall that failed, then the one that worked

The Times tried charging for the web once before — and quit. In 2005 it launched TimesSelect, walling off op-ed columnists like Thomas Friedman plus the archives for $49.95 a year ([AllThingsD](https://allthingsd.com/20100120/the-new-york-times-officially-starts-construction-on-its-paywall-metered-model-coming-2011/); [NewsBreaks](https://newsbreaks.infotoday.com/NewsBreaks/The-New-York-Times-Announces-Plans-for-a-Metered-Model-for-NYTimescom-in-2011-60830.asp)). It generated around $10 million a year, was judged a failed experiment, and was dropped in September 2007 ([AllThingsD](https://allthingsd.com/20100120/the-new-york-times-officially-starts-construction-on-its-paywall-metered-model-coming-2011/)). Then the advertising collapse of 2008–2009 changed the math: as one 2010 report put it, $10 million a year suddenly "sounds like a nice boost for a paper that lost more than $35 million in its most recent quarter" ([AllThingsD](https://allthingsd.com/20100120/the-new-york-times-officially-starts-construction-on-its-paywall-metered-model-coming-2011/)).

On January 20, 2010, after studying more than 30 businesses that charged for web content, the Times announced it would try again — this time with a metered model copied from the Financial Times, offering a set number of free articles a month before asking readers to pay ([AllThingsD](https://allthingsd.com/20100120/the-new-york-times-officially-starts-construction-on-its-paywall-metered-model-coming-2011/); [NewsBreaks](https://newsbreaks.infotoday.com/NewsBreaks/The-New-York-Times-Announces-Plans-for-a-Metered-Model-for-NYTimescom-in-2011-60830.asp)). The details, unveiled in March 2011: 20 free articles a month, then three plans at $15, $20 or $35 per four weeks depending on whether the reader wanted smartphone, tablet, or both apps alongside the website ([AFP](https://phys.org/news/2011-03-ny-unveils-digital-subscription.html?loadCommentsForm=1)). Canada got it first so the company could "fine-tune the customer experience"; the U.S. and the rest of the world followed on March 28, while print home-delivery subscribers kept free digital access ([AFP](https://phys.org/news/2011-03-ny-unveils-digital-subscription.html?loadCommentsForm=1)). Journalism professor Dan Kennedy called it a smart approach but cautioned the Times was "not a very good test case for the news business as a whole" — its readers, he said, included "a huge base of people out there who are willing to pay for the Times and pretty much nothing else" ([AFP](https://phys.org/news/2011-03-ny-unveils-digital-subscription.html?loadCommentsForm=1)).

## From newspaper to bundle

The paywall bought the paper time; the bundle bought it growth. In 2014, A. G. Sulzberger — Arthur's son — wrote an internal Innovation Report arguing that a lingering print-first culture was slowing digital change; he became publisher in January 2018 and chairman in 2021 ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)). Mark Thompson, recruited from the BBC, ran the company as CEO from 2012, and his deputy Meredith Kopit Levien succeeded him in 2020 ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)).

The acquisitions that defined the era were not news properties. In January 2022 the Times bought Wordle — the once-a-day word game created by Brooklyn software engineer Josh Wardle — for an undisclosed price "in the low seven figures," promising it would "initially" stay free and setting a public goal of 10 million subscribers by 2025 ([Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2022/02/02/business/wordle-new-york-times/)). The "initially" provoked a minor uproar from fans fearing a paywall ([Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2022/02/02/business/wordle-new-york-times/)). The bet paid off fast: Wordle drew "10s of millions of users," enough of whom sampled the other Times games to drive the best quarter of paid games-subscription growth the company had ever seen ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2022/new-york-times-wordle-games-subscription-growth/)). A month later the Times closed the $550 million purchase of The Athletic — about half its cash on hand — adding 1.1 million subscribers in a single stroke ([Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2022/new-york-times-wordle-games-subscription-growth/); [Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2022/02/02/business/wordle-new-york-times/)). Earlier digital buys had included the product-review site Wirecutter and the audio app Audm ([Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2022/02/02/business/wordle-new-york-times/)).

By the end of 2025 the strategy had produced 12.78 million total subscribers — 12.21 million of them digital-only — with more than half (6.48 million) on bundles combining news with Games, Cooking, Wirecutter and The Athletic ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/media-platforms/journalism/the-new-york-times-q4-2025-earnings/)). The company added 1.4 million digital-only subscribers in 2025 alone and set its sights on 15 million by the end of 2027 ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/media-platforms/journalism/the-new-york-times-q4-2025-earnings/); [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2022/new-york-times-wordle-games-subscription-growth/)). In September 2025 it introduced family subscriptions — up to four users on one account, each counted as two subscribers — and by early 2026 it was investing in video and a "Watch" tab in its app while suing OpenAI and Perplexity over alleged copyright violations ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/media-platforms/journalism/the-new-york-times-q4-2025-earnings/)).

## The paper behind the business

The business story sits on top of a 175-year-old newspaper. The first issue of the New-York Daily Times appeared on September 18, 1851, founded by journalist and politician Henry Jarvis Raymond and former banker George Jones; it became The New York Times in 1857 and added a Sunday edition in April 1861 as Civil War readers demanded news every day ([Smithsonian](https://blog.library.si.edu/blog/2010/09/18/all-the-news-thats-fit-to-print/)). In 1871 the paper's publication of city boss William "Boss" Tweed's "secret accounts" brought down the Tweed Ring ([Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/history/culture-magazines/new-york-times)). By the 1890s the paper was failing — until 38-year-old Adolph S. Ochs of the Chattanooga Times bought it in 1896 for $75,000, much of it put up by wealthy Democrats, promising to "give the news impartially, without fear or favor" ([Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/history/culture-magazines/new-york-times)). Ochs coined the motto "All the News That's Fit to Print" in 1896; it first appeared on the front page on February 10, 1897 ([Smithsonian](https://blog.library.si.edu/blog/2010/09/18/all-the-news-thats-fit-to-print/)). His business plan was simple — spend on quality and profits will follow — and in his first 25 years the paper plowed 97 percent of what it earned back into the enterprise ([Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/history/culture-magazines/new-york-times)).

The publisher's chair then passed down the family line: Ochs to his son-in-law Arthur Hays Sulzberger, to his son-in-law Orvil Dryfoos, to Arthur Ochs "Punch" Sulzberger, to Arthur Ochs Sulzberger Jr., to A. G. Sulzberger ([Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/history/culture-magazines/new-york-times); [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/business-work/2025/poynter-50-carlos-slim-loan-new-york-times-2009/)). Along the way the paper published the Pentagon Papers in 1971 — a decision vindicated by the Supreme Court on June 30, 1971 — and won the 1964 case New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, which extended First Amendment protection against libel suits by public officials ([Encyclopedia.com](https://encyclopedia.com/history/culture-magazines/new-york-times)). Its trophy case now holds 139 Pulitzer Prizes, a record high as of May 2025, including that year's breaking-news photography prize for Doug Mills' images of the July 2024 assassination attempt on Donald Trump — one frame capturing a bullet in mid-air ([Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/world/pulitzer-prize-2025-nyt-reuters-fentanyl-full-list-of-winners-9984918/lite/)).
