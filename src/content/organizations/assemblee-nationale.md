## Overview

The Assemblée nationale (National Assembly) is the directly elected lower house of the French Parliament: 577 deputies (*députés*) elected for five-year terms, sitting in the Palais Bourbon in [Paris](/places/paris/). Together with the Senate it votes laws, approves the budget and scrutinises the government — and alone among the two chambers it can dismiss a government outright and have the final word when the chambers disagree ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)).

The current chamber dates to the Constitution of 4 October 1958, which created the Fifth Republic's semi-presidential system ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)). Its president is Yaël Braun-Pivet. Since the snap elections of June–July 2024 the Assembly has been hung, with no bloc near the 289 seats needed for a majority — and in barely a year it has toppled two governments, a reminder that the chamber's power to withdraw confidence is real even when its power to pass laws is constrained.

## How the Assembly is elected and how it makes laws

All 577 deputies are elected by universal adult suffrage in single-member constituencies: 555 seats for metropolitan [France](/places/france/) and 22 for the overseas territories ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)). The vote uses a two-round system: a candidate wins in the first round with an absolute majority of the vote and the support of at least 25% of registered voters; otherwise every candidate winning more than 12.5% of registered voters' votes goes through to the second round ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)). Deputies serve five years, but the President of the Republic may dissolve the Assembly and force new elections — though not twice within a year.

Legislation should be adopted in identical form by both houses, but the Assembly has the last word: if the chambers cannot agree, a joint committee of deputies and senators tries to work out a text, and failing that the government may ask the Assembly to decide finally after a further reading in each house ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)). The government's sharpest tool is Article 49.3, under which a bill is adopted unless the Assembly passes a motion of no confidence within twenty-four hours — usable without limit for finance bills, and once per session for any other bill ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)). The Prime Minister, appointed by the President, must command a majority in the Assembly, and ministers may not sit in Parliament.

## From the Palais Bourbon to the Fifth Republic

The Assembly's seat is older than the institution itself. Construction of the Palais Bourbon began in Paris in 1722 on the left bank of the Seine, as a palace for the Duchesse de Bourbon, the legitimised daughter of Louis XIV and the Marquise de Montespan; the palace and the neighbouring Hôtel de Lassay, the President's residence, were completed around 1728 ([Assemblée nationale](https://www2.assemblee-nationale.fr/static/langues/english/beaux-arts.pdf)). From the moment the Council of Five Hundred moved there in 1798, the buildings "became the seat of national sovereignty and the cradle of our Republican values" ([Assemblée nationale](https://www2.assemblee-nationale.fr/static/langues/english/beaux-arts.pdf)).

The name Assemblée nationale reaches back to the Revolution, but the present chamber is a product of 1958: the Fifth Republic strengthened the executive at Parliament's expense, and the Commons Library's briefing calls France's legislature one of the weakest in Europe ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)). When President and Prime Minister come from different parties — "cohabitation", which had happened three times before 2024, most recently 1997–2002 under President Jacques Chirac and Prime Minister Lionel Jospin — the President cannot impose a domestic agenda and must negotiate it ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)). The Assembly is also a publisher of record: its written questions and replies document the institutions of the French state, including the history of [CNP Assurances](/organizations/cnp-assurances/) — created in 1959 from the fusion of two [Caisse des Dépôts](/organizations/caisse-des-depots-et-consignations/) funds, made a public industrial-and-commercial establishment by the decree of 12 October 1987, and converted into a société anonyme by the law of 16 July 1992 ([Assemblée Nationale](https://questions.assemblee-nationale.fr/q15/15-199QE.htm)). The takeover that ended CNP's listed life was declared compliant by the [Autorité des marchés financiers](/organizations/autorite-des-marches-financiers/) in April 2022.

## A hung parliament: the Assembly in 2024–2025

The Assembly's recent history is a case study in what a hung parliament does. After the National Rally led the June 2024 European Parliament elections with 31.4% of the vote, President Emmanuel Macron dissolved the Assembly, saying he could not act "as if nothing had happened" ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)). The gamble backfired: in the snap elections of 30 June and 7 July, the left-wing New Popular Front (NFP) won the most seats, the National Rally led the first-round vote yet finished third on seats after mass second-round withdrawals, and no bloc came near a majority ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)).

Appointed on 5 September 2024, Michel Barnier's minority government survived one no-confidence vote in October, then fell on 4 December 2024 when the left and the far right combined to pass a censure motion with 331 votes in support, after Barnier used Article 49.3 to push through a budget seeking €60 billion in savings ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/french-lawmakers-set-to-vote-on-ousting-prime-minister-barnier/); [House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10112/CBP-10112.pdf)). It was the first government toppled by a confidence vote since Georges Pompidou's in 1962, and the shortest-lived of the Fifth Republic ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/french-lawmakers-set-to-vote-on-ousting-prime-minister-barnier/)). François Bayrou, appointed in December 2024, then called a confidence vote on his own €44 billion savings plan and lost it 364–194 on 8 September 2025, leaving France's public debt at €3.346 trillion — 114% of GDP — and Emmanuel Macron searching for a fourth prime minister in twelve months ([Associated Press](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/34669835/french-government-collapses-in-a-confidence-vote-forcing-macron-to-seek-yet-another-prime-minister)).

## FAQ

### How many deputies sit in the French National Assembly?

There are 577 deputies, each elected in a single-member constituency by a two-round majority vote for a five-year term. 555 seats represent metropolitan France and 22 represent the overseas territories. An absolute majority — 289 seats — is required to pass a motion of censure and bring down a government.

### What is the difference between the National Assembly and the Senate?

Both chambers vote on laws, but only the Assembly is directly elected, only the Assembly can dismiss the government, and only the Assembly has the final word when the two chambers disagree. The Senate's 348 members are elected indirectly by local representatives for six-year terms.

### What is Article 49.3?

Article 49.3 of the Constitution lets the government make a bill a matter of confidence: the text is adopted unless the National Assembly passes a motion of no confidence within twenty-four hours. It can be used without limit for finance bills and once per session for other bills.

### Where does the National Assembly meet?

In the Palais Bourbon in Paris, on the left bank of the Seine, together with the neighbouring Hôtel de Lassay. The palace was built for the Duchesse de Bourbon from 1722 and has housed France's lower chamber since the Council of Five Hundred moved in during 1798.

### Why did the Assembly topple two governments in less than a year?

The snap elections of 2024 produced a hung parliament split three ways, so both Michel Barnier's and François Bayrou's minority governments depended on opponents' tolerance. Barnier fell on 4 December 2024 (331 votes) over his budget; Bayrou lost a confidence vote he called himself on 8 September 2025 (364–194).
