## Overview

Fortune is an American business magazine founded by Henry Luce in 1929 — its first issue appeared in February 1930, four months after the Wall Street crash — and the publisher of the Fortune 500, the annual ranking of the largest [United States](/places/united-states/) companies by revenue ([Smithsonian Libraries](https://blog.library.si.edu/blog/2010/02/10/fortune-magazine-80th-anniversary/)). Since December 2018 it has been owned by Fortune Media Group Holdings Limited, wholly owned by Thai businessman Chatchaval Jiaravanon, who bought the brand from Meredith Corporation for $150 million in cash ([Meredith Corporation](https://dotdashmeredith.mediaroom.com/2018-12-21-Meredith-Finalizes-Sale-Of-FORTUNE-Media-Brand-For-150-Million?asPDF=1)).

Fortune is not Forbes. Both are century-old American business magazines, but Fortune was Luce's creation inside Time Inc. — a sibling of [Time](/organizations/time/) magazine — while [Forbes](/organizations/forbes/) was founded in 1917 by B.C. Forbes and is best known for wealth rankings such as the Forbes 400 and its billionaire lists. Fortune's signature product is a ranking of companies, not people: the Fortune 500, published since 1955, has become the benchmark against which American businesses measure themselves ([Smithsonian Libraries](https://blog.library.si.edu/blog/2010/02/10/fortune-magazine-80th-anniversary/)). Fortune's reporting is itself cited as a source in business coverage — FactsLedger's [ClickUp](/organizations/clickup/) profile names Fortune as the source for the reporting that ClickUp introduced roughly 3,000 internal AI agents.

## Founding and the golden age

Luce proposed the magazine in September 1928 to Time Inc. co-founder Briton Hadden, aiming at "the men who ran businesses in the United States" with a sophisticated view of industrial business and its role in society ([NYU / New-York Historical Society](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/nyhs/timeinc_ms3009_rg25/)). Hadden doubted the idea but let Time business writer Parker Lloyd-Smith explore it; after Hadden's sudden death in February 1929, Luce took the proposal to the board, which agreed, and the first issue came out in February 1930. Luce named himself editor, as he had with Time. There were no focus groups or trial issues, yet 30,000 subscribers signed up at $1 a copy or $10 a year — a deliberately steep price meant to admit only the most passionate and well-off readers. By the end of 1930, after eleven issues, circulation had risen by 10,000 and the magazine was turning a profit ([NYU / New-York Historical Society](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/nyhs/timeinc_ms3009_rg25/)).

What set Fortune apart was that Luce hired literary talent instead of business experts or economists. Its writers included James Agee, John Kenneth Galbraith, Ernest Hemingway, Archibald MacLeish and Dwight Macdonald, and its pages carried art and photography by Ansel Adams, Margaret Bourke-White, Diego Rivera, Ben Shahn and Walker Evans, its first and only staff photographer ([NYU / New-York Historical Society](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/nyhs/timeinc_ms3009_rg25/)). In the 1940s and 1950s the magazine ran monthly issues of up to 300 pages with 6,000- to 8,000-word essays on economics, social issues and politics, pioneered public-opinion polling through staffer Elmo Roper, and kept its own in-house economics department ([Milken Institute Review](https://www.milkenreview.org/index.php/articles/fortune-magazine-in-the-1940s-and-1950s)). Its ambitions showed in the work: in 1941 it reproduced 26 of the 60 paintings in Jacob Lawrence's "Great Migration" series, and Agee and Evans's book *Let Us Now Praise Famous Men* grew out of a 1936 Fortune assignment. Luce redefined the magazine's mission in 1948 as the active defence of the free enterprise system, yet it published some of the sharpest critiques of American capitalism, including William H. Whyte's *The Organization Man* (1956) and Galbraith's *The Affluent Society* (1958). By the late 1950s it sold 325,000 copies a month ([Milken Institute Review](https://www.milkenreview.org/index.php/articles/fortune-magazine-in-the-1940s-and-1950s)).

The golden age had limits the record should not hide. The newsroom was largely white men with Ivy League backgrounds: women could rise no higher than researcher until the 1960s, although Carol Loomis — who coined the term "hedge fund" — later became an award-winning financial journalist, and Fortune had no Black writers during the 1950s ([Milken Institute Review](https://www.milkenreview.org/index.php/articles/fortune-magazine-in-the-1940s-and-1950s)). The magazine also stumbled after the Second World War, becoming unprofitable and, in its own editors' view, short of cohesion and new ideas, until Luce refocused it on guidance about America's political economy ([NYU / New-York Historical Society](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/nyhs/timeinc_ms3009_rg25/)). By the 1960s its writing and covers had grown more prosaic even as circulation kept growing, and it later took the blows that laid glossy magazines low: round-the-clock news, fragmenting advertising and soaring postal rates ([Milken Institute Review](https://www.milkenreview.org/index.php/articles/fortune-magazine-in-the-1940s-and-1950s)).

## The Fortune 500

Fortune's most durable invention came from its data, not its prose. Editor Edgar P. Smith began compiling corporate statistics in the early 1950s from annual reports, Moody's and Standard & Poor's, and in 1955 the magazine published its first list of the nation's 500 largest corporations ([Milken Institute Review](https://www.milkenreview.org/index.php/articles/fortune-magazine-in-the-1940s-and-1950s)). The first list, originally called the Fortune Industrial 500, covered only manufacturing, mining and energy companies: General Motors topped it with $9.8 billion in revenue, and the lowest-ranked company, Copperweld Steel, had $49.7 million ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/f/fortune500.asp)). More than 2,200 American companies have appeared on the list over its history.

| | 1955 (first list) | 2025 (71st list) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Top company | General Motors, $9.8B revenue | Walmart, 13th straight year at No. 1 |
| Cutoff to make the list | $49.7M (lowest-ranked company) | $7.4B in revenue |
| Coverage | Manufacturing, mining, energy only | All sectors; 500 companies in 225 cities, 37 states |
| Scale | 500 companies | $19.9T combined revenue; $1.87T profits; 31M employees |
| Women CEOs | Not recorded | 55 (11%), a record high |

The list's biggest change came when service companies were admitted — 291 of them entered in a single year, three reaching the top 10 — a shift that eventually put Walmart, a retailer, at No. 1 ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/f/fortune500.asp)). The 2025 top 10, announced June 2, 2025, was Walmart, [Amazon](/organizations/amazon/), [UnitedHealth Group](/organizations/unitedhealth-group/), [Apple](/organizations/apple/), CVS Health, Berkshire Hathaway, [Alphabet](/organizations/alphabet/), Exxon Mobil, McKesson and Cencora, and 2024 was the most profitable year on record for the list, at $1.87 trillion in collective earnings ([citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/700963/fortune-announces-2025-fortune-500-list/)). [New York City](/places/new-york-city/), with 43 companies, is the top municipality on the list, and [California](/places/california/) leads all states with 58 ([citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/700963/fortune-announces-2025-fortune-500-list/)). Fortune also publishes the Global 500, the worldwide counterpart, alongside rankings such as Most Powerful Women and World's Most Admired Companies ([citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/700963/fortune-announces-2025-fortune-500-list/)).

## Ownership, paywall and the AI-era reset

Fortune changed hands twice in the modern era's churn. Meredith acquired it as part of its purchase of Time Inc., which closed on January 31, 2018, then sold it on — along with Time magazine, which went to Marc and Lynne Benioff for $190 million — to focus on brands serving its core audience of American women ([Meredith Corporation](https://dotdashmeredith.mediaroom.com/2018-12-21-Meredith-Finalizes-Sale-Of-FORTUNE-Media-Brand-For-150-Million?asPDF=1)). Jiaravanon, affiliated with Thailand's Charoen Pokphand Group, owns Fortune as a personal private investment independent of the group's family businesses ([Meredith Corporation](https://dotdashmeredith.mediaroom.com/2018-12-21-Meredith-Finalizes-Sale-Of-FORTUNE-Media-Brand-For-150-Million?asPDF=1)).

Under the new owner, Fortune rebuilt its business around subscriptions and events rather than print advertising. CEO Alan Murray said the owner "was very explicit that he's taking a long-term view," after two years in which no investment that would not pay off in the same quarter could be made ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/new-ownership-fortune-seeks-diversify-revenues/)). Events were already the fastest-growing and most profitable revenue source, over 40% of revenue, and combined digital and events passed 60% of revenues of nearly $100 million in 2018; the CEO Initiative membership cost $15,000 a year, and the company planned new paid products and a paywall ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/new-ownership-fortune-seeks-diversify-revenues/)). The paywall arrived on March 4, 2020: longform stories, the video library, proprietary research and conference coverage went behind it, while breaking news and the franchise lists stayed free, with tiers at $49, $99 and $199 a year ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/348001/fortune-puts-more-content-behind-paywall-introd.html)). The print magazine, monthly for its first 48 years, then biweekly from 1978 and tri-weekly from 2009, now appears six times a year ([NYU / New-York Historical Society](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/nyhs/timeinc_ms3009_rg25/); [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/f/fortune500.asp)).

The reset continued in July 2025. On July 21 the magazine cut about 10% of its 360 full-time staff, with CEO Anastasia Nyrkovskaya citing "the onset of AI, the resulting decline of website traffic trend and the rise of audio and video as popular formats," and saying Fortune would focus on premium editorial content built on staples like the Fortune 500 while leaning into events ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/fortune-layoffs-ai-cuts/)). The episode captures Fortune's current position: a 95-year-old print brand whose rankings still define corporate America, now trying to sell depth and convening power in a traffic market AI is shrinking.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is Fortune magazine?** Fortune is an American business magazine founded by Henry Luce in 1929, first issued in February 1930 as part of Time Inc. It covers business, leadership and the economy, and is best known for the Fortune 500 ranking of the largest US companies by revenue ([Smithsonian Libraries](https://blog.library.si.edu/blog/2010/02/10/fortune-magazine-80th-anniversary/)).

**Is Fortune the same as Forbes?** No. Fortune was founded by Henry Luce in 1929/1930 inside Time Inc.; Forbes was founded in 1917 by B.C. Forbes. Fortune's signature ranking is the Fortune 500 (companies by revenue); Forbes is best known for wealth rankings such as the Forbes 400 and billionaire lists.

**Who owns Fortune now?** Since December 2018, Fortune Media Group Holdings Limited, wholly owned by Thai businessman Chatchaval Jiaravanon, who bought the brand from Meredith Corporation for $150 million in cash ([Meredith Corporation](https://dotdashmeredith.mediaroom.com/2018-12-21-Meredith-Finalizes-Sale-Of-FORTUNE-Media-Brand-For-150-Million?asPDF=1)).

**What is the Fortune 500?** An annual list, first published in 1955 and created by editor Edgar P. Smith, ranking the 500 largest US companies by total revenue for their fiscal years. In 2025 Walmart topped it for the 13th straight year, and the 500 companies together earned $1.87 trillion, the most profitable year on record ([Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/f/fortune500.asp); [citybiz](https://www.citybiz.co/article/700963/fortune-announces-2025-fortune-500-list/)).

**Why did Fortune cut staff in 2025?** On July 21, 2025 it cut about 10% of its staff. CEO Anastasia Nyrkovskaya cited AI, declining website traffic and the rise of audio and video, and said the company would concentrate on premium editorial content and events ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/fortune-layoffs-ai-cuts/)).
