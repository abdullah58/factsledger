## Overview

Quinnsworth was an Irish supermarket chain founded by Pat Quinn in 1966, which grew into one of Ireland's leading grocery retailers before its acquisition by the British retailer Tesco in 1997. It is remembered in Ireland chiefly for its budget "Yellow Pack" own-brand range — Quinn is credited with coining the term — and for the flamboyant, personality-driven advertising that made its founder one of the country's best-known businessmen of the 1960s and 1970s ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)). Tesco bought the 75-store Quinnsworth and Crazy Prices chain from Associated British Foods for IR£630m, marking its entry into the Irish grocery market, and absorbed it into a growing Irish business ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/news/irelands-battle-at-the-checkouts-tesco-the-irish-experien/63032.article)).

## Founding and Pat Quinn

Pat Quinn was born in 1935 in Cloone, County Leitrim, where his father was a garda and his mother ran a pub and grocery; he was educated locally and at St Mel's College in Longford before entering retail with a Woolworth's outlet in Limerick ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)). After working with his uncle's shop in Longford, he emigrated to Canada, where he worked in Toronto department stores — rising to assistant general manager of a London, Ontario branch — while moonlighting as a music promoter who introduced acts including Johnny Cash, the Rolling Stones and the Dave Clark Five to Canadian audiences ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)).

In 1965 Quinn returned to Ireland and was recruited as general manager of the H Williams supermarket group. One of his first recommendations was that the group open a branch in the new Stillorgan shopping centre; when Williams declined, Quinn decided to open a store there himself, modelled on Woolworth's ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)). The business got off to what the obituary calls a disastrous start, but picked up when he switched to grocery — he and his wife working long hours, staying after closing to wash the floors ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)). By 1971 the chain had a turnover of £6 million, with branches in Dundrum, Rathfarnham, Ballymun in Dublin, Shannon in County Clare, Cork and Galway, competing against Dunnes Stores, Superquinn, H Williams, Liptons and Five Star ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)).

On the advice of his advertising agency, Quinn became the public face of the business, appearing in advertisements in his trademark polo-neck jumper; a journalist who watched him at work described him as "friendly, informal, approachable, likeable, straightforward, and a man of the people" ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)). He was once on the verge of hiring George Best to open a new store, but when told the fee was £1,000 he decided he was sufficiently well known to perform the opening himself — and gave the money to charity ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)).

## Yellow Pack

Quinn is credited with inventing the term "yellow pack" for his cut-price brand, and the phrase became one of the chain's most memorable contributions to Irish retailing ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)). It featured heavily in the chain's advertising and became closely associated with Quinnsworth's value positioning in the public mind. This profile follows the Irish Times obituary's attribution; a separate tertiary account credits the formal own-brand range to the British chain Fine Fare in 1980, but no independent citable source for that version was found in this research (see open items).

## Sale to Associated British Foods and Quinn's later ventures

In the early 1970s Quinn sold his controlling interest in the Quinnsworth group to Galen Weston's Associated British Foods ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)). He then pursued a series of ventures outside grocery: the Pat Quinn Club, a sports and leisure complex at Kilternan in south Dublin, which the obituary describes as "one of the most spectacular [Irish] business failures on record"; stakes in Dublin pubs including the Mooneys chain, which went into receivership; pool halls in Rathmines, Bray and Drogheda; an "executive coach service" ferrying businessmen to events; and the Shoparound Centre on South Great George's Street, which closed early in 1986 after poor Christmas trading ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)). Later that year he returned to Canada, where he and his family ran pubs and restaurants in Toronto and Montreal; he died in Toronto on November 23, 2009, aged 74 ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)).

## The Tesco acquisition and the Irish business

Tesco bought the 75-store Quinnsworth and Crazy Prices chain from Associated British Foods for IR£630m — a price some in the trade regarded as high — and subsequently invested a further IR£200m in upgrades and new technology, developing replacement stores at Maynooth and Newbridge in County Kildare and building new outlets in Killarney and Castlebar, County Mayo ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/news/irelands-battle-at-the-checkouts-tesco-the-irish-experien/63032.article)). By around 2000, Tesco employed 10,000 staff in Ireland — 2,000 more than the Quinnsworth total — and held a 23.4% share of the IR£5bn Irish grocery market according to consultants Taylor Nielsen, with sales up 6% to IR£1.12bn and business with Irish suppliers up by more than 20% since the takeover ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/news/irelands-battle-at-the-checkouts-tesco-the-irish-experien/63032.article)).

The early Tesco years were not without trouble. When the chain refused to stock Irish beef in its UK stores as a gesture of support for British farmers during the BSE crisis, it faced boycott threats in Ireland; it took what The Grocer calls an unqualified apology from managing director Maurice Pratt — the former public face of Quinnsworth — to win customers back ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/news/irelands-battle-at-the-checkouts-tesco-the-irish-experien/63032.article)). Pratt also had to apologise publicly after Tesco was prosecuted for overcharging, blaming a technology snag; the fine was nominal but the publicity damaging ([The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/news/irelands-battle-at-the-checkouts-tesco-the-irish-experien/63032.article)).

## A note on the two Quinns

Pat Quinn of Quinnsworth should not be confused with Feargal Quinn, the Dublin businessman who founded the entirely separate Superquinn supermarket chain in 1960 with a first store in Dundalk, County Louth ([ShelfLife](https://www.shelflife.ie/supermarket-legend-feargal-quinn-passes-away/)). Feargal Quinn built Superquinn into one of Ireland's biggest supermarket businesses, sold it to an investor consortium in 2005, and later served as an independent senator from 1993 to 2016; he died in April 2019 aged 82 ([Retail Week](https://www.retail-week.com/grocery/superquinn-chain-founder-feargal-quinn-dies/7031762.article); [ShelfLife](https://www.shelflife.ie/supermarket-legend-feargal-quinn-passes-away/)). The two chains were competitors — Superquinn appears among Quinnsworth's rivals in Pat Quinn's obituary — but the two founders and companies were distinct ([The Irish Times](https://web.archive.org/web/20171229112252/https://www.irishtimes.com/life-and-style/people/chain-store-founder-who-coined-term-yellow-pack-1.780770)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not assert a peak store count or market share for Quinnsworth beyond the 75-store figure reported at the 1997 acquisition, and it does not attempt a complete history of the chain's store network. It does not repeat material from an earlier version of this profile that rested solely on a source which could not be re-fetched in this research pass. It does not adjudicate the origin of the "Yellow Pack" brand beyond the Irish Times obituary's credit to Pat Quinn, and it does not assert the term's later generic use in Irish slang, which was not independently verified. Financial and staffing figures are those reported by the cited sources at the time, not current figures, and Tesco's subsequent Irish history lies outside this profile's scope.
