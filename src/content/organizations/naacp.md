## An award from a local branch, not a red carpet

In 2023, a civil-rights attorney named [Amir Ali](/people/amir-ali/) received the Justice Tribute Award — not from Hollywood and not from a national stage, but from the Arab American Civil Rights League and the NAACP's Detroit branch ([Alliance for Justice](https://afj.org/article/afj-letter-of-support-for-amir-ali/)). The same letter that records the award describes Ali's record: three Supreme Court wins for under-resourced clients, including persuading the justices that the constitutional right to counsel includes the right to appeal regardless of plea conditions ([Alliance for Justice](https://afj.org/article/afj-letter-of-support-for-amir-ali/)). It is a small, telling detail about how the NAACP works — a national organization whose local branches still hand out honors for courtroom work most awards shows will never cover.

The NAACP is the United States' oldest civil-rights organization, formed in 1909 as an interracial effort to advance justice for African Americans ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). It is headquartered in Baltimore, Maryland, and its mission is grounded in the Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments — ending slavery, guaranteeing equal protection, and protecting the right to vote ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

## Founded in outrage

The organization was born out of the 1908 Springfield race riot, when a white mob burned the homes and businesses of African Americans and lynched two Black men during a two-day siege of the Illinois capital ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). The violence spurred liberal white activists to unite with Black leaders, and on February 12, 1909 — the 100th anniversary of Abraham Lincoln's birth — the NAACP was founded ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

Its roots ran through the Niagara Movement, the group of Black intellectuals led by W.E.B. Du Bois who demanded equal civil and political rights, in sharp contrast to Booker T. Washington's more conciliatory emphasis on vocational training and economic gains ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). The founding group was dominated by white activists but included seven Black Americans, among them Du Bois, investigative journalist Ida B. Wells, and activist Mary Church Terrell ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). Despite its stated commitment to interracial leadership, Du Bois — as director of publications and research and editor of the newly launched magazine — was the only Black member of the original executive team ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

## The pen and the press

In 1910 the NAACP launched *The Crisis*, its magazine, with Du Bois as editor, guaranteeing the organization a place in the national conversation on politics and the arts ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). The magazine played a key role in supporting the Harlem Renaissance by giving a platform to the emerging African American literary scene, publishing Langston Hughes, Countee Cullen, Claude McKay, and Jean Toomer ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

One of the organization's earliest campaigns tried to ban the 1915 racist film *The Birth of a Nation*, a movie that sparked protests in a number of U.S. cities ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). The campaign ultimately failed, but it foreshadowed the street protests that would arrive later in the century ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

The NAACP also waged a decades-long campaign against lynching. Its advocacy led President Woodrow Wilson to disavow the practice in 1918, and in 1919 the organization published what was then the most comprehensive study of lynching in the country, *Thirty Years of Lynching in the United States, 1889–1919* ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). From 1920 to 1938, whenever a lynching occurred, the NAACP flew a flag outside its New York City headquarters declaring, "A man was lynched yesterday" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). Despite stalwart lobbying for anti-lynching legislation through the 1920s and 1930s, lynching was not formally declared a hate crime in the United States until the 2022 passage of the Emmett Till Antilynching Act ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

## The courtroom as a battlefield

Many of the group's victories in its first fifty years were won in court. The NAACP filed its first friend-of-the-court brief in the 1915 voting-rights case *Guinn v. United States*, in which the Supreme Court struck down discriminatory voting rules in Oklahoma 8–1 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). In 1917, *Buchanan v. Warley* struck down race-based housing segregation unanimously, and in 1946 *Morgan v. Virginia* produced a 7–1 ruling barring segregation on interstate buses — a decision that would prove essential to the Freedom Rides of the 1960s ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

The organization's most enduring legacy is its systematic legal dismantling of the "separate but equal" doctrine from *Plessy v. Ferguson*. In 1930 Walter F. White became executive secretary, and by 1935 he had recruited Charles H. Houston as general counsel; Houston, a former Howard University law vice-dean, had a protégé in a young lawyer named Thurgood Marshall ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). Houston designed an incremental strategy starting in higher education: Marshall, then head of the NAACP's Legal Defense and Educational Fund, successfully argued *Sweatt v. Painter* in 1950, and four years later, on May 17, 1954, Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote for a unanimous court in *Brown v. Board of Education* that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

## The movement years

When 14-year-old Emmett Till was murdered in Mississippi in 1955, the NAACP declared it a lynching, and a young NAACP leader, Medgar Evers, gathered evidence as Till's mother held an open-casket funeral ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). Months later, another young NAACP organizer, Rosa Parks, refused to give up her seat on a Montgomery, Alabama, bus ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

As the civil-rights movement grew, the NAACP functioned as its legal and legislative backbone — providing bail assistance, legal defense, and federal lobbying muscle — even as strategic friction arose with the more direct tactics of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee and Martin Luther King Jr.'s Southern Christian Leadership Conference ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)). The groups united for the 1963 March on Washington, and the era culminated in the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/National-Association-for-the-Advancement-of-Colored-People)).

## The Image Awards, 2026

The NAACP's annual Image Awards bill themselves as a celebration of Black excellence across film, television, music, literature, and podcasts ([MyNewsLA](https://mynewsla.com/orange-county/2026/03/01/sinners-dominates-57th-naacp-image-awards/)). The 57th edition took place on February 28, 2026, at the Pasadena Civic Auditorium, hosted by actor-comedian Deon Cole, capping a four-day event that included non-televised virtual ceremonies earlier in the week ([MyNewsLA](https://mynewsla.com/orange-county/2026/03/01/sinners-dominates-57th-naacp-image-awards/)).

The Oscar-nominated film *Sinners* dominated, winning 13 of its 18 nominations, including Outstanding Motion Picture; Michael B. Jordan took Outstanding Actor in a Motion Picture and Entertainer of the Year, while Miles Caton won for his breakthrough performance ([MyNewsLA](https://mynewsla.com/orange-county/2026/03/01/sinners-dominates-57th-naacp-image-awards/)). Colman Domingo received the President's Award for special achievement and distinguished public service, and Viola Davis was honored with the Chairman's Award for a career "defined by excellence, impact, and fearless advocacy" ([MyNewsLA](https://mynewsla.com/orange-county/2026/03/01/sinners-dominates-57th-naacp-image-awards/)). Salt-N-Pepa and DJ Spinderella were inducted into the NAACP Hall of Fame, and Samuel L. Jackson led a tribute to the late Rev. Jesse Jackson ([MyNewsLA](https://mynewsla.com/orange-county/2026/03/01/sinners-dominates-57th-naacp-image-awards/)).
