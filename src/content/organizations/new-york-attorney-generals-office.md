## Overview

The New York Attorney General's Office is the state's chief legal office, headed by the attorney general as head of the Department of Law ([Gotham Gazette](https://www.gothamgazette.com/state/6939-the-evolving-powers-of-the-new-york-attorney-general/)). What makes it unusual is how little of its power is written down: the state constitution says almost nothing about the office, noting only that the attorney general is elected "at the same general election as the governor and hold[s] office for the same term" and heads the Law Department ([Gotham Gazette](https://www.gothamgazette.com/state/6939-the-evolving-powers-of-the-new-york-attorney-general/)). The office's real authority comes from statutes and common law — and in recent decades, from attorneys general who chose to act as high-profile prosecutors rather than the state's chief lawyer ([Gotham Gazette](https://www.gothamgazette.com/state/6939-the-evolving-powers-of-the-new-york-attorney-general/)).

The modern Department of Law was created in 1926, and by FY 2002–03 it had grown to 1,869 authorized positions with a $186.9 million budget ([New York State Division of the Budget](https://ur.budget.ny.gov/pubs/archive/fy0203archive/fy0203app1/law.pdf)). The current officeholder is [Letitia James](/people/letitia-james/), who has made the office a national force — and a national target ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/new-york-sues-polymarket-says-it-ran-illegal-gambling-operation/)).

## The statute that made the office

The single most important tool in the office's arsenal is the Martin Act of 1921, which gives the attorney general vastly greater latitude in prosecuting financial crimes against shareholders than peer states' laws allow ([Gotham Gazette](https://www.gothamgazette.com/state/6939-the-evolving-powers-of-the-new-york-attorney-general/)). Among "blue sky" laws — statutes designed to protect investors from securities fraud — it is largely considered the toughest in the nation ([Gotham Gazette](https://www.gothamgazette.com/state/6939-the-evolving-powers-of-the-new-york-attorney-general/)).

The act sat mostly unused for big cases until Eliot Spitzer's 2002 settlement with Merrill Lynch, totaling $100 million over conflict-of-interest allegations — a deal seen as the pivotal point on the road to what the office has become ([Gotham Gazette](https://www.gothamgazette.com/state/6939-the-evolving-powers-of-the-new-york-attorney-general/)). Spitzer's successors kept expanding its reach: Eric Schneiderman's office filed the initial lawsuit against Trump University, cracked down on daily fantasy sports operators like DraftKings, and opened a Martin Act probe into ExxonMobil over its climate disclosures ([Gotham Gazette](https://www.gothamgazette.com/state/6939-the-evolving-powers-of-the-new-york-attorney-general/)). The visibility of these pursuits has turned the office into a springboard for higher office — both Spitzer and Andrew Cuomo jumped from attorney general to governor after tenures marked by major prosecutorial victories ([Gotham Gazette](https://www.gothamgazette.com/state/6939-the-evolving-powers-of-the-new-york-attorney-general/)).

## The Trump fraud case

The office's most consequential recent action is the civil fraud case against Donald Trump. In September 2022, James sued Trump, Trump Organization companies, and senior executives over years of alleged financial fraud; in September 2023, Justice Arthur Engoron granted her motion for partial summary judgment, finding the defendants had committed fraud by falsely inflating asset values ([Office of the New York Attorney General](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2024/attorney-general-james-wins-landmark-victory-case-against-donald-trump)). After an 11-week trial, Engoron ordered the defendants to pay more than $450 million in total — $363.8 million in disgorgement plus pre-judgment interest — barred Trump from serving as an officer or director of any New York company for three years, banned him from borrowing from New York banks for three years, and installed an independent compliance role at the Trump Organization ([Office of the New York Attorney General](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2024/attorney-general-james-wins-landmark-victory-case-against-donald-trump)).

The penalty did not survive appeal intact. On August 21, 2025, a deeply divided Appellate Division threw out the half-billion-dollar penalty while preserving the fraud case: four judges voted to let the fraud finding stand, though all found problems in the trial judge's handling of the case, and there was no majority opinion ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/trumps-civil-fraud-penalty-is-thrown-out-by-new-york-appeals-court/)). The original judgment had totaled $464.6 million — $454.2 million against Trump personally — growing with interest to about $527 million, and James said she would ask the Court of Appeals, the state's highest court, to review ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/trumps-civil-fraud-penalty-is-thrown-out-by-new-york-appeals-court/)).

## The prediction-market front

The office's newest enforcement front is prediction markets. On September 24, 2026, James and Governor Kathy Hochul sued Polymarket US (QCX LLC) in Manhattan state court under Executive Law 63(12), alleging it ran an unlicensed gambling operation in New York, marketed to 18-to-20-year-olds despite the state's 21-and-over rule for mobile sports betting, and avoided the taxes licensed operators pay ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/new-york-sues-polymarket-says-it-ran-illegal-gambling-operation/); [TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/113996-new-york-sues-polymarket-over-unlicensed-gambling-operation.html)). The suit expanded similar 2026 cases the office had already brought against Kalshi, Coinbase Financial Markets, and Gemini Titan ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/new-york-sues-polymarket-says-it-ran-illegal-gambling-operation/)).

Polymarket fought back the same day, moving the case to federal court and suing state officials — arguing the federal Commodity Futures Trading Commission has exclusive authority to regulate prediction markets and calling New York's move "an extraordinary assertion of state power squarely foreclosed by federal law" ([Reuters](https://srnnews.com/new-york-sues-polymarket-says-it-ran-illegal-gambling-operation/)). New York is seeking penalties of three times the company's gains, forfeiture of allegedly illegal profits, and restitution to users ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/113996-new-york-sues-polymarket-over-unlicensed-gambling-operation.html)).
