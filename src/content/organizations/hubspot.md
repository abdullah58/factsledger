## Overview

HubSpot is a Cambridge, Massachusetts, software company whose business is inseparable from an idea it invented: inbound marketing. Founders Brian Halligan and Dharmesh Shah started the company at MIT in 2006, built a platform that made it easy for small businesses to attract customers with useful content instead of interrupting them with ads and cold calls — and popularized the very tactics, content marketing and social-media marketing, that now sit at the center of the company's CRM platform ([Entrepreneur (India)](https://www.Entrepreneur.Com/en-in/marketing/this-company-helps-start-ups-up-their-marketing-game/344838); [MarTech](https://martech.org/topic/marketing-technology/hubspot/page/3/)). It went public on the New York Stock Exchange in October 2014 at $25 a share, raising $125 million, and rose more than 20% on its first day of trading ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/story/12909309/1/hubspot-defies-the-market-ends-first-trading-day-up-20.html); [Nasdaq (Renaissance Capital)](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/hubspot-prices-ipo-25-above-upwardly-revised-range-2014-10-08)).

## Inventing inbound marketing — and giving it away

The genesis of HubSpot, in co-founder Dharmesh Shah's own telling to Entrepreneur India, was an observation that the old marketing playbook had stopped working: "People traditionally did marketing by attending events, sponsoring food and such activities but those things were just not working" ([Entrepreneur (India)](https://www.Entrepreneur.Com/en-in/marketing/this-company-helps-start-ups-up-their-marketing-game/344838)). The founders' answer was to build a software platform — "a content management system, social media tools, Web analytics" brought under one roof — that made it easy for small businesses to "do marketing right": earning attention by creating content others share, essentially "attracting visitors naturally through search engines," rather than blasting a whole audience without knowing whether the message was useful or the audience was right ([Entrepreneur (India)](https://www.Entrepreneur.Com/en-in/marketing/this-company-helps-start-ups-up-their-marketing-game/344838)).

The distinctive move was marketing as pedagogy. When HubSpot began its blog, the Small Business Hub, "the company did not focus on selling the product but put out the ideas out there to be evaluated by consumers" — selling the idea of inbound marketing, not the software ([Entrepreneur (India)](https://www.Entrepreneur.Com/en-in/marketing/this-company-helps-start-ups-up-their-marketing-game/344838)). The bet was that teaching the category would create the category, and the company would be its default brand. MarTech, writing as an independent trade observer, confirms the bet paid off in cultural terms: HubSpot's focus on inbound marketing "helped popularize marketing tactics like content marketing and social media marketing," and a thriving community of HubSpot administrators, users, and consultants grew up around the platform, convening annually at the company's Inbound event ([MarTech](https://martech.org/topic/marketing-technology/hubspot/page/3/)).

What the platform became is a CRM organized into "hubs" — Marketing, Sales, Service, CMS, Operations, and Commerce — covering content management, social media marketing, lead generation, website analytics, SEO, live chat, email marketing, and customer support, used by more than 200,000 businesses per MarTech's account ([MarTech](https://martech.org/topic/marketing-technology/hubspot/page/3/)).

## The 2014 IPO: pricing above an already-raised range

HubSpot filed confidentially for its IPO on 28 March 2014, unprofitable but growing — $51.2–51.3 million in revenue in the first half of the year, generated by a monthly subscription service, and up nearly 50% year over year ([Nasdaq (Renaissance Capital)](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/hubspot-prices-ipo-25-above-upwardly-revised-range-2014-10-08); [TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/story/12909309/1/hubspot-defies-the-market-ends-first-trading-day-up-20.html)). Demand forced the pricing up twice: the $19–$21 range was lifted to $22–$24, and the offering ultimately priced at $25 a share for 5 million shares — $125 million raised, with Morgan Stanley, J.P. Morgan, and UBS Investment Bank as lead managers ([Nasdaq (Renaissance Capital)](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/hubspot-prices-ipo-25-above-upwardly-revised-range-2014-10-08); [TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/story/12909309/1/hubspot-defies-the-market-ends-first-trading-day-up-20.html)).

The debut was a genuine pop: on 9 October 2014, the stock opened at $32.95 and closed at $30.10, up more than 20% on a day the Nasdaq itself fell 2%, after executives rang the opening bell at the NYSE ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/hubspot-ipo); [TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/story/12909309/1/hubspot-defies-the-market-ends-first-trading-day-up-20.html)). CEO Brian Halligan described the company as "a software platform that helps businesses transform the way they market, to match the way modern humans actually shop and buy stuff today," and said management planned to use the IPO proceeds to fund international expansion ([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/story/12909309/1/hubspot-defies-the-market-ends-first-trading-day-up-20.html)).

VentureBeat's IPO-day interview with COO J.D. Sherman revealed the strategic read underneath: HubSpot had moved beyond marketing into sales territory with Sidekick — a service weaving lead information into salespeople's inboxes — which Sherman called "the second act," a way to expand the addressable market "all the while avoiding competition from big tech companies with marketing clouds like IBM and Oracle" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/hubspot-ipo)). "We're thinking about our sales product as potentially the second act for HubSpot," Sherman said — eight years in, the IPO was "more of a starting line than as a finishing line" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/technology/hubspot-ipo)).
