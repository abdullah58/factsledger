## Overview

Modern Maturity was the flagship magazine of [AARP](/organizations/aarp/), published from 1958 until 2003. Founded by AARP founder [Ethel Percy Andrus](/people/ethel-percy-andrus/), the glossy bimonthly grew into the largest-circulation magazine in the [United States](/places/united-states/) — reaching some 23 million households by the late 1990s without ever being sold on newsstands ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/modern-maturity)) — before AARP merged it with its boomer title My Generation into the singly branded AARP The Magazine. It is a distinct creative work from the [AARP Bulletin](/creative-works/aarp-bulletin/), AARP's separate newspaper-style sister publication ([Smithsonian Institution](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/america%E2%80%99s-mailing-industry-industry-segments-nonprofit-organizations/aarp)).

## Origins: a magazine planned at a kitchen table

During the summer of 1958, Ethel Percy Andrus began planning a magazine at her Ojai, California, home that would serve AARP members and project "a new image of aging" ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/about-aarp/history/modern-maturity-aarp-the-magazine/)). By AARP's account it was crucial to the launch that insurance broker Leonard Davis cut short a Disneyland vacation to help Andrus, her sister Maud, and AARP pioneer Ruth Lana perfect a mock-up of the first issue. The debut was dated October–November 1958 — it would have been the September–October issue, but Andrus judged the proposed cover's pastel pinks and blues unlikely to appeal to prospective male members and chose autumn trees instead ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/about-aarp/history/modern-maturity-aarp-the-magazine/)). The first issue went out in late 1958 to members of Andrus's other organization, the National Retired Teachers Association, who were asked to join AARP for $2 a year, subscription included ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/modern-maturity)). Davis put up $50,000 in seed money and persuaded Continental Casualty of Chicago to insure the AARP group when most insurers would not sell to anyone over 65 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/modern-maturity)).

## The circulation story: the biggest magazine America never bought

Hubert Pryor became editor in 1967 and professionalized the magazine's content, writing, and photography; circulation stood at 700,000 when he took over and reached 7 million by his 1983 retirement, when he was also an AARP associate director ([AARP Bulletin](https://www.aarp.org/advocacy/hubert-pryor-early-2008/)). Pryor had spent 15 years as a senior editor of Look and four as editor of Science Digest before AARP hired him ([AARP Bulletin](https://www.aarp.org/advocacy/hubert-pryor-early-2008/)). By the late 1990s the glossy, Time-sized bimonthly was mailed to some 23 million households comprising over 33 million AARP members — "the largest average circulation magazine in the nation — without being available on newsstands" ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/modern-maturity)). Its 1997 readers were moderately well-off: median household income $34,408, nearly two-thirds college-educated, most homeowners, women outnumbering men nearly two-to-one, median age 65.2 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/modern-maturity)). The business model was membership dues: at one point an $8 annual fee included $2.40 for Modern Maturity and 85¢ for the AARP Bulletin ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/media/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/modern-maturity)).

## Reinvention and the end of the name, 2001–2003

In January 2001 AARP redesigned Modern Maturity with a two-big-M's cover and launched a sister publication, My Generation, for boomers aged 45 to 55 ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/?p=1219888), quoting a 2001 New York Times report). AARP would shrink Modern Maturity's readership from 20.8 million to 17 million while growing My Generation from a 3.4 million maiden circulation to 10 million ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/?p=1219888)). The experiment lasted two years: with the March/April 2003 issue, AARP combined both into a singly branded AARP The Magazine, split into three demographic editions — 50–59, 60–69, and 70-plus — on a combined rate base of 21.5 million ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/7048/aarp-re-brands-titles.html?edition=33194)). That rate base was larger than TV Guide and Reader's Digest combined — member-funded distribution on a scale no newsstand title could match ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/7048/aarp-re-brands-titles.html?edition=33194)). Publisher Jim Fishman put the rationale plainly: research showed "the AARP name resonates with members, while the magazine titles meant little or nothing" — "no one writes a check to Modern Maturity," he said; "they write a check to AARP every year for membership" ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/7048/aarp-re-brands-titles.html?edition=33194)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What was Modern Maturity?**
Modern Maturity was the flagship magazine of AARP, published bimonthly from 1958 to 2003. Founded by AARP founder Ethel Percy Andrus, it was mailed to members as part of their dues and grew into the largest-circulation magazine in the United States before being folded into AARP The Magazine.

**When did Modern Maturity become AARP The Magazine?**
With the March/April 2003 issue, AARP combined Modern Maturity and its boomer sister title My Generation into a singly branded AARP The Magazine, split into 50–59, 60–69, and 70-plus editions, according to contemporary trade-press reporting — a year later than Wikipedia's 2002 date.

**How large was Modern Maturity's circulation?**
By the late 1990s, at its peak, it reached about 23 million households comprising over 33 million AARP members, all by mail — no newsstand sales. The Smithsonian's National Postal Museum confirms the successor publication as "the world's largest circulation magazine."

**Was Modern Maturity the same as the AARP Bulletin?**
No. They were distinct AARP publications: Modern Maturity was the glossy bimonthly magazine, while the AARP Bulletin was the separate newspaper-style sister publication. Membership dues were once itemized as $2.40 for Modern Maturity and 85¢ for the Bulletin — two different publications, not two names for one.

## What this profile does not claim

- It does not resolve the 2002-versus-2003 rename dating: Wikipedia dates the combination to 2002, while contemporary trade press reports the singly-branded consolidation with the March/April 2003 issue. This profile follows the trade-press dating and flags the conflict.
- Circulation figures are not directly comparable across sources — they mix copies, readers, households, and members — and the two 1967 figures (700,000 vs. "well over one million") are left as stated by their sources.
- It does not assert a precise final standalone circulation for Modern Maturity; the 20.8 million figure is from January 2001, before the final carve-up.
