## Overview

Pokémon is a Japanese media franchise created by game designer Satoshi Tajiri that began with the Game Boy games Pokémon Red and Green, released in [Japan](/places/japan/) on 27 February 1996, and grew into video games, an animated series, films and a trading card game whose cards now trade as high-value collectibles ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)).

This profile covers the franchise as a creative work — its origins, its games and cards, and its life as a collectibles market — not a catalogue of its games or card sets, and not the fictional world of the creatures themselves. The franchise is unusual in its ownership: its trademark rights are managed worldwide by Nintendo, Game Freak and Creatures together, organised by The Pokémon Company, rather than by a single owner ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)). Outside Asia, The Pokémon Company International handles trademark management, licensing, the trading card game, television series and other media products ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)).

## From a bug-collecting idea to a Game Boy hit

The creator of Pokémon is the Japanese game developer Satoshi Tajiri, born in 1965, whose inspiration was his childhood hobby of collecting insects — a pastime that earned him the nickname "Dr Bug" among friends ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)). When Tajiri saw a Game Boy with a link cable in the early 1990s, he imagined little creatures wandering through the cable, and a vision was born: collect digital creatures, trade them and let them fight each other ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)). Back in 1982, Tajiri had founded the gaming magazine Game Freak with friends, and it later became the development company of the same name that created the Pokémon games together with Nintendo and Creatures, with co-founder Ken Sugimori responsible for the iconic Pokémon illustrations ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)).

Development of the first video games took around six years and faced financial ruin more than once, but on 27 February 1996 the first editions were released in Japan ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)). Tajiri had originally wanted to call his creatures "Capsule Monsters", since they were stored in capsules in early drafts of the game, but the rights to that name were already taken, so the short form of "Pocket Monsters" — Pokémon — was born ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)). Britannica describes the series as debuting in Japan in February 1996 as Pokémon Green and Pokémon Red, and reaching the United States in 1998 as Red and Blue ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pokemon-electronic-game?utm_source=thebrief-newsletter.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=o-poder-do-anel)).

In the games, players act as Pokémon trainers, obtaining cartoon monsters and developing them to battle other Pokémon ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pokemon-electronic-game?utm_source=thebrief-newsletter.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=o-poder-do-anel)). Pokémon are divided into types, such as water and fire, each with different strengths, and with experience they grow stronger and gain new abilities, while trainers earn acclaim by defeating Gym Leaders and obtaining Gym Badges ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pokemon-electronic-game?utm_source=thebrief-newsletter.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=o-poder-do-anel)). Pikachu, a yellow mouselike creature, is the undisputed face of Pokémon and helped the series become a worldwide phenomenon, inspiring a cartoon series, movies, books, a toy line, sequels, spin-offs, a clothing line and a popular trading-card game ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pokemon-electronic-game?utm_source=thebrief-newsletter.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=o-poder-do-anel)). The game's central character, Ash Ketchum, is based on Tajiri himself: in the Japanese original he is even named "Satoshi", and "Ketchum" refers to the slogan "Catch 'em all" ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)).

## Games, anime and the second craze

From its Game Boy beginnings, the series moved across Nintendo's handheld generations, with titles produced exclusively for the Nintendo DS from 2006 and Pokémon X and Pokémon Y debuting on the Nintendo 3DS in 2013 ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pokemon-electronic-game?utm_source=thebrief-newsletter.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=o-poder-do-anel)). The franchise's second great wave came on phones rather than consoles: the mobile application Pokémon GO was a blockbuster success on its 2016 release, developed by Niantic Labs, a former division of Google, using GPS data and the phone's camera to create an augmented reality that let users capture Pokémon from real-life locations ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pokemon-electronic-game?utm_source=thebrief-newsletter.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=o-poder-do-anel)). By October 2019, Sensor Tower estimated the game had passed $3 billion in lifetime gross revenue since its 2016 launch, with approximately 541 million downloads worldwide and average revenue of nearly $5.60 per download ([Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/blog/pokemon-go-catches-3-billion-usd-in-lifetime-revenue)). Its strongest year was still its first: 2016 generated $832.4 million despite the game launching only in select countries, with the United States accounting for about $1.1 billion (36.2%) of lifetime spending to that point and Japan $884.5 million (29.4%) ([Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/blog/pokemon-go-catches-3-billion-usd-in-lifetime-revenue)).

The scale of the whole franchise by its 30th anniversary was large by any measure: The Pokémon Company had shipped over 515 million Pokémon video games and produced 85 billion trading cards since the games' inception, with cards available in 16 languages in more than 90 countries ([GameRant](https://gamerant.com/pokemon-tcg-85-billion-cards-printed/)). Success also brought friction early on: in 1999, the parents of two nine-year-old boys sued Nintendo, saying the Pokémon card game had caused the children to develop gambling problems and likening the trading-card craze to an illegal lottery ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pokemon-electronic-game?utm_source=thebrief-newsletter.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=o-poder-do-anel)).

## The card game as a collectibles market

The trading card game is where Pokémon stops behaving like an ordinary media franchise and starts behaving like an asset market. Its first sets were released in Japan in 1996 ([GameRant](https://gamerant.com/pokemon-tcg-85-billion-cards-printed/)), and in the game itself two players with decks of 60 cards each compete against each other ([DPMA](https://dpma.DE/english/our_office/publications/background/world-trade-marks/pokemon/index.html)). Demand has outrun supply for years: The Pokémon Company printed 10 billion cards worldwide from March 2025 to March 2026, yet said it had been printing at maximum capacity since early 2025, and the October 2024 launch of Pokémon TCG Pocket sparked the latest boom, with the November 2024 Surging Sparks and January 2025 Prismatic Evolutions sets quickly selling out ([PokéBeach](https://www.pokebeach.com/2026/05/pokemon-tcg-printed-10-billion-cards-in-2025-as-overwhelming-demand-outpaced-production-capacity)). The long view is starker still: Pokémon printed 43 billion cards between October 1996 and March 2022, then printed an additional 42 billion in the four years after that — essentially matching its first 25 years combined ([PokéBeach](https://www.pokebeach.com/2026/05/pokemon-tcg-printed-10-billion-cards-in-2025-as-overwhelming-demand-outpaced-production-capacity)). Scalpers make new sets increasingly hard to buy on release, and despite vastly increased production, sets sell out worldwide, leaving fans disappointed ([GameRant](https://gamerant.com/pokemon-tcg-85-billion-cards-printed/)).

That scarcity built a second-hand market with its own institutions. [Alt](/organizations/alt/), a platform launched for researching, trading and securely storing high-value sports cards, had by November 2021 expanded to support other trading cards, with first-edition Charizards already appearing on its exchange alongside sports cards ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/11/11/collectible-card-trading-platform-alt-raises-75m-as-it-eyes-other-asset-categories-and-preps-its-mobile-launch/)). Cards sold on Alt had first to be graded by an established group — [PSA](/organizations/professional-sports-authenticator/), [BGS](/organizations/beckett-grading-services/) or [SGC](/organizations/sgc/) — then sent to Alt's temperature- and light-controlled Vault, and users had stored over $70 million of cards there ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2021/11/11/collectible-card-trading-platform-alt-raises-75m-as-it-eyes-other-asset-categories-and-preps-its-mobile-launch/)).

Grading itself has become a price-maker, and this is the detail most top-ranking pages miss: at Heritage's TCG Signature Auction of 12–13 December 2025, two copies of the iconic 1st Edition Base Set Charizard #4 sold at wildly different prices despite sharing the top Gem Mint 10 grade — $550,000 in a PSA holder against $106,250 in a CGC holder ([ICv2](https://icv2.com/print/article/61222)). The PSA sale was an auction record for that card, beating the previous $420,000 PWCC sale of March 2022, and the more than $400,000 gap showed buyers treating PSA grading as the "gold standard" ([ICv2](https://icv2.com/print/article/61222)). The same auction exposed the same effect at the very top of the market: a CGC Gem Mint 9 Pikachu Illustrator Promo sold for $325,000, while a lower-graded PSA Gem Mint 7 example had sold for $900,000 in 2022 ([ICv2](https://icv2.com/print/article/61222)). At that auction four Pikachu Trophy cards, given out only at specific tournaments, fetched $450,000 (2nd Tournament, PSA 9) and $275,000, $237,000 and $225,000 (1st Tournament Trainers #2, #1 and #3) ([ICv2](https://icv2.com/print/article/61222)). More broadly, high-end Pokémon card prices hit all-time highs around the first quarter of 2022 and have since been rebalancing to find stability ([ICv2](https://icv2.com/print/article/61222)).

The record that crowns the market came in February 2026, when a rare Pokémon card fetched US$16.492 million including the buyer's premium in New Jersey, making it the most expensive Pokémon trading card ever sold at public auction ([The Malay Tribune](https://themalaytribune.com/rare-pikachu-illustrator-card-sells-for-us16-5m-sets-record-for-most-expensive-pokemon-card/)). The card was the Pikachu Illustrator, graded 10 — the highest possible grade — and one of only 39 illustration cards given to winners of a 1998 drawing contest held by the Japanese magazine CoroCoro Comic ([The Malay Tribune](https://themalaytribune.com/rare-pikachu-illustrator-card-sells-for-us16-5m-sets-record-for-most-expensive-pokemon-card/)). It had been consigned by Logan Paul, who bought it in 2021 for US$5.27 million, a purchase that earned the Guinness World Record title for the most expensive Pokémon card sold at a private sale ([The Malay Tribune](https://themalaytribune.com/rare-pikachu-illustrator-card-sells-for-us16-5m-sets-record-for-most-expensive-pokemon-card/)). For scale, the previous world-record trading card of any kind was an autographed Michael Jordan and Kobe Bryant basketball card that sold for US$12.93 million at Heritage in August 2025 ([The Malay Tribune](https://themalaytribune.com/rare-pikachu-illustrator-card-sells-for-us16-5m-sets-record-for-most-expensive-pokemon-card/)).

## What this profile does not claim

- No founding year is claimed for The Pokémon Company: tertiary summaries disagree, and no fully-read source in this pass states the year.
- The Pikachu Illustrator copy count is stated only as the 39 cards given to contest winners; wider estimates of roughly 41 copies in existence were not verified from a fully-read source and are not claimed.
- The February 2026 sale price of US$16.492 million includes the buyer's premium; the hammer price is not stated in the wire report read and is not claimed.
- Pre-2017 trading-card print totals may be imprecise, since PokéBeach itself notes Pokémon may not have kept accurate records for totals before 2017; the 43-billion/42-billion comparison is company-reported, not audited.
- ICv2's suggestion that the CGC Gem Mint 9 Illustrator buyer might profit by re-grading with PSA is ICv2's interpretation, and no re-grade is claimed to have happened.
- This profile does not rank Pokémon against other media franchises by total revenue: widely repeated "highest-grossing franchise" estimates rest on aggregator figures that were not verified from a fully-read primary source in this pass, so no such ranking is asserted.
- No per-generation game catalogue, anime episode history, or card-set checklist is attempted; the profile is a focused franchise overview, not an exhaustive one.
