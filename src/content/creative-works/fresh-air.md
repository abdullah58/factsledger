## The interviewer who made herself disappear

The most important person on *Fresh Air* is the one who tried hardest to be
forgotten. When Terry Gross took over the show at WHYY in Philadelphia in
1975, she was 24 years old and so determined to keep the focus on her guests
that she didn't even want her picture taken. "I liked to remain a little bit
unknown so that no one could say, 'Oh, she likes this,' or 'She believes
that, therefore I'm not going to listen to her interviews,'" she said in a
2024 interview. "The interviews aren't about me, they're about the guest"
([Nebraska Public Media](https://nebraskapublicmedia.org/en/news/news-articles/a-conversation-with-terry-gross-host-of-nprs-fresh-air/)).
Five decades and more than 13,000 interviews later, she has loosened — but
never abandoned — that discipline
([UGA Grady College](https://grady.uga.edu/news/fresh-air-with-terry-gross-wins-peabodys-institutional-award/)).
No standard reference profile leads with this. They describe *Fresh Air* as a
fixture of public radio; they rarely describe the deliberate invisibility of
the woman who made it one.

The show itself started as someone else's title. David Karpoff, WHYY's
program director, created *Fresh Air* in 1973 — a free-form interview and
music show airing weekday afternoons in Philadelphia — and coined the name
himself. When Gross arrived two years later, after host Judy Blanc left for a
job in New York, she found the name "corny." Karpoff's challenge was simple:
come up with something better. "I failed to come up with a good title. I'm
not great at titles," Gross admitted. "Thus, the show remained 'Fresh Air.'"
She had a practical worry too — listeners would confuse it with the Fresh Air
Fund, the New York charity that sends city children to summer camp, or assume
it was about environmental issues
([Nebraska Public Media](https://nebraskapublicmedia.org/en/news/news-articles/a-conversation-with-terry-gross-host-of-nprs-fresh-air/)).
Karpoff had hired her when she was already co-hosting a similar program at
WBFO in Buffalo, where he had previously run the station; he died in 2015,
and Gross wrote the remembrance herself
([NPR](https://www.wamc.org/2015-11-04/remembering-fresh-air-creator-david-karpoff)).

## From local afternoon show to national institution

Gross's radio career began in 1973 at Buffalo's WBFO, a CPB-funded college
station, before she joined WHYY-FM as producer and host of *Fresh Air*, then
a local, daily interview and music program
([UGA Grady College](https://grady.uga.edu/news/fresh-air-with-terry-gross-wins-peabodys-institutional-award/)).
The national arc followed: in 1985 WHYY-FM launched a weekly half-hour
edition of "Fresh Air with Terry Gross," distributed by NPR, and since 1987 a
daily one-hour national edition has been produced by WHYY-FM. By fall 2008,
during a presidential election season, it became the first non-drive-time
show in public radio history to pass five million weekly listeners — a
measure of how completely Gross's intimate, research-heavy style had colonized
American public radio's daytime hours
([UGA Grady College](https://grady.uga.edu/news/fresh-air-with-terry-gross-wins-peabodys-institutional-award/)).

The awards followed the audience. The program won its first Peabody in 1993,
and in May 2022 the Peabody Board of Jurors gave it the Institutional Award —
the honor reserved for bodies of work with "iconic impact on both the media
landscape and the public imagination" — presented by Stephen Colbert. Peabody
executive director Jeffrey Jones praised Gross's "big ears": "Her mastery of
dialogue is seen in questions that unearth the rudiments and alchemy of
artistry. *Fresh Air* is just that as an interview show. The mission is the
soul of art, not the spectacle of celebrity"
([UGA Grady College](https://grady.uga.edu/news/fresh-air-with-terry-gross-wins-peabodys-institutional-award/)).
The same announcement notes Gross's longtime co-executive producer Danny
Miller, underscoring how small and stable the operation has remained behind
the famous voice.

Succession, when it finally came, was additive rather than subtractive. On
May 4, 2023, Gross announced on air that Tonya Mosley — previously a
correspondent and host of *Here & Now* (co-produced by NPR and WBUR), a host
and Silicon Valley bureau chief at KQED, a TV correspondent for Al Jazeera
America, a Murrow Award winner, and a 2016 Emmy winner — was joining as
co-host. Gross was explicit that she would keep doing about the same number of
interviews herself; Mosley simply expands the show's range, working out of NPR
West in Los Angeles while Gross stays in Philadelphia. Dave Davies, who had
been doing interviews on the show for nearly twenty years, stays in the
family but does fewer of them
([NPR](https://www.wkyufm.org/news/news/2023-05-04/fresh-air-welcomes-new-co-host-tonya-mosley)).

## The show as a news engine

Because *Fresh Air* guests speak at length and Gross prepares forensically,
the program routinely generates the moment other outlets quote for days. The
clearest recent example is the October 31, 2023 interview with
[Adam Kinzinger](/people/adam-kinzinger/), then a former Republican
congressman promoting his memoir *Renegade: Defending Democracy and Liberty
in Our Divided Country*. Gross had introduced him as one of the two
Republicans on the House committee that investigated the January 6 attack;
mid-interview, Kinzinger described the letter in which his father's cousins
disowned him for joining the committee. "It said things like, you serve in
the devil's army, you've lost the trust of great men like Mark Levin and
[Sean Hannity](/people/sean-hannity/)," he said. "And it goes to show, to me,
the brain rot — right? — the rot going on, the absolute abuse of people that
put their trust in some of these — you know, these radio folks or these TV
folks"
([NPR](https://www.wuga.org/2023-10-31/rep-adam-kinzinger-on-investigating-jan-6-and-being-a-renegade-in-the-gop)).
A quiet cultural interview had become a news story about how the conservative
media ecosystem consumes its own — which is what *Fresh Air* has done, week
after week, for half a century: let people talk long enough to say something
they wouldn't say anywhere else.
