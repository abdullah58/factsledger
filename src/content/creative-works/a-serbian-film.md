**A Serbian Film** (*Srpski film*) is the 2010 feature debut of Serbian
director Srđan Spasojević, co-written with Aleksandar Radivojević and starring
Srđan Todorović as Miloš, a semi-retired porn star and family man who accepts
one last, well-paid "art film" job without ever seeing the script — and
descends into scenarios of escalating sexual violence
([Screen Anarchy](https://screenanarchy.com/2010/03/sxsw-2010-a-serbian-film-review.html)).
Handled worldwide by sales agent Jinga Films
([Flickering Myth](https://www.flickeringmyth.com/controversial-horror-the-bunny-game-gets-blu-ray-release-from-jinga-films/)),
the film premiered at SXSW in March 2010 and then detonated across the world's
censorship regimes: 49 BBFC cuts for a UK 18, a criminal prosecution of the
Sitges festival director in Spain (charges dropped), outright bans in Norway,
Australia and New Zealand, and a court ban at the San Sebastián horror festival
([Participations](https://www.participations.org/11-02-06-weir.pdf);
[Screen Anarchy](https://screenanarchy.com/amp/2012/02/charges-against-sitges-festival-director-angel-sala-dropped.html);
[The Norway Post](https://www.tnp.no/norway/culture/2850-these-films-were-banned-in-norway-last-year/)).

For clarity: this profile is about the **2010 film** — not about Serbia as a
country, and not a plot synopsis. It documents the film's documented real-world
legal history and keeps plot description to what the cited classification
documents and contemporary reviews themselves state.

## A debut engineered for infamy

Spasojević's debut arrived at SXSW in March 2010 already carrying the
reputation it would never shed: Screen Anarchy's reviewer called it one of the
most raw and transgressive films he had ever seen, while arguing it was "no
mere exploitation film" but a satire with the substance to withstand the
firestorm it triggered
([Screen Anarchy](https://screenanarchy.com/2010/03/sxsw-2010-a-serbian-film-review.html)).
The credits are straightforward — directed by Srđan Spasojević, screenplay by
Aleksandar Radivojević and Spasojević, with Srđan Todorović, Sergej Trifunović,
Jelena Gavrilović and Slobodan Bestić in the cast
([Screen Anarchy](https://screenanarchy.com/2010/03/sxsw-2010-a-serbian-film-review.html);
[New Zealand OFLC](https://dn710604.ca.archive.org/0/items/office-of-film-and-literature-classification_1200259.000/117360_35_4_82659.pdf)).
Spasojević himself has described the film publicly as "a diary of our own
molestation by the Serbian government" — an allegory of leaders who hypnotise
people into acts they would not choose — a framing the censors' own documents
note while ruling against the film anyway
([New Zealand OFLC](https://dn710604.ca.archive.org/0/items/office-of-film-and-literature-classification_1200259.000/117360_35_4_82659.pdf)).
The peer-reviewed University of Leicester study that pairs the film with
[The Human Centipede 2 (Full Sequence)](/creative-works/the-human-centipede-2/)
notes that formal comparisons between the two films' BBFC ordeals began even
before the Centipede sequel had secured classification
([Participations](https://www.participations.org/11-02-06-weir.pdf)).

## The bans, territory by territory

The United Kingdom cut first and deepest: Westminster Council required advance
BBFC classification before the film's scheduled uncut FrightFest premiere on 29
August 2010, the BBFC demanded 49 individual cuts across 11 scenes, the
premiere was pulled, and the resubmitted version was granted an 18 certificate
with 4 minutes 12 seconds removed — promptly labelled the most cut and most
censored UK cinema release in 16 years
([Participations](https://www.participations.org/11-02-06-weir.pdf)). Australia
first granted an R18+ on 5 April 2011, but the Minister for Justice sought a
review, and on 19 September 2011 the Classification Review Board unanimously
refused classification, ruling that the sexual violence, incest themes and
depictions of child sexual abuse had an impact "very high and not justified by
context" — making the film unsellable, unhirable and unadvertisable across the
country ([Australian CRB](https://sup1a9wrlpyh5li9ro.vcmarry.top/web/20120106034451/http://classification.gov.au/www/cob/rwpattach.nsf/VAP/(9A5D88DBA63D32A661E6369859739356)~Media+release+-+announcing+review+decision+-+A+Serbian+Film.pdf/$file/Media+release+-+announcing+review+decision+-+A+Serbian+Film.pdf)).
New Zealand's Classification Office followed on 24 May 2012, rating the DVD
"Objectionable" with no excisions recommended — an outright ban — on grounds
including extreme sexual violence, sexual conduct with children and young
persons, and necrophilia, with the office's published reasoning weighing the
injury-to-the-public-good test against the film's disputed claims to merit
([New Zealand OFLC](https://dn710604.ca.archive.org/0/items/office-of-film-and-literature-classification_1200259.000/117360_35_4_82659.pdf)).
Norway's Mediatilsynet banned it for containing violence with the sexualisation
of children, consultant Ove Wathne telling the Norwegian press that this was
the basis for the authority's assessment
([The Norway Post](https://www.tnp.no/norway/culture/2850-these-films-were-banned-in-norway-last-year/)).
The stat, source and implication: 49 cuts and 4m 12s (Participations, citing
BBFC records) versus 32 cuts and 2m 37s for The Human Centipede 2 (BBFC, via
4RFV) — implying that between 2010 and 2011 the BBFC's appetite for salvage-by-editing
grew even as its headline rhetoric about "uncuttable" films stayed severe.

## The prosecution that wasn't

Spain produced the film's strangest legal episode. After an adults-only
midnight screening at the Sitges Film Festival in October 2010, a complaint
from a Roman Catholic organisation led the Spanish prosecutor, in May 2011, to
charge festival director Àngel Sala with exhibiting child pornography — a
charge carrying potential jail time
([Screen Anarchy](https://screenanarchy.com/amp/2012/02/charges-against-sitges-festival-director-angel-sala-dropped.html)).
Separate court orders also barred the film from the San Sebastián horror
festival's 21st Semana de Cine Fantástico y de Terror, and screenings were
cancelled at other Spanish venues
([Screen Anarchy](https://screenanarchy.com/amp/2012/02/charges-against-sitges-festival-director-angel-sala-dropped.html)).
In February 2012, El País reported that the charges had been dropped: the
authorities ruled the film was not pornographic, placed it within the "gore"
genre, and concluded that even with its graphic content it was "quite clearly
speaking against" the acts it depicted
([Screen Anarchy](https://screenanarchy.com/amp/2012/02/charges-against-sitges-festival-director-angel-sala-dropped.html)).
The Sitges festival's own statement expressed satisfaction with the dismissal
and committed to continuing the debate around the limits of film and freedom of
expression ([Screen Anarchy](https://screenanarchy.com/amp/2012/02/charges-against-sitges-festival-director-angel-sala-dropped.html)).
Unlike [The Bunny Game](/creative-works/the-bunny-game/) — which the BBFC
refused outright and which never recovered a UK certificate — A Serbian Film
survived every jurisdiction's process in some cut form except the ones that
banned it entirely.

## Frequently asked questions

**Who made A Serbian Film?**
It is the feature debut of Serbian director Srđan Spasojević, co-written with Aleksandar Radivojević, starring Srđan Todorović as Miloš, a semi-retired porn star, alongside Sergej Trifunović and Jelena Gavrilović. It premiered at SXSW in March 2010, and Jinga Films handled it as world sales agent.

**Why was it banned in so many countries?**
The bans cluster around sexual violence and the sexualisation of children: the UK's BBFC required 49 cuts across 11 scenes (4m 12s) before granting an 18; Australia reversed an R18+ to Refused Classification in September 2011; New Zealand rated it Objectionable in May 2012; Norway's Mediatilsynet banned it for violence combined with the sexualisation of children.

**Was anyone actually prosecuted over the film?**
Yes, in Spain. After an adults-only midnight screening at the 2010 Sitges Film Festival, the Spanish prosecutor charged festival director Àngel Sala with exhibiting child pornography in May 2011 following a complaint from a Roman Catholic organisation. The charges were dropped in February 2012 after authorities ruled the film was not pornographic and fell within the "gore" genre.

**Did the film get banned in Spain itself?**
Courts barred it from the San Sebastián horror festival and other Spanish screenings were cancelled, but the theatrical version was not permanently banned nationwide; the DVD version received a Pelicula "X" classification. (Note: the nationwide-ban details appeared only in tertiary sources and are not asserted here.)

**What did the director say the film was about?**
Spasojević has described it as "a diary of our own molestation by the Serbian government" — an allegory of monolithic power hypnotising people into acts they would not choose. New Zealand's censors noted the film's disputed claims to merit while ruling against it anyway.
