## The sitcom with a particle physicist in the writers' room

No reference page for The Big Bang Theory leads with its strangest credit: the
show employed a working particle physicist to keep its science honest. David
Saltzberg, a professor of physics and astronomy at UCLA, served as the series'
technical adviser, and much of the show's scientific texture — the equations
scrawled across the apartment whiteboards, the cutting-edge topics dropped into
dialogue — came from him
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).
The fandom wikis that dominate page one will tell you the episode count. They
will not tell you that a real scientist vetted the jokes.

Saltzberg described the job modestly. He did not develop characters, write
dialogue, or make jokes, he said — the writers, whom he compared to principal
investigators running a lab, kept all creative control
([Scienceline](https://scienceline.org/2010/11/the-science-of-the-big-bang-theory/)).
What he did was supply science on request: "freshman" physics factoids, or a
fresh scrawl of real formulas for the whiteboards each episode. "There's a
small community of viewers that reads those boards each week and there are
little treats in there for them," he said
([Scienceline](https://scienceline.org/2010/11/the-science-of-the-big-bang-theory/)).
He also liked weaving in physics that was current at the time of writing. In
one episode, Sheldon puzzled over why electrons in graphene behave as if they
have no mass; eight months later, the [Nobel Prize](/events/nobel-prize/) in Physics went to two
British scientists for graphene research
([Scienceline](https://scienceline.org/2010/11/the-science-of-the-big-bang-theory/)).

His motive was outreach, not Hollywood. "My hidden agenda is education," he
joked, noting that a physics talk reaches a hundred people while the show
reached millions: "Even if I gave a physics talk to a hundred people, I would
have to do 100,000 talks per week to reach the number of people this show
reaches"
([Scienceline](https://scienceline.org/2010/11/the-science-of-the-big-bang-theory/)).
The show returned the favor in kind, putting real scientists on screen as
guests — Nobel laureate George Smoot, Apple co-founder Steve Wozniak, and in
later years figures like Bill Nye and Stephen Hawking
([Scienceline](https://scienceline.org/2010/11/the-science-of-the-big-bang-theory/);
[phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).

## A Nobel Prize for a theory that doesn't exist

The series staked its finale on Saltzberg's craft. Across the final season,
Sheldon and Amy chase a Nobel Prize in Physics for "Super-Asymmetry," a theory
that exists nowhere in the real literature — the name was inspired by
supersymmetry, the genuine (and still unproven) proposal that every subatomic
particle has a heavier partner
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).
The showrunners asked Saltzberg to invent a discovery that was highly
plausible, grounded in cutting-edge science, but undiscovered; Super-Asymmetry
was his answer, and the script even has Leonard note that no papers mention it
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).

The in-story breakthrough arrives almost as a parable of the show's method:
Sheldon, stuck on his equations, realizes from a crooked bow tie that
describing an imperfect world requires building imperfection into the theory —
"instead of supersymmetry, it would be super asymmetry"
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).
A physicist writing about the finale noted the idea's real-world resonance:
his own research on "asymmetry-induced symmetry" showed that interacting
systems can synchronize precisely because their components differ
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).

## Twelve seasons, 279 episodes, one Chairman's Award

The show itself ran twelve seasons and 279 episodes on CBS, produced by Warner
Bros. Television, ending May 16, 2019 — a run its finale coverage described as
the longest for a multicamera sitcom on television
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).
It centered on theoretical physicist Sheldon Cooper and experimental physicist
Leonard Hofstadter, Caltech roommates whose fluency in quantum physics
exceeded their fluency in ordinary social life, alongside aerospace engineer
Howard Wolowitz and astrophysicist Raj Koothrappali
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).

The industry noticed early. In February 2010 the [National Association of
Broadcasters](https://factsledger.com/organizations/national-association-of-broadcasters/)
announced that Jim Parsons, the show's star, would receive the 2010 NAB
Television Chairman's Award — an honor for a significant breakthrough in
television — at the NAB Show luncheon that April in Las Vegas
([National Association of
Broadcasters](https://www.nab.org/documents/newsroom/pressRelease.asp?id=2191)).
The NAB's release is also the cleanest primary-source credit for the show's
authorship: "The Big Bang Theory," co-created by Chuck Lorre and [Bill
Prady](https://factsledger.com/people/bill-prady/)
([National Association of
Broadcasters](https://www.nab.org/documents/newsroom/pressRelease.asp?id=2191)).
By then the show had already collected a Television Critics Association award
for outstanding achievement in comedy, an American Film Institute program-of-the-
year selection for 2009, and a People's Choice Award for favorite TV comedy
([National Association of
Broadcasters](https://www.nab.org/documents/newsroom/pressRelease.asp?id=2191)).

The show's footprint may have extended beyond entertainment. A 2011 Guardian
report suggested, anecdotally, that the series was helping lift physics-major
enrollment — teenagers telling physicists the show was their way in
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).

## The criticism the show couldn't laugh off

The same finale coverage that praised the science leveled a serious charge:
the show's weakest point was its use of misogyny as a punchline and the lack
of diversity in its main cast, a sensitive issue in a field already struggling
with dropout rates among underrepresented groups
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).
The show did evolve — later seasons centered leading women, including
microbiologist Bernadette and, most consequentially, neurobiologist Amy Farrah
Fowler, the online-dating match who became Sheldon's wife in the season 11
finale and his Nobel co-winner in the series finale
([phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-05-big-theory-sheldon-amy-fictional.amp)).

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not assert the show's syndication economics or reported
billion-dollar cable deals — none were verified to citation standard. It does
not describe the unaired original pilot, which surfaced only in snippet-level
sources. It does not claim Parsons's later Emmy wins; the February 2010 NAB
release calls him a nominee, and no read source in this profile covers what
followed. The "longest-running multicamera sitcom" description is the finale
coverage's characterization, repeated here as such.
