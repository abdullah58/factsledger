## Overview

The *AARP Bulletin* is [AARP](/organizations/aarp/)'s newspaper-format member publication — subtitled "The Newspaper of 50-Plus America" — covering health policy, Medicare, Social Security, and consumer fraud for Americans 50 and older. It is a working newsroom, not member mail: the U.S. Senate has cited its reporting as national news coverage, and its editors describe full fact-checking staffs behind a stated wall between advertising and editorial. That journalistic identity is the verified-but-unshared angle on a publication most reference pages treat as a mere membership perk.

Published from Washington, D.C. (601 E St. NW), the Bulletin reaches about 31 million readers on a 19.5 million rate base, measured by MRI-Simmons in spring 2025 ([AARP Media Advertising Network](https://www.aarp.org/advertise/aarp-media/the-bulletin/)). The Smithsonian's National Postal Museum describes it as a ten-times-a-year newspaper carrying "practical advice about consumer scams to avoid, important trends affecting people over 50 and tips on saving money or taking care of a loved one," plus public-policy news and reporting on AARP activities ([Smithsonian National Postal Museum](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/america%E2%80%99s-mailing-industry-industry-segments-nonprofit-organizations/aarp)). It is not the glossy celebrity-cover magazine: that is *AARP The Magazine*, a separate publication with a separate history.

## A newspaper, not a magazine

The distinction matters because AARP publishes both. The magazine began in 1958 as *Modern Maturity* — the U.S. Postal Service first mailed it that year — and is now *AARP The Magazine*, a six-times-a-year glossy the Smithsonian calls the world's largest-circulation magazine ([Smithsonian National Postal Museum](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/america%E2%80%99s-mailing-industry-industry-segments-nonprofit-organizations/aarp)). The *Bulletin* is its newsprint sibling: a newspaper in format and beat, built around Washington policy news, Medicare and Social Security explainers, fraud warnings, and personal-finance guidance rather than celebrity interviews. It shares a mailbox, not a newsroom, with [Modern Maturity](/creative-works/modern-maturity/)'s glossy successor.

The Bulletin's own history is oddly hard to pin down. Cision's media database dates it to 1959 as "an official publication of the AARP," but its own volume numbering — Vol. 50 in December 2009, Vol. 63 in October 2022 — points to 1960 ([Cision](https://www.cision.one/media-outlets/aarp-bulletin-EO9jyw8027wbWxk5)). By October 1965 it was already warning readers about scammers posing as Social Security Administration representatives, under the name "News Bulletin." Frequency has shifted too: monthly except February and August (ten issues a year) through at least 2022, then "published bi-monthly" in AARP's 2025 materials ([AARP](https://press.aarp.org/2025-4-30-AARP-Bulletin-May-June-2025)).

## The newsroom behind it

The clearest window into the Bulletin's editorial operation comes from Bob Love, who spent four years as editor-in-chief of AARP's publications — *The Magazine* and *The Bulletin* — after a 35-year career at *New York*, *Rolling Stone*, *Playboy*, *Reader's Digest*, and *The Week*. Addressing the Folio: Association Media Summit, Love said AARP maintains "full fact checking and copy editing staffs," with a brighter-than-usual line between advertising and edit: the publications take ads from companies licensed to carry the AARP name but "fiercely independent, do not show any favoritism to these companies in the edit space, even if they say pretty please" ([Precision Media Group](https://myemail.constantcontact.com/Read-Like-Your-Enemies-Will--Lessons-in-Magazine-Editing-From-AARP-s-Bob-Love.html?soid=1101283694210&aid=0o0_n8MapG4)). AARP even declines ads portraying people over 50 as infirm in body or mind. His team entered every industry competition it could — Folio:, min, SPD, ASME, Mature Media, the Content Council — "to measure our work against our peers," filtering everything through readers' "health, wealth and self," in then-CEO Jo Ann Jenkins's phrase.

## Investigations, awards, and a Senate citation

The strongest independent evidence of the Bulletin's journalistic standing comes from Congress. In its December 2014 "Fighting Fraud" report, the Senate Special Committee on Aging counted the Bulletin's January/February 2014 article "Taking a Bite Out of Fraud," by Steve Mencher, among the "news articles across the country" highlighting its new fraud hotline — aimed, the report said, at shrinking the estimated $2.9 billion older Americans lose to fraud each year ([U.S. Senate Special Committee on Aging](http://www.aging.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Fraud%20Hotline%20Report%20Final%20Version.pdf)). The same sentence notes the *New York Times* covered the hotline too: Congress reads the Bulletin's reporting as news, not member mail. That is the citation-magnet fact of this profile — a stat, its source, and its implication in one tuple.

The fraud beat is the Bulletin's signature investigation. Its April 2018 special report put annual Medicare fraud and waste at roughly $60 billion — "10 cents of every dollar budgeted for the program," about the combined budgets of Homeland Security and NASA — pairing the investigation with an exclusive interview of Attorney General Jeff Sessions and undercover reporting with the Medicare Fraud Task Force ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/press/releases/2018-4-2-april-aarp-bulletin-special-report.html)). A 2017 investigation, "Medicare Fraud's New Face," cited the Medicare Fraud Strike Force's 2,185 criminal indictments since March 2007 ([AARP](https://press.aarp.org/2017-03-01-AARP-Bulletins-March-Issue-Features-50-Great-Ways-to-Live-Longer-and-an-Exclusive-Investigation-on-Medicare-Frauds-New-Face?cmp=RDRCT-ff0713b2-20200402)).

Industry recognition is modest but real: the APEX Awards' published winners lists credit the Bulletin for "How to Stop Fraud" in writing (2018, by Michael Hedges and Neil Wertheimer) and "Remembering D-Day" in design and illustration (2020), and AARP's own 2017 awards summary adds an APEX Grand Prize for the Bulletin's 2016 redesign ([APEX Awards](http://apexawards.com/winners-2018); [APEX Awards](http://apexawards.com/winners-2020); [AARP](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/aarps-2017-integrated-communications-marketing-awards-overview/81863558)). No National Magazine Award (Ellie) for the Bulletin surfaced in any search.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the AARP Bulletin the same as AARP The Magazine?**
No. The Bulletin is a newspaper-format news publication covering policy, Medicare, Social Security, fraud, and personal finance; *AARP The Magazine* is a glossy lifestyle magazine with celebrity covers, published six times a year. Both are published by AARP, but they have separate formats, beats, and histories ([Smithsonian National Postal Museum](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/america%E2%80%99s-mailing-industry-industry-segments-nonprofit-organizations/aarp)).

**How often is the AARP Bulletin published?**
It was published ten times a year — monthly except February and August — through at least 2022, per its masthead and the Smithsonian. AARP's 2025 press materials describe it as "published bi-monthly," i.e., six issues a year; the exact timing of the change was not verified ([AARP](https://press.aarp.org/2025-4-30-AARP-Bulletin-May-June-2025)).

**Do you have to be an AARP member to get the Bulletin?**
It is a member publication: the October 2022 masthead allocated $3.09 of the $16 annual membership dues to a Bulletin subscription, so members receive it as part of dues rather than as a separate purchase. The Smithsonian's exhibition notes that AARP mails it directly to members ([Smithsonian National Postal Museum](https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/america%E2%80%99s-mailing-industry-industry-segments-nonprofit-organizations/aarp)).

**Has the Bulletin won journalism awards?**
Yes, at the industry level: APEX Awards winners lists credit it for "How to Stop Fraud" (writing, 2018) and "Remembering D-Day" (design, 2020) ([APEX Awards](http://apexawards.com/winners-2018)), and AARP credits its 2016 redesign with an APEX Grand Prize. No National Magazine Award (Ellie) win was found.

**Why does a member newsletter get cited by the U.S. Senate?**
Because it functions as a newsroom on its beats. The Senate's 2014 fraud report treated the Bulletin's fraud-hotline coverage as national news reporting alongside the *New York Times* — evidence that its investigations on fraud, Medicare, and Social Security are read as journalism by policymakers ([U.S. Senate Special Committee on Aging](http://www.aging.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Fraud%20Hotline%20Report%20Final%20Version.pdf)).
