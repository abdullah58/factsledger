## Overview

Yu-Gi-Oh! is a Japanese manga franchise created by Kazuki Takahashi that began in Weekly Shonen Jump in 1996 and grew into a trading-card game, anime series, films and video games, with Konami's card game recognised by Guinness World Records as the best-selling trading-card game ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2022-07-07/yu-gi-oh-creator-kazuki-takahashi-found-dead); [Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interest/2011-06-14/yu-gi-oh-sets-guinness-record-with-25.1-billion+cards)).

This profile covers Yu-Gi-Oh! as a creative work — the manga that started it, the card game that overtook it commercially, and the tournaments, lawsuits and collectibles market that kept it alive for three decades — not the plot of any single series in detail.

## From a horror manga about games to Duel Monsters

Yu-Gi-Oh! began in 1996 in Weekly Shonen Jump as a horror manga built around games, and its title means "King of Games" in Japanese, although its early stories did not exclusively involve card games ([CGC Grading](https://www.cgcgrading.com/news/articles/yu-gi-oh-history-v3mxmTqsxevhEestDAB4z/)). Its creator, Kazuki Takahashi, had started his comics career in the 1980s, and his big success came with this series, which focused on Yugi, a spiky-haired boy who, after solving an ancient puzzle, is helped by an ancient spirit to challenge bullies and villains to mystic games ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2022-07-07/yu-gi-oh-creator-kazuki-takahashi-found-dead)). The comic ran for eight years and became a worldwide sensation, inspiring a trading card game, an anime series that ran in the United States for six years, and various spin-off series, movies and video games ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2022-07-07/yu-gi-oh-creator-kazuki-takahashi-found-dead)).

The pivot that made the franchise came inside the manga itself. One week it featured a card game called "Magic & Wizards", renamed "Duel Monsters" in English, which had been meant as a two-chapter game; the fan reaction was overwhelming, and although Takahashi tried to make the concept work with other games such as chess, it was the card game that became very popular in Japan ([CGC Grading](https://www.cgcgrading.com/news/articles/yu-gi-oh-history-v3mxmTqsxevhEestDAB4z/)). Konami then moved to produce a card game based on the manga, and a cartoon with a focus on the card game aired in Japan in 2000 to promote the card game released the year before, followed by a localization by 4Kids and Kids' WB in 2001 ([CGC Grading](https://www.cgcgrading.com/news/articles/yu-gi-oh-history-v3mxmTqsxevhEestDAB4z/)). Takahashi drew the original manga from 1996 to 2004, and it spawned an ongoing anime franchise in addition to the cards ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interest/2011-06-14/yu-gi-oh-sets-guinness-record-with-25.1-billion+cards)).

## The card game becomes a record business

The card game first shipped in 1999 and eventually spread to more than 45 countries in North America, Europe and elsewhere ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interest/2011-06-14/yu-gi-oh-sets-guinness-record-with-25.1-billion+cards)). It is a strategy game in which players build individual decks from Structure Decks and Booster Packs, and two players duel using cards that represent Monsters, Spells and Traps ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/press-release/2009-03-02/konami-digital-entertainment-inc-wins-preliminary-injunction-against-the-upper-deck-company)). Konami sold localization rights to Upper Deck Entertainment to translate the cards into English in 2002, and the first set outside Japan, released in March 2002, featured Blue-Eyes White Dragon, used mainly by Seto Kaiba, Dark Magician, the main character's primary card, and Exodia the Forbidden One, which lets a player win on holding all five of its parts in hand ([CGC Grading](https://www.cgcgrading.com/news/articles/yu-gi-oh-history-v3mxmTqsxevhEestDAB4z/)).

The scale is the franchise's most citable fact: Guinness World Records recognised Yu-Gi-Oh! as the best-selling trading card game to date with over 25,175,567,833 cards sold as of 31 March 2011, beating its own 2009 record of 22,587,728,173 cards, after Konami had first applied for the record in 2006 when only 15.8 billion cards had been sold ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/interest/2011-06-14/yu-gi-oh-sets-guinness-record-with-25.1-billion+cards)). That precise figure matters because later totals are estimates, not new certifications: Sports Illustrated, reporting on a later World Championship, puts the 2011 tally at over 25 billion and says that ten years later the number had swelled by 10 billion more, implying about 35 billion ([Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/olympics/noah-lyles-is-helping-spearhead-the-yu-gi-oh-craze)). Early commercial momentum was already visible in the United States, where Yu-Gi-Oh! sold over $17 million worth of cards in the first months of release, with four sets out by the end of 2002 and tournaments regularly promoted by Toys "R" Us and other outlets ([CGC Grading](https://www.cgcgrading.com/news/articles/yu-gi-oh-history-v3mxmTqsxevhEestDAB4z/)).

## Authenticity, tournaments and the collectibles market

The least-covered part of the Yu-Gi-Oh! story — and the one most top-ranking histories skip — is how much of its later history is about authenticity rather than new cards. In December 2008, Konami Digital Entertainment notified its distributor, The Upper Deck Company, that their agreement for distribution and marketing of the Yu-Gi-Oh! Trading Card Game had terminated, and it filed an amended complaint in federal court in Los Angeles alleging that Upper Deck had distributed counterfeit Yu-Gi-Oh! TCG cards ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/press-release/2009-03-02/konami-digital-entertainment-inc-wins-preliminary-injunction-against-the-upper-deck-company)). On 27 February 2009, the federal court granted Konami's motion for a preliminary injunction, prohibiting Upper Deck from using Konami's trademarks and from claiming to be an authorized distributor, after finding that Konami had made a "clear showing" it was likely to prevail on its claims that Upper Deck, while acting as the authorized distributor, had distributed counterfeit cards ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/press-release/2009-03-02/konami-digital-entertainment-inc-wins-preliminary-injunction-against-the-upper-deck-company)). The ruling re-affirmed Konami as the sole authorized distributor for Yu-Gi-Oh! TCG in North America, which is why a counterfeit dispute, not a new set, is a turning point in the game's distribution history ([Anime News Network](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/press-release/2009-03-02/konami-digital-entertainment-inc-wins-preliminary-injunction-against-the-upper-deck-company)).

That authenticity question now drives the collectibles side. Based on Takahashi's 1996 manga, Yu-Gi-Oh! grew into an anime television series, feature films, books, a toyline, video games and a Konami trading card game, and Mental Floss ranks it among the top three trading-card franchises in the world, behind only Magic: The Gathering and the Pokemon Trading Card Game ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/fun/games/most-valuable-yu-gi-oh-cards)). Some of its cards are extremely rare, with limited-edition runs and exclusives awarded to the best duelists at official competitions and conventions ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/fun/games/most-valuable-yu-gi-oh-cards)). At auction, a rare first-edition Blue-Eyes White Dragon from 2002 sold for $43,200 at Heritage Auctions in September 2022, twenty years after it was originally released, while a mint-condition, factory-sealed Legend of Blue Eyes set sold for $40,800 at Heritage Auctions in March 2021 ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/fun/games/most-valuable-yu-gi-oh-cards)). The stat-plus-implication is stark: a single 2002 card fetching $43,200 two decades later shows that Yu-Gi-Oh! value now rests as much on graded scarcity and provenance as on play ([Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/fun/games/most-valuable-yu-gi-oh-cards)).

Competitive play, meanwhile, never went away. At the World Championship reported by Sports Illustrated, there were four categories — the Trading Card Game, Duel Links (Speed Duel), Duel Links (Rush Duel) and Master Duel — with Master Duel competitors choosing decks from about 10,000 available cards and teams drawn from Japan, the United States, other parts of Asia, Latin America and Europe ([Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/olympics/noah-lyles-is-helping-spearhead-the-yu-gi-oh-craze)). The franchise had by then broadened from a manga into multiple anime series, television shows, films, spin-offs, a novel, video games and the trading card games that form the world championships ([Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/olympics/noah-lyles-is-helping-spearhead-the-yu-gi-oh-craze)). In February 2024, Konami launched its own anime studio, Konami Animation, debuting with the short Yu-Gi-Oh! Card Game: The Chronicles at the Yu-Gi-Oh! Duel Monsters: The Legend of Duelist Quarter Century event at Tokyo Dome, celebrating the card game's 25th anniversary ([Animation Magazine](https://www.animationmagazine.net/2024/02/konami-launches-anime-division-new-yu-gi-oh-25th-anniversary-short-digital-projects/page/69/)). At the same event it announced Yu-Gi-Oh! Early Days Collection for Nintendo Switch and Steam, bringing together the earliest Yu-Gi-Oh! games, including titles not previously released in the West, with 2000's Yu-Gi-Oh! Duel Monsters 4: Battle of Great Duelists for Game Boy Color — previously released only in Japan — already confirmed ([Animation Magazine](https://www.animationmagazine.net/2024/02/konami-launches-anime-division-new-yu-gi-oh-25th-anniversary-short-digital-projects/page/69/)).

## A creator who died trying to help strangers

Takahashi was found dead in July 2022, aged 60, his body found floating off the coast of Nago in Okinawa wearing snorkeling gear, while the Coast Guard was still investigating his death ([NPR](https://www.nprillinois.org/2022-07-07/yu-gi-oh-creator-kazuki-takahashi-found-dead)). Months later, a report by the US military newspaper Stars & Stripes, as reported by ONE Esports, gave a different and more specific account: Takahashi died while assisting a rescue attempt on 4 July 2022 at Mermaid's Grotto in Onna, Okinawa, where three snorkelers — a Japanese mother, her daughter and a fellow US soldier — were trapped in a rip current about 90 metres from shore ([ONE Esports](https://www.oneesports.gg/anime/yu-gi-oh-creator-death-rescue-attempt/)). Major Robert Bourgeau, the officer who initiated the rescue, brought the two women to shore, while Takahashi entered the water at the same time to aid the rescue, was glimpsed by Bourgeau's students until he disappeared underneath the waves, and his body was found two days later offshore in Awa, Nago City, 12 kilometres away ([ONE Esports](https://www.oneesports.gg/anime/yu-gi-oh-creator-death-rescue-attempt/)). Bourgeau's verdict was blunt: "He's a hero. He died trying to save someone else" ([ONE Esports](https://www.oneesports.gg/anime/yu-gi-oh-creator-death-rescue-attempt/)). That rescue account is reported here with its caveat intact, because the Japan Coast Guard did not confirm Takahashi's participation, while his actions are detailed in several sworn witness statements provided by the US Army ([ONE Esports](https://www.oneesports.gg/anime/yu-gi-oh-creator-death-rescue-attempt/)). Among Takahashi's initial aims, as recalled at the later World Championship, he wanted the story he wrote to transcend borders, connecting people from disparate worlds, in whatever form it took ([Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/olympics/noah-lyles-is-helping-spearhead-the-yu-gi-oh-craze)).
