## Overview

*Khosrov and Shirin* is a Persian romantic epic by [Nizami Ganjavi](/people/nizami-ganjavi/), completed about 1180 as the second poem of his five-poem *Khamsa* ([The Morgan Library & Museum](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/treasures-of-islamic-manuscript-painting/102)). It retells the love of the Sasanian king Khosrow II for Shirin, a princess whom most of the sources read here identify as Armenian, through long separation, rivalry, and a tragic ending ([Brooklyn Museum](https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/objects/157598); [The Morgan Library & Museum](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/treasures-of-islamic-manuscript-painting/104)).

Nizami did not invent the story. An earlier, briefer telling appears in Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*, where Khosrow figures as a flawed king; Nizami expanded that episode into a full narrative poem and made Shirin a loyal lover who waits for an inconstant Khosrow ([Cahiers d'Asie centrale](https://journals.openedition.org/asiecentrale/2958?lang=en)). Cambridge University Library describes the poem as perhaps the most loved and least enigmatic of Nizami's demanding works, and as an analysis of how different kinds of love affect different characters ([Cambridge University Library](https://exhibitions.lib.cam.ac.uk/ywa/artifacts/khusraw/)).

## What the poem is and where it sits in the Khamsa

Nizami Ganjavi, who lived about 1141–1209 according to the Morgan Library, wrote in Persian from Ganja, and his five long narrative poems are usually collected as one *Khamsa*, or Quintet ([The Morgan Library & Museum](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/treasures-of-islamic-manuscript-painting/102); [Cambridge University Library](https://exhibitions.lib.cam.ac.uk/ywa/artifacts/khusraw/)). *Khosrov and Shirin* is its second poem, dated 1180 in the Morgan's catalogue entries, and it belongs to the *masnavi* form of rhyming couplets used for extended narrative ([The Morgan Library & Museum](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/treasures-of-islamic-manuscript-painting/102)). The other poems include the *Treasury of Mysteries*, *Layla and Majnun*, the *Seven Beauties*, and the *Book of Alexander*.

The poem's distinction lies in its psychological contrast. Cambridge's reading presents Khosrow as led by physical urges and Shirin as thoughtful and spiritual, so their story turns on two conceptions of love rather than on obstacles alone ([Cambridge University Library](https://exhibitions.lib.cam.ac.uk/ywa/artifacts/khusraw/)). Shirin falls for an idealized portrait of Khosrow, while he falls for her at first sight; she withholds herself until marriage, and he must move beyond appetite before he can become a rightful king ([Cambridge University Library](https://exhibitions.lib.cam.ac.uk/ywa/artifacts/khusraw/)).

## Story in outline

The poem follows Khosrow and Shirin through repeated separation before union, a shape Cambridge summarizes as long separation and heartache ([Cambridge University Library](https://exhibitions.lib.cam.ac.uk/ywa/artifacts/khusraw/)). The plot is set in motion by images: court painter Shapur describes Shirin's beauty and carries portraits between the two, so each falls in love before meeting the other ([Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/khusraw-discovers-shirin-bathing/)). Their first encounter comes when Khosrow chances upon Shirin bathing, the poem's most illustrated moment ([Brooklyn Museum](https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/objects/157598)).

Farhad, an engineer smitten with Shirin, digs a canal for her palace; Khosrow sets him the near-impossible task of cutting through Mount Bisutun and, when Farhad completes it, sends false word that Shirin is dead, after which Farhad takes his own life ([Cahiers d'Asie centrale](https://journals.openedition.org/asiecentrale/2958?lang=en)). Shirin reproaches Khosrow for Farhad's death and for his affair with Shekar, insisting he must be sober and marry her ([The Morgan Library & Museum](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/treasures-of-islamic-manuscript-painting/104)). The marriage happens, but Khosrow is later imprisoned and assassinated by his son from another marriage, and Shirin dies by her own hand rather than marry that son ([The Morgan Library & Museum](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/treasures-of-islamic-manuscript-painting/104)).

## Shirin as a character, and the poem's idea of love

Shirin is the poem's moral center. In one episode, her aunt Queen Mihin Banu permits the couple to sit side by side in public only after Shirin promises she will not belong to Khosrow before marriage, and the queen does not permit them to be alone ([The Morgan Library & Museum](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/treasures-of-islamic-manuscript-painting/102)). In Cambridge's reading, Shirin dominates her passion and holds Khosrow to a standard he must grow into, so his education in love and kingship become one story ([Cambridge University Library](https://exhibitions.lib.cam.ac.uk/ywa/artifacts/khusraw/)).

Scholarship on the classical Persian romance presents Shirin as an ideal woman figure joining strong human sensitivity with symbolic mysticism, carrying both sacred and secular qualities ([Journal of the Japanese Association for Oriental Studies](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jorient1962/28/1/28_1_37/_article/-char/en)). That double quality explains her long afterlife: she is neither a passive prize nor a purely allegorical figure, but a character whose refusals and final choice drive the plot. The poem thus reads as an ethical argument — desire disciplined by commitment — rather than a simple tale of lovers kept apart.

## Manuscripts, painting, and the polo match

*Khosrov and Shirin* became one of the most illustrated texts of the Persianate world. Cambridge holds a sixteenth- or seventeenth-century Persian copy with eleven full-page paintings, including the lovers hunting together, and notes that its mid-Safavid miniatures need not date the copied text itself ([Cambridge University Library](https://exhibitions.lib.cam.ac.uk/ywa/artifacts/khusraw/)). Brooklyn Museum holds a mid-eighteenth-century oil painting from Shiraz showing Khosrow discovering Shirin bathing ([Brooklyn Museum](https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/objects/157598)). Smarthistory observes that such paintings often depart from Nizami — for example by showing Khosrow in royal dress rather than disguised, and by adding attendants to a meeting whose force depends on isolation ([Smarthistory](https://smarthistory.org/khusraw-discovers-shirin-bathing/)).

The poem also preserves court sport. Nizami interweaves the love story with [chovgan](/events/chovgan/), the horseback game ancestral to polo, describing matches between Khosrow and his courtiers and between Shirin and her ladies-in-waiting ([Circle of Ancient Iranian Studies](http://www.cais-soas.com/CAIS/Sport/polo.htm)). That detail presents Shirin as a skilled, public participant in court life, matching Khosrow in physical and intellectual games, as Cambridge's account of their hunting party and debate suggests ([Cambridge University Library](https://exhibitions.lib.cam.ac.uk/ywa/artifacts/khusraw/)).

## Imitations, operas, and modern censorship

Nizami's poem set a pattern later poets answered directly. Scholars call this practice *khamsa-nawisi*: attempting to match Nizami's five-poem feat ([Cahiers d'Asie centrale](https://journals.openedition.org/asiecentrale/2958?lang=en)). Amir Khusrau of Delhi retold it as *Farhad and Shirin*, recasting Farhad as a model of the suffering mystical lover; Ali Shir Navai later produced a Chaghatay version in his own Khamsa ([Cahiers d'Asie centrale](https://journals.openedition.org/asiecentrale/2958?lang=en)). Uzeyir Hajibeyov's *Farhad and Shirin* appeared among his early Azerbaijani operas in 1911, followed by 1936–1941 Tajik, Uzbek, and Azerbaijani musicals ([Azernews](https://www.azernews.az/culture/126137.html); [Cahiers d'Asie centrale](https://journals.openedition.org/asiecentrale/2958?lang=en)). In 2011 Iran refused publication of a new edition ([Index on Censorship](https://www.indexoncensorship.org/2011/08/epic-12th-century-poem-ravaged-by-irans-censors/)).

## Frequently asked questions

**What is *Khosrov and Shirin*?**
*Khosrov and Shirin* is a Persian romantic epic by Nizami Ganjavi, completed about 1180 as the second poem of his five-poem *Khamsa*. It retells the love of King Khosrow II and Shirin through separation, rivalry, and tragedy, and it remains a classic of Persian literature.

**Is *Khosrov and Shirin* based on real people?**
The central pair has a historical basis in a sixth- to seventh-century Sasanian king and queen, but Nizami's poem is a fictionalized expansion, not history. Ferdowsi's earlier *Shahnameh* gives a brief episode; Nizami rebuilt it as a full romance with invented scenes and characters.

**Who is Farhad in the poem?**
Farhad is an engineer who loves Shirin and digs a canal for her palace. Khosrow, jealous, sets him the near-impossible task of cutting through Mount Bisutun, then falsely reports Shirin dead when Farhad succeeds. Farhad then takes his own life, and his death haunts Khosrow's later courtship.

**Why is Shirin important in Persian literature?**
Shirin is presented as an ideal figure who combines human feeling with symbolic, spiritual depth, and who sets the terms of her own union by insisting on marriage. Her refusals and reproaches shape the plot, making her a moral center rather than a passive object of desire.
