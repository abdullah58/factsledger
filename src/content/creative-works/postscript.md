## Overview

PostScript is a page-description language created at [Adobe](/organizations/adobe/) that describes a printed page in mathematical terms — where every letter, curve and image sits — so that any device able to interpret it can render the same page at any resolution it supports ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/computer-programming-language/Visual-Basic)). Launched in 1984 as Adobe's first product, it became the invisible engine of desktop publishing when [Apple](/organizations/apple/) put it inside the LaserWriter, and its outline-font technology later fed the document and type systems that replaced it.

The language grew out of work by [John Warnock](/people/john-warnock/) and [Charles Geschke](/people/charles-geschke/) at [Xerox PARC](/organizations/xerox-parc/) ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Adobe-Systems-Incorporated)). When [Xerox](/organizations/xerox/) declined to bring the technology to market — Geschke recalled being told it took seven years to develop a product at Xerox — the pair founded Adobe in 1982 to do it themselves, naming the company after a creek near their homes ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Adobe-Systems-Incorporated); [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). The Computer History Museum, which preserved PostScript's source code in 2022, calls it "an essential ingredient in the desktop publishing industry" that drove Adobe's growth ([Computer History Museum](https://computerhistory.org/press-releases/computer-history-museum-makes-adobe-postscripts-source-code-available-to-the-public-as-a-part-of-its-art-of-code-series/)).

## From a turnkey plan to selling software

Adobe's original plan was not to sell a language at all. [Hambrecht & Quist](/organizations/hambrecht-quist/) agreed $2.5 million over two years for a complete turnkey publishing system — computers, printers and typesetting equipment sold to large companies ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Customers talked them out of it: Digital Equipment's Gordon Bell said he needed only the software to join his systems together, and [Steve Jobs](/people/steve-jobs/) said the same about the Macintosh in development. Jobs first tried to buy Adobe outright, then settled for a licensing deal in which he took a 19.9% stake at five times the original venture valuation, plus prepaid royalties — about $3.5 million in total, by Geschke's account ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Britannica gives a different figure, 15%, for Apple's 1983 stake; see open items.

What caught people's attention, Geschke said, was not the language alone but rendering type on the fly from outlines — the curves defining a character at any size or angle — rather than from pre-built bitmaps for every size ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). That bet made PostScript's other famous decision possible: Adobe published the specification about three or four months before the first LaserWriter shipped, because, Geschke said, getting hardware makers, software developers and platform vendors to collaborate left no other choice, and the goal from the beginning was a universal standard ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). At one time there were over 75 PostScript clones — evidence the standard had escaped its maker.

## The type business that decided the standard

PostScript's success was also a font deal. Adobe built its own outline-type business and, Geschke said, was the first to be licensed the trademarks "Times" and "Helvetica" by [Linotype](/organizations/linotype/) ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)). Adobe had gone to [Compugraphic](/organizations/compugraphic/) first, because it was American and looked like the easier conversation; Geschke called the incumbent arrogant, holding the lion's share of the market. Linotype, needing an edge, agreed instead — and within about two years held the lion's share of the United States business because it had PostScript compatibility, while Compugraphic, in Geschke's words, "had to sell itself to Agfa and basically went out of business" ([Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/driving-adobe-co-founder-charles-geschke-on-challenges-change-and-values/)).

The practical payoff was resolution independence. Alphr's history of desktop publishing puts it plainly: any application on any platform could output a PostScript file that printed the same way on any supported device, and the same layout could be proofed locally at 300 dpi and then image-set at 1,250 or 2,540 dpi for commercial printing ([Alphr](https://www.alphr.com/realworld/94537/a-brief-history-of-dtp/)). Britannica records the same arc from the other end: imagesetter makers led by Linotype-Hell followed Apple in licensing PostScript, and within a few years it was common throughout the publishing industry ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Adobe-Systems-Incorporated)).

## The font wars and the long fade

Dominance invited a challenge. After Adobe's 1986 public offering, relations with Apple deteriorated over PostScript licensing fees, and in 1989 Apple announced it would sell its Adobe stock, collaborate with Microsoft on an enhanced PostScript clone, and introduce its own font technology, TrueType ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Adobe-Systems-Incorporated)). The dispute, known as the font wars, roiled the computer and publishing worlds for more than a year before a compromise; Microsoft abandoned its clone and adopted TrueType for Windows ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/Adobe-Systems-Incorporated)). Alphr notes the deeper consequence: scalable TrueType handling built into Mac OS and Windows removed PostScript's biggest selling point, and licensing revenues fell ([Alphr](https://www.alphr.com/realworld/94537/a-brief-history-of-dtp/)).

PostScript did not vanish so much as dissolve into its successors. The Computer History Museum traces the line directly: PostScript technology led to the creation of the document format at the heart of [Acrobat](/creative-works/acrobat/) in the 1990s, and to the digital typefaces and fonts that followed ([Computer History Museum](https://computerhistory.org/press-releases/computer-history-museum-makes-adobe-postscripts-source-code-available-to-the-public-as-a-part-of-its-art-of-code-series/)). The [LaserWriter](/creative-works/laserwriter/) that carried it into offices is the subject of its own profile; the language itself remains the reason a page designed on one machine could be trusted to print identically on another.

## What this profile does not claim

This profile does not resolve the Apple-stake conflict (15% per Britannica, 19.9% per Geschke) or the exact PostScript 3 and Type 1 end-of-support dates, which were not verified from a source read in full in this pass. It does not claim PostScript was invented in 1982 — 1982 is Adobe's founding year; the product launched in 1984. It does not present Geschke's characterisations of Compugraphic ("arrogant", "lion's share") as measured market-share data; they are his account of the negotiation. And it does not treat PostScript as still the dominant document-exchange format: by the late 1990s that role had passed to the format it spawned, even as PostScript interpreters remained inside printers.
